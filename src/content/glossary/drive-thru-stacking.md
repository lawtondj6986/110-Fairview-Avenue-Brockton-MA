## What it means

Drive-thru stacking is the on-site queue capacity of a drive-thru lane: how many vehicles can wait in line, from the order point or pickup window back to where the lane begins, without spilling into parking aisles or the public street. Zoning bylaws set a minimum number of stacking spaces by use, and site plan review tests whether the layout, curb cuts, and circulation actually deliver them. Stacking is often the single constraint that decides whether a pad site works for a given tenant.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Drive-thru demand on Route 18, Route 139, Route 3A, and Route 44 is intense, and coffee, pharmacy, bank, and quick-service users will pay premium rents for sites that work. Planning Boards in Hanover, Weymouth, Pembroke, and Plymouth have seen morning queues back into state highways and now scrutinize stacking closely, sometimes requiring bypass lanes and queuing studies. A pad that fits a 2,200 square foot building but only six stacked cars will lose the coffee tenant to the site across the road that holds twelve.

## In practice

A developer markets a 0.9-acre pad on Route 139 in Marshfield to a national coffee chain. The chain requires 12 stacking spaces, about 240 feet of lane, plus a bypass lane. The first site plan fits nine. Rotating the building and shifting the curb cut 40 feet costs 7 parking spaces, which the town allows through a shared parking agreement with the adjacent retail. The chain signs a 15-year ground lease at $135,000 a year, roughly 20 percent above what a non-drive-thru user offered.
