## What it means

Unit economics is the revenue and cost of a business measured at the level of a single unit: one customer, one job, one appointment, one table turn. It includes the direct cost to acquire that unit (marketing and sales time) and the direct cost to deliver it (labor, materials, subs), and leaves out fixed overhead. The result shows whether each additional unit adds money or burns it, and how fast acquisition cost is paid back.

## Why it matters for a local business

Every ICS system is sold against unit economics, because that is how an owner decides whether a $1,500 a month Lead Engine makes sense. If a Brockton roofer's average job nets $3,200 after direct costs and the system books three extra jobs a month, the math is obvious. If a Quincy nail salon's average visit nets $18, the same system has to book many more and the conversation is about volume and repeat rate instead. The payback calculator on this site runs that comparison.

## In practice

A Rockland landscaper reviewed 60 jobs from the prior season. Lawn maintenance accounts netted $34 per visit after labor and fuel, with almost zero acquisition cost because they were referrals. One-time cleanups averaged $410 in revenue, $290 in direct cost, and $85 in ad spend to acquire, netting $35 per job for a full afternoon of work. He raised the cleanup minimum to $650, dropped the cleanup ad campaign, and put the money into a maintenance-plan offer to past cleanup customers.
