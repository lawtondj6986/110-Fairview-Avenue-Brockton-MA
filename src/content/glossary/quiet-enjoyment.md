## What it means

Quiet enjoyment is the landlord's covenant that the tenant may possess and use the premises for the term without disturbance by the landlord or by anyone claiming a superior right through the landlord. Massachusetts law implies it in every lease, and most commercial leases also state it expressly, often conditioned on the tenant paying rent and not being in default. It is the foundation that makes an SNDA matter: if the landlord's lender forecloses, the SNDA preserves the tenant's quiet enjoyment against the new owner.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Problems surface most often in older multi-tenant buildings in Brockton and Quincy that are being renovated or repositioned. A landlord that shuts down the only elevator for three months, blocks the loading area with a dumpster, or relocates a tenant's parking to a back lot may be breaching the covenant. It also protects against the landlord's own deals: a tenant with a strong clause cannot be forced out early because the landlord found a better tenant or a buyer who wants the building empty. Owner-users who lease out part of their building should understand what they are promising.

## In practice

A medical billing company leases 3,000 square feet on the second floor of a Quincy office building. The landlord begins a lobby and elevator renovation, and for 11 weeks the only access is a rear stairwell, with jackhammering during business hours. Two employees quit and the tenant documents $18,000 in lost productivity and temporary space. The tenant's attorney sends notice of breach; the landlord agrees to abate rent for three months, $19,500 at $26 per square foot, and to limit noisy work to before 8 a.m. for the rest of the project.
