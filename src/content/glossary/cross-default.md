## What it means

A cross-default clause provides that a default by a party under one contract constitutes a default under a second contract with the same or a related counterparty, even if the second contract has been performed perfectly. Lenders use it to consolidate their position across multiple loans to one borrower, so they can act on the whole relationship if one loan sours. Landlords use it when a tenant occupies more than one space, or to link a lease with a guaranty, a storage license, or a parking agreement. The clause can be mutual, but in practice it almost always runs in favor of the lender or landlord.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Growing South Shore businesses often end up with several agreements with the same counterparty: a contractor with two bays in the same Rockland industrial park, a medical group with offices in two buildings owned by one Quincy landlord, an investor with three loans at one local bank secured by buildings in Brockton, Weymouth, and Plymouth. Cross-default turns a problem at one location into a problem at all of them. ICS flags the clause in every lease abstract and loan summary, and for tenant clients negotiates to limit it to uncured monetary defaults after notice.

## In practice

An investor has three loans at one bank totaling $4.6 million on three South Shore buildings, each cross-defaulted and cross-collateralized. A Plymouth tenant stops paying, the Plymouth loan's debt service coverage falls below the 1.20 covenant, and the bank issues a notice of default. Under the cross-default, the bank can also call the Brockton and Weymouth loans, both performing with coverage above 1.40. The investor negotiates a forbearance by adding $150,000 of cash collateral and a new tenant in Plymouth within 90 days. Without cross-default, the Plymouth problem would have stayed in Plymouth.
