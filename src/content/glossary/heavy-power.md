## What it means

Heavy power describes a building's electrical service when it is substantially greater than standard commercial capacity. It is expressed in amperage, voltage, and phase: a typical small retail or office unit has 200 amps of single-phase or 200 to 400 amps of three-phase service, while a heavy power building might carry 1,200 to 4,000 amps at 480 volts three-phase. The term also implies the distribution behind it: panel capacity, bus duct, and the utility transformer serving the building.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Power is one of the few building features a tenant cannot fix quickly. Older industrial buildings in Brockton, Avon, and Taunton were built for manufacturing and often have more service than their current use needs, which makes them valuable to machine shops, commercial kitchens, and cannabis cultivators who need it. Newer flex buildings along Route 24 were built for contractors and distributors and often top out at 400 amps. Utility upgrade timelines in eastern Massachusetts have stretched to a year or more, so an existing heavy power service can be worth a rent premium of several dollars per square foot.

## In practice

A commercial bakery needs 800 amps of three-phase service for ovens and refrigeration. It looks at two 8,000 square foot buildings. One in Rockland has 400 amps and quotes $12 per square foot net; the utility estimates $140,000 and nine months for the upgrade. One in Brockton has 1,200 amps already and quotes $14 per square foot net. The $16,000 annual rent difference takes almost nine years to equal the upgrade cost, and the bakery opens nine months sooner. It takes the Brockton building.
