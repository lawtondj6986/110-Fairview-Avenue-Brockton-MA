## What it means

A work letter is an exhibit to a commercial lease that governs the construction of the premises before the tenant occupies them. It allocates the build-out between landlord's work, which the landlord performs at its cost, and tenant's work, which the tenant performs often with a landlord-funded tenant improvement allowance. It sets the design and approval process, construction standards, schedule, cost responsibility for changes and delays, and the mechanics for disbursing allowance funds. In a turnkey deal the work letter effectively is the construction contract between landlord and tenant.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Second-generation space dominates the South Shore market, so most leases involve converting someone else's build-out: a former insurance office in Quincy into a dental suite, a Brockton retail bay into a physical therapy clinic, a Rockland warehouse into a contractor's shop with office. Small landlords often offer a lump-sum allowance and a one-paragraph description of their work. That is where disputes over who pays for the electrical upgrade or the ADA restroom begin. ICS insists on a full work letter with plans attached for every tenant client, and reviews it alongside the tenant's architect before the lease is signed.

## In practice

A dental practice leases 3,000 square feet in Quincy at $30 per square foot with a $75 per square foot allowance, $225,000. The work letter specifies landlord's work as demising, new HVAC sized for medical use, a 400-amp electrical service, and a code-compliant restroom, all at landlord cost outside the allowance. Tenant's work covers everything else, funded by the allowance in three draws against invoices and lien waivers. The build-out comes in at $268,000; the tenant pays the $43,000 overage. Without the letter, the $38,000 electrical upgrade would have come out of the allowance and the overage would have been $81,000.
