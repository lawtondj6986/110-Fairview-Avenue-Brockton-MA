## What it means

Infill is development on vacant, abandoned, or underused parcels within an existing built-up area, as opposed to greenfield development on open land at the edge of town. Infill sites are served by existing streets and utilities and surrounded by established uses. They range from a single vacant lot between two buildings to a former gas station on a commercial corridor to the parking lot of a half-empty shopping center.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Brockton is an infill market: a city of roughly 105,000 people with hundreds of vacant lots, former factory parcels, and closed gas stations, most of them with sewer, water, and zoning that already allows commercial or multifamily use. Quincy and Weymouth see infill on former auto dealerships and underused retail along Route 3A and Route 18. In the suburban towns, infill often means a pad building on the excess parking of an older shopping center, which the Planning Board tends to prefer over clearing woods on Route 53. Sewer availability is the dividing line: a Brockton or Quincy infill lot can hold a use that an equivalent lot on septic in Pembroke cannot.

## In practice

An investor buys a 14,000 square foot former gas station lot on Main Street in Brockton for $275,000. Tanks were removed in 2009 with a closure statement on file, confirmed through MassDEP records. Zoning allows a 4,000 square foot building by right with 16 parking spaces. A dialysis provider signs a 15-year NNN lease at $24 per square foot before construction, and the $1.3 million all-in project produces a $96,000 NOI, a 7.4 percent yield on cost with city sewer and no wetlands review.
