## What it means

Capital expenditures are outlays for assets or improvements with a useful life beyond one year: roof replacement, HVAC units, parking lot repaving, elevator modernization, structural repairs, and major tenant improvements. They are distinguished from operating expenses, which cover the recurring cost of running the building. CapEx is not deducted in arriving at net operating income, but it directly affects cash flow, and lenders and buyers underwrite it separately through reserves and holdbacks.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Much of the South Shore commercial stock was built between 1965 and 1990, which means roofs, rooftop HVAC units, and parking lots are on their second or third life cycle. A 20,000 square foot Brockton flex building with a 25-year-old rubber roof is a $200,000 to $300,000 bill waiting for an owner. New England weather accelerates asphalt and roof wear. ICS prices every component with a remaining-life estimate in the Deal tools model, because a cap rate that ignores CapEx is a cap rate on paper only.

## In practice

A buyer evaluates a 12,000 square foot Hanover retail strip at $2.1 million, NOI $147,000, a 7 percent cap. The inspection finds four 18-year-old rooftop units, a roof with roughly five years left, and a cracked lot. The buyer's CapEx estimate: $48,000 for HVAC, $110,000 for roof, $35,000 for paving, about $193,000 over the first five years. Spread across the hold, that cuts effective annual return by nearly $39,000, so the buyer offers $1.95 million and asks for a $50,000 roof credit at closing.
