## What it means

A submarket is a geographic subdivision of a metropolitan real estate market that behaves as a distinct unit for a property type: tenants consider its buildings substitutes for one another, and brokers and data services track its inventory, vacancy, rents, and absorption separately. Submarkets are defined by highways, municipal lines, and clusters of similar property. A metro-wide statistic averages across submarkets that may be moving in opposite directions.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

The South Shore is itself a submarket in most Boston market reports, but it splits further in practice. Industrial users think in terms of the Route 24 corridor from Randolph through Brockton to Taunton, the Route 3 corridor from Braintree to Plymouth, and the smaller Route 18 and Route 139 clusters in Rockland, Hanover, and Abington. Office centers on Quincy and Braintree with secondary nodes in Hingham, Hanover, and Plymouth. Retail follows corridors, not towns. ICS's market sheets break the South Shore into these working submarkets because a Quincy office rent tells you nothing about Plymouth, and a Taunton distribution rent tells you nothing about a Rockland small-bay unit.

## In practice

An investor comparing two 20,000 square foot industrial buildings, one in Brockton off Route 24 and one in Plymouth off Route 3, finds the same asking price of $160 per square foot. The Route 24 submarket shows 3.2 percent vacancy and rents of $13 to $15 NNN; the Plymouth submarket shows 7 percent vacancy and rents of $10 to $12. Underwritten to submarket rent, the Brockton building supports a 7.8 percent yield and the Plymouth building 6.4 percent, and the identical price is not identical value.
