## What it means

The holding period is the span between acquiring a property and disposing of it, either by sale or by a refinance that returns the invested capital. It is a core assumption in any pro forma because it sets when the terminal value is realized, how much of the loan has amortized, how many lease rollovers the owner absorbs, and how much capital expenditure falls inside the ownership window. Transaction costs on both ends are spread across the hold, so short holds need stronger appreciation to work.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

South Shore commercial properties are not liquid. A Brockton industrial building or a Hanover retail strip may take six to twelve months to sell at a fair price, and the buyer pool is thin compared to Boston. That argues for longer holds and for underwriting that does not depend on a quick exit. The typical ICS investor client plans a seven to ten year hold with a refinance at year five to pull out equity, and matches the loan term, often a five, seven, or ten year fixed period, to that plan so a maturity does not force a sale into a bad market.

## In practice

An investor models a $2 million Rockland flex building at a 7.5 percent cap with 3 percent annual rent growth and a 7.5 percent exit cap. Transaction costs: 2 percent in, 5 percent out. At a three-year hold the IRR is about 9 percent because costs eat the gain. At seven years it is roughly 12 percent. At ten years it is about 12.5 percent with a 2.1x equity multiple. The investor chooses a seven-year hold and a seven-year fixed-rate loan to match.
