## What it means

Provenance is the answer to "where did this come from and how do we know." For every fact in a brief, it records the source, the date retrieved, the identifier (book and page, docket number, filing ID, archive timestamp), and any intermediate step. A fact without provenance is an assertion. With it, the client, their attorney, or an opposing party can pull the same public record and see the same thing.

## Why it matters in a dossier

Dossiers get challenged. A seller's counsel in Quincy will ask how ICS knows about the lien; a ballot committee will ask where the contribution figure came from. Provenance makes every finding reproducible from public sources. It also keeps the methods honest: a record whose origin cannot be stated is a record that was probably obtained the wrong way, and it does not go in.

## In practice

A due-diligence brief on a Taunton warehouse makes 31 factual claims. The appendix lists 31 sources: 12 Registry instruments by book and page, 6 MassCourts docket numbers, 5 Secretary of the Commonwealth filing IDs, 4 municipal documents by meeting date, 3 archived web pages by timestamp, and 1 licensed database query by date. Opposing counsel verifies 9 of them in an afternoon and stops arguing.
