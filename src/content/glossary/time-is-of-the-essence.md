## What it means

"Time is of the essence" is a contract provision stating that the dates and deadlines in the agreement are material terms, so that failure to perform by a stated date is a breach even if the delay is brief and causes no harm. In Massachusetts commercial purchase and sale agreements, the clause is standard and applies to the closing date, due diligence expiration, financing contingency date, and deposit deadlines. Absent the clause, a court may treat a short delay as immaterial and give the late party a reasonable time to perform.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

South Shore deals move on a small number of professionals: a handful of closing attorneys, local banks with their own appraisal queues, and town offices that issue certificates on their own schedule. A Title V inspection in Plymouth, a municipal lien certificate from Brockton, or a bank appraisal in Quincy can each slip two weeks for reasons no one controls. Under a time-is-of-the-essence clause, that slip can cost a buyer its deposit or a seller its deal. ICS calendars every date in the agreement and pushes for written extensions a week before any deadline is at risk, not the day of.

## In practice

A buyer signs a purchase and sale agreement for a $1.4 million Hanover retail building with a $70,000 deposit, a financing contingency expiring on day 45, and closing on day 60, time being of the essence. On day 41 the bank says the appraisal will not be back until day 50. The buyer's attorney requests an extension of the financing contingency to day 52 in writing on day 42. The seller agrees in exchange for the buyer increasing the deposit by $15,000. Had the buyer waited until day 46, the contingency would have expired, the deposit would have gone hard, and the seller could have kept it if the loan fell through.
