## What it means

Trade fixtures are items of personal property a tenant attaches to leased premises for the purpose of conducting its trade, which remain the tenant's property and may be removed at the end of the term, subject to repairing any damage. They differ from leasehold improvements, which become part of the real estate and belong to the landlord. The distinction matters at surrender, in a sale of the business, in a lender's collateral, and when the tenant defaults.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Restaurant, medical, fitness, and salon leases make up a large share of South Shore retail, and those uses carry the most expensive trade fixtures. A pizza shop in Brockton may have $150,000 in hoods, ovens, and walk-ins; a dental office in Hanover may have $400,000 in chairs, imaging, and cabinetry. The landlord's lender may claim a lien on fixtures; the tenant's equipment lender will file a UCC and want a landlord's waiver. Who owns what has to be written down, because second-generation restaurant space on Route 18 is worth more with the hood than without it.

## In practice

A restaurant tenant in Weymouth spends $210,000 on build-out: $90,000 in walls, floors, plumbing, and electrical that become leasehold improvements, and $120,000 in a hood, walk-in cooler, ovens, and a bar that are trade fixtures. The lease, negotiated up front, lists the hood and walk-in as items that stay at surrender in exchange for an extra month of free rent, worth $7,500. When the owner sells the business six years later, the buyer pays $65,000 for the remaining removable fixtures and assumes the lease with the landlord's consent.
