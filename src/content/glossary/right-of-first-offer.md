## What it means

A right of first offer gives its holder, usually a tenant or neighboring owner, the right to receive the first offer when the owner decides to lease specified space or sell the property. The owner must present terms to the holder before marketing to anyone else. If the holder declines or the parties cannot agree within the response period, the owner is free to deal with third parties, often subject to a rule that it cannot accept a materially worse deal than the holder rejected without re-offering. ROFOs appear in leases as expansion rights and in purchase contexts as a path to ownership.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

For an owner-user tenant on the South Shore, a ROFO on the building is often the realistic path to owning it. Small landlords in Brockton, Rockland, and Whitman who built or bought a flex building decades ago eventually sell, and the tenant who has been there ten years is the natural buyer if it has a seat at the table. For a growing tenant, a ROFO on adjacent space is cheaper to get than a fixed expansion option because it costs the landlord little. ICS negotiates ROFOs into leases for owner-user clients who intend to buy eventually.

## In practice

An electrical contractor leases 8,000 square feet of a 16,000 square foot Whitman flex building with a ROFO on any sale. Six years in, the owner decides to retire and, as required, offers the building to the tenant at $2.1 million with a 20-day response window. The tenant orders an appraisal that comes in at $1.95 million and counters at $1.98 million. The parties agree at $2.0 million, and the contractor finances with an SBA 504 loan at 10 percent down. It never competes with other buyers, and the owner saves roughly $100,000 in brokerage commission.
