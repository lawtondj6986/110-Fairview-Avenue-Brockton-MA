## What it means

Transit-oriented development is compact, mixed-use development within roughly a half mile of a rail or bus rapid transit station, designed so residents and workers can walk to transit and daily needs. It typically combines multifamily housing, ground-floor retail, and office with reduced parking requirements and pedestrian-oriented streets. Towns encourage it through overlay districts, Chapter 40R smart growth zoning, and the multifamily districts required by the MBTA Communities Act.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

The Red Line made Quincy the South Shore's TOD market, with thousands of apartments built around Quincy Center and North Quincy since 2015 and ground-floor retail that depends on them. Commuter rail TOD is earlier stage: Brockton's downtown station area, Abington and Whitman's village centers, Weymouth Landing, and Kingston's station area all have new zoning and a handful of projects. For commercial owners near a station, TOD zoning often raises land value by allowing housing above retail, and brings new customers to the ground floor. The Planning Board will still want a traffic study, and abutters will still raise parking.

## In practice

An owner holds a 28,000 square foot lot with a 1960s single-story retail building two blocks from the Brockton commuter rail station, leased at $14 per square foot gross to three local tenants. New TOD zoning allows four stories, 48 units, and ground-floor commercial at 1.1 parking spaces per unit. A developer offers $2.4 million for the land, about $50,000 per unit, versus roughly $1.5 million the building was worth on its income. The owner structures the sale with a 1031 exchange into a NNN property.
