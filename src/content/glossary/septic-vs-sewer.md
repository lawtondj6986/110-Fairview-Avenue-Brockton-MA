## What it means

Every building disposes of wastewater either through an on-site septic system regulated under Title 5 of the Massachusetts Environmental Code, or through a connection to a municipal sewer. Septic puts the capacity question on the lot: soil, groundwater, and land area set how much flow the site can handle. Sewer moves it to the town plant, with a connection fee and user charges, and usually allows far more intensive use. Which one a property has, and whether sewer is available on the street, is a basic fact of commercial due diligence.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Sewer is the dividing line in South Shore commercial real estate. Brockton, Quincy, Weymouth, Rockland, Abington, and Whitman are largely sewered. Hanover, Pembroke, Hanson, Halifax, Duxbury, and most of Plymouth outside the center are largely not, though sewer districts are expanding in some. A restaurant or medical office on a septic lot in Pembroke may be impossible, while the same use on sewer in Rockland is routine. Sewered land near Route 18 and Route 3 trades at a premium for exactly this reason, and a sewer tie-in alone can add value to an unsewered building.

## In practice

Two 6,000 square foot retail buildings are listed at $1.2 million, one on sewer in Abington and one on septic in Hanover. The Hanover system is rated for 450 gallons per day, enough for retail but not the 1,700 gallons a 48-seat restaurant would need, and the soil will not support an upgrade that large. The Abington building takes the restaurant tenant at $24 per square foot NNN. The Hanover building leases to a retailer at $18, a $36,000 annual difference in rent.
