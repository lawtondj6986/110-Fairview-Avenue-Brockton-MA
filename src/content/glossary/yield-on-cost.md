## What it means

Yield on cost is the ratio of a property's stabilized net operating income to the total cost to produce it: land, hard costs, soft costs, financing, and lease-up. It measures what the project earns on the dollars spent, as distinct from the cap rate, which measures what the market pays for the income. The difference between yield on cost and the exit cap rate is the value a developer creates, and the test of whether a project is worth doing at all.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Most "development" on the South Shore is small: converting a tired Brockton warehouse to flex, adding a drive-through pad in Hanover, renovating a Weymouth office to medical. The question each time is the same: does the stabilized rent, divided by purchase price plus renovation, beat what a finished building would cost to buy? With flex and industrial trading around 7 to 8 percent caps and renovation costs high, a conversion needs to pencil to 9 percent or better to be worth the risk. ICS runs this number in every Deal tools model before a buyer commits.

## In practice

An investor buys a vacant 20,000 square foot Brockton warehouse for $1.6 million and spends $600,000 on roof, lighting, demising walls, and loading doors, for total cost of $2.2 million. It leases as three flex units at $11 per square foot NNN, generating $220,000 of NOI after a vacancy allowance. Yield on cost is 10 percent. At a 7.5 percent market cap the stabilized value is about $2.93 million, a $730,000 gain over cost.
