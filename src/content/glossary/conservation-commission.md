## What it means

Every Massachusetts city and town has a Conservation Commission, a volunteer board appointed under Chapter 40, Section 8C. Its main regulatory job is administering the Wetlands Protection Act, Chapter 131, Section 40, and any local wetlands bylaw the town has adopted. The commission decides whether proposed work falls under the Act, holds public hearings on Notices of Intent, issues Orders of Conditions, and enforces them. Most commissions also have a staff conservation agent who does site visits and reviews plans.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

The South Shore is wet. Cranberry bogs, cedar swamps, the Taunton River watershed, and coastal resource areas mean a large share of commercial parcels in Hanover, Pembroke, Plymouth, Marshfield, and Kingston have some jurisdictional area on or near them. Many towns also have a local bylaw that is stricter than the state Act, with 25- or 50-foot no-disturb zones. A buyer who skips the commission on a site that "looks dry" can lose a building season. Pull the wetlands maps, walk the line with a wetland scientist, and read the local bylaw.

## In practice

A contractor buys a 3-acre lot in Pembroke to build a 10,000 square foot shop and yard. The wetland scientist flags a bordering vegetated wetland along the rear line. The 100-foot buffer takes about 40,000 square feet off the usable area, and the town's 50-foot no-build zone holds the building forward. The Notice of Intent costs roughly $8,000 in consultant and filing fees and runs three months, but the Order of Conditions issues with stormwater and erosion conditions and the project proceeds.
