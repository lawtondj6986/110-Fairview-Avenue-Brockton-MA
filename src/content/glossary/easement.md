## What it means

An easement is a legal right to use another person's land for a defined purpose without owning it. Common commercial examples are utility easements for power and water lines, access easements for shared driveways, drainage easements, and parking or cross-access agreements between neighboring shopping centers. Easements run with the land, meaning they bind future owners, and they are recorded at the Registry of Deeds. The property that benefits is the dominant estate; the property burdened is the servient estate.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Many South Shore commercial lots were carved out of larger parcels decades ago, and the paper trail is not always clean. A Brockton industrial lot may rely on a 1970s driveway easement over a neighbor to reach Route 28, or a Plymouth retail pad may share a curb cut under a cross-access agreement with the adjacent plaza. These rights determine what you can build, where you can park, and whether your access survives a neighbor's sale. ICS flags them in the Buyer Brief before an offer goes in.

## In practice

A buyer looks at a 1.8-acre commercial lot in Taunton listed at $650,000. The title search turns up a 25-foot-wide drainage easement held by the town along the rear lot line and a 15-foot utility easement along the side. Together they remove about 18,000 square feet, roughly 23 percent of the site, from the buildable area, and the planned 12,000 square foot building shrinks to 9,000. The buyer reprices the offer to $540,000 and the seller, who knew about neither easement, accepts.
