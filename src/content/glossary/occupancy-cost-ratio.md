## What it means

Occupancy cost ratio is a retailer's total cost of occupying its space, base rent plus CAM, taxes, insurance, and percentage rent, divided by its gross sales at that location, expressed as a percentage. It is the single most useful number for judging whether a retail rent is sustainable. A tenant at 7 percent has room to renew and absorb increases; a tenant at 16 percent is a renewal risk and probably a default risk. Sophisticated landlords track it for every tenant, and sophisticated tenants calculate it before signing.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

South Shore retail landlords often set asking rents by what the neighbor pays rather than what the space can produce, and a local tenant without a real estate department signs without running the math. A 1,500 square foot cafe on Route 139 in Hanover doing $650,000 a year can afford a very different rent than one in a Brockton strip doing $380,000. The ratio also drives percentage rent negotiations: a natural breakpoint is just the base rent divided by the percentage. Tenants should know their number before the first conversation about rent.

## In practice

A gift and card shop considers a 1,800 square foot inline space in a Plymouth plaza at $26 per square foot net, with CAM, taxes, and insurance adding $7.50. Total occupancy cost is $33.50 per square foot, $60,300 a year. The owner's sales projection is $520,000, so the ratio is 11.6 percent, at the top of the sustainable range for a specialty retailer. She negotiates the rent to $23, bringing the ratio to 10.6 percent, and offers a percentage rent kicker in exchange: 6 percent of sales above $690,000, the natural breakpoint at that rent.
