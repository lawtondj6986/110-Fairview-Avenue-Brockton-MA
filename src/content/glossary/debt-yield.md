## What it means

Debt yield is net operating income divided by the loan amount, expressed as a percentage. A $1.4 million loan on a building producing $126,000 of NOI has a 9 percent debt yield. Lenders use it as a sizing constraint alongside loan-to-value and debt service coverage ratio because it is independent of interest rate, amortization schedule, and appraisal. The lower the debt yield, the more leverage the borrower has relative to the income, and the thinner the lender's cushion.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

When interest rates were low, DSCR tests allowed generous loans, and buyers on the South Shore got used to 75 percent leverage. As rates rose, debt yield became the binding constraint on many deals, especially retail and office in Quincy, Braintree, and Brockton where cap rates are 6.5 to 7.5 percent. At a 7 percent cap and a 9 percent minimum debt yield, the maximum loan is about 78 percent of value; at a 6 percent cap it drops to 67 percent. ICS runs all three tests in the Deal tools model so buyers know which one will bite before they talk to a lender.

## In practice

A buyer offers $2.4 million for a Braintree office building with NOI of $156,000, a 6.5 percent cap. The bank quotes 75 percent LTV, $1.8 million, but requires a 9 percent debt yield. The loan is capped at $156,000 divided by 0.09, or about $1.73 million, roughly 72 percent of price. The buyer needs an extra $70,000 of equity. Knowing this before the letter of intent avoids a financing contingency scramble three weeks before closing.
