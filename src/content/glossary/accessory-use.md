## What it means

An accessory use is a use of land or a building that is incidental and subordinate to the principal permitted use on the same lot and is customarily associated with it. Zoning bylaws typically allow accessory uses by right wherever the principal use is allowed, subject to size limits and sometimes a special permit for specific types such as outdoor storage, fuel dispensing, or a residential unit above a store. The test is customary and subordinate: an accessory use that grows to dominate the lot is a new principal use.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Contractors, landscapers, and auto operators on the South Shore live and die by accessory uses. The yard behind the shop in Hanson, the equipment rental counter inside a supply house in Brockton, the apartment over a storefront in Rockland: each depends on the bylaw treating it as accessory. Towns vary. Some cap outdoor storage at a percentage of the building footprint; some require screening; some ban it in the front setback. When a buyer's business model needs the accessory use more than the principal one, confirm with the building department before signing.

## In practice

A landscaper buys a 4,000 square foot shop on a 1-acre lot in Hanson. The bylaw permits outdoor storage as an accessory use up to 50 percent of the building footprint, screened, behind the front setback. That allows 2,000 square feet of material bins, enough for mulch and loam but not the 8,000 square feet of equipment parking the business needs. A special permit for expanded outdoor storage takes four months and $6,000 in engineering, and the buyer makes the purchase contingent on it.
