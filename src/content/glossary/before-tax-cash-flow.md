## What it means

Before-tax cash flow is the annual cash a property produces for its owner after paying operating expenses, debt service, and capital items, but before income taxes. The sequence runs: gross income, less vacancy, equals effective gross income; less operating expenses, equals NOI; less debt service, equals cash flow after financing; less capital expenditures and reserves, equals before-tax cash flow. Divide it by the equity invested and you have cash-on-cash return.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Owner-users and small investors on the South Shore usually buy with 25 to 35 percent down and a bank or SBA loan, so debt service eats most of the NOI. A Quincy or Weymouth building at a 6.5 percent cap with a loan at 7 percent interest can produce almost no cash flow in the early years, even though the investment may still make sense through principal paydown and appreciation. ICS builds before-tax cash flow year by year in the Deal tools model so a buyer sees the real number, not just the cap rate in the offering memorandum.

## In practice

An investor buys a 9,000 square foot Rockland retail building for $1.5 million with NOI of $105,000, a 7 percent cap. The loan is $1.05 million at 7 percent over 25 years, annual debt service about $89,000. A $0.25 per square foot reserve adds $2,250. Before-tax cash flow is roughly $13,750 on $450,000 of equity plus closing costs, about a 2.9 percent cash-on-cash return in year one. Rent escalations of 3 percent a year roughly double that by year four.
