## What it means

Tax increment financing is a local economic development tool in which a municipality agrees to forgo part of the property tax on the increased assessed value a project creates, for a fixed number of years, in exchange for the owner's commitment to invest and typically to create or retain jobs. In Massachusetts the TIF is a negotiated agreement rather than a bond-funded district as in some other states, generally structured under the state's Economic Development Incentive Program framework. The exemption applies only to the increment, never to the base value.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Brockton, Taunton, and several South Shore towns have used TIFs to attract manufacturers, distribution users, and larger commercial projects to sites that would otherwise sit idle. For an owner-user building or expanding a facility, a TIF can shave a meaningful share of operating cost in the early years when debt service is heaviest. The process is political: it requires a town meeting or council vote and a public case for the jobs and investment. ICS helps clients decide whether a project is large enough to justify the effort, since a TIF on a $900,000 renovation rarely is, and refers the agreement itself to counsel.

## In practice

A manufacturer buys a 2.8 acre Brockton parcel with a tired 20,000 square foot building, assessed at $1.1 million, and invests $5.5 million in a new 45,000 square foot facility, committing to 35 new jobs. The city negotiates a ten-year TIF exempting 60 percent of the increment in years one through five and 30 percent in years six through ten. With a new assessed value around $6 million and a commercial tax rate near $25 per thousand, the increment tax is roughly $122,000 a year; the TIF saves about $73,000 a year early on and roughly $37,000 a year later, for total savings over the term of around $550,000.
