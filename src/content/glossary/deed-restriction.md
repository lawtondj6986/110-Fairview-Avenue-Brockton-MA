## What it means

A deed restriction is a covenant written into a deed or a separate recorded instrument that limits what the owner can do with the property: no restaurants, no buildings over two stories, no competing grocery use, no subdivision. Unlike zoning, which is public regulation, a deed restriction is a private agreement that runs with the land and binds successive owners. It is enforced by whoever holds the benefit, often a neighboring parcel owner or a former developer.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

Deed restrictions show up constantly on South Shore commercial parcels. Shopping centers on Route 18 in Abington and Route 139 in Marshfield often carry restrictions barring uses that would compete with an anchor. Pad sites sold off from a larger center may be restricted to no fast food, no liquor, or no drive-through. Subdivided industrial parks in Rockland and Hanover sometimes restrict outdoor storage or truck idling. A buyer who learns of a restriction after signing can be stuck with a building its intended use cannot occupy. ICS pulls recorded restrictions early in every buyer engagement.

## In practice

A buyer under agreement on a 6,000 square foot freestanding building in Weymouth plans to open a daycare. The title examination surfaces a 1998 restriction, recorded by the developer who sold the parcel, barring "childcare or educational uses" to protect a daycare in the adjacent center. The benefit holder agrees to release it for $15,000 and attorney fees. Without the title search, the buyer would have closed on a $1.1 million building and discovered the problem at the building permit counter.
