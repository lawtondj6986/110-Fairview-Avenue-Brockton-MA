## What it means

A grease trap, or grease interceptor, slows kitchen wastewater so fats, oils, and grease separate and stay behind while water flows on to the sewer or septic system. Small units sit under a sink. Large interceptors, often 1,000 gallons or more, are buried outside the building.

## Why it matters on the South Shore

For any food use, the grease trap is a gating item. Massachusetts plumbing code and local boards of health require one for food service, and the municipal sewer department may have its own sizing rules. An exterior interceptor means excavation, which needs landlord consent and room on the site. On septic, common in towns such as Hanover, Norwell, and Pembroke, a restaurant can be far harder to permit at all. Confirm what exists before signing a letter of intent.

## In practice

A tenant takes a 2,400-square-foot former retail unit in Rockland for a restaurant. The town requires a 1,500-gallon exterior interceptor. Installation, including sawcutting the parking lot and repaving, is quoted at $32,000 and adds five weeks. The tenant negotiates $15,000 of added allowance and 45 more days of free rent to cover it. Pumping then runs about $450 every two months.
