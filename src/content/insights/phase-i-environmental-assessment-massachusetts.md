Walk Route 28 through Brockton and Avon, Route 18 through Abington and Whitman, or Route 139 through Rockland and Hanover, and you are walking past a century of commercial reuse. Buildings that were gas stations became auto body shops became tire stores became whatever they are today. Dry cleaners operated for decades on corner lots that are now medical offices. Small machine shops filled industrial parks before anyone tracked what went down the floor drain.

None of that history is visible from the parking lot. All of it can become the buyer's problem at closing. The tool that stands between the two is the Phase I environmental site assessment, and this is what a South Shore buyer should know about it.

## What a Phase I actually does

A Phase I environmental site assessment is a structured investigation of a property's environmental history and current condition, performed by an environmental professional to a recognized national standard. It has four parts:

**Records review.** The consultant pulls federal and state regulatory databases for the property and its surroundings: registered tanks, reported spills, hazardous waste generators, listed cleanup sites. They review historical sources, including city directories, fire insurance maps, aerial photographs going back decades, and prior assessments, to reconstruct what has operated on the parcel and next to it.

**Site reconnaissance.** They walk the property and its neighbors, looking for the physical evidence: staining, vent pipes, fill ports, drums, floor drains, transformers, stressed vegetation, the outline of a former tank pad.

**Interviews.** They talk to the owner, the occupants, and sometimes the local fire or health department about what has happened on the site.

**Report.** They write up the findings and identify any recognized environmental conditions, meaning the presence or likely presence of a release of hazardous substances or petroleum. They recommend next steps, which is usually either nothing or a Phase II.

A Phase I involves no sampling. No soil borings, no groundwater wells, no laboratory results. Its purpose is to determine whether sampling is warranted. That distinction matters for both cost and expectations.

## What a Phase II adds

If the Phase I identifies a recognized environmental condition, a Phase II collects the physical evidence: soil and groundwater samples analyzed for the contaminants the history suggests. A former gas station is sampled for petroleum compounds; a former dry cleaner for chlorinated solvents; a machine shop for metals and oils.

Phase II results fall into three buckets. Nothing above regulatory thresholds, which clears the way. Something above thresholds, which triggers reporting obligations and a cleanup process under the Massachusetts Contingency Plan. Or something below thresholds but present, which becomes a negotiation about price, indemnity, and a documented baseline that protects the buyer from being blamed for it later.

A Phase II costs more and takes longer than a Phase I, often three to six weeks and several thousand to tens of thousands of dollars depending on the scope. It is the reason a sixty-day due diligence period on an older commercial property is not excessive.

## Why Massachusetts makes this unavoidable

Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 21E is the state's cleanup law. It makes current owners and operators of a site strictly liable for cleanup of oil and hazardous materials, alongside the parties who caused the release. Strictly liable means it does not matter that you did not cause it, did not know about it, and bought the property in good faith. If you own it when the contamination is discovered, you are a responsible party.

There are defenses, and the one that matters to a buyer requires having conducted appropriate inquiry before purchase. In practice that means a Phase I performed to the standard, before closing, by a qualified professional. A buyer who skipped it has no defense. A buyer who did it, found nothing, and closed, has one.

That is why lenders require a Phase I on nearly every commercial loan. It is also why a cash buyer who feels clever for skipping the bank should not skip the assessment: the bank was protecting itself from the same risk you are now carrying alone.

## The South Shore pattern

Our experience across the region suggests a few consistent things.

Properties on the older highway corridors should be presumed to have a history until the Phase I says otherwise. The number of former fuel stations on Route 28, Route 18, Route 123, and Route 139 is remarkable, and many have been reused several times since the tanks were pulled, if they were pulled.

Small industrial buildings in Avon, Rockland, Hanover, and Stoughton frequently housed machine, plating, or printing operations in earlier decades. The Phase I will find those tenants in old directories even when the current owner has never heard of them.

Dry cleaners are the quiet one. A solvent release from a cleaner that closed in 1985 can still be present in soil and groundwater and can migrate under neighboring parcels. A Phase I looks at the neighbors for exactly this reason.

Septic systems and floor drains in buildings without sewer, which is much of the South Shore outside Brockton, create their own path for historic contamination. The Phase I and a Title V septic inspection often run in parallel on the same property.

## Fitting it into the deal

Order the Phase I the day the purchase and sale agreement is signed, not the day the lender asks. Two to three weeks for the Phase I, plus three to six for a Phase II if needed, consumes most of a sixty-day due diligence window. Buyers who start late end up asking for extensions from sellers who have no reason to grant them.

Make the due diligence period and the deposit's refundability explicit in the agreement. A clean Phase I is cheap insurance; a Phase I that finds something is leverage, but only if you can still walk away.

If the assessment finds a condition, the deal is not necessarily dead. It becomes a negotiation among four tools: a price credit sized to the estimated cost, an escrow holding back funds until the issue is resolved, a seller indemnity for the known condition with a cap and a term, and a documented baseline so the buyer is never blamed for what was already there. We have closed deals with all four and walked from deals where the seller refused all of them. Both were the right outcome.

## The seller's version

Sellers benefit from the same assessment, earlier. A Phase I in the data room before the property is marketed removes the single biggest reason buyers hesitate, retrade, or disappear during due diligence. If it finds something, the seller learns it on their own timeline and can address it or price it rather than discovering it from a buyer's consultant three days before a closing.

On any South Shore building with a commercial history, we advise sellers to commission the assessment before listing. It consistently shortens the sale and protects the price.

## The short version

A Phase I is a records-and-walk investigation, not a test. It costs a few thousand dollars and takes a few weeks. Under Massachusetts law it is the step that keeps a buyer from inheriting liability for a problem they did not create. On the South Shore's older corridors, it should be assumed necessary, ordered immediately, and read carefully.
