A parent in Bridgewater gets the baby down at 8:15. At 8:40 she is on the couch with her phone, finally doing the thing she has been putting off: finding care for September. She fills out tour request forms at four centers in twenty minutes.

One center replies at 8:42 with two tour times. Two reply the next afternoon. One replies Thursday.

She tours the first center on Tuesday morning. She likes it. She cancels the other tours. The other three directors never learn that they lost a family worth about $14,500 a year over a gap of eighteen hours.

That is how enrollment is won and lost, and the fix is not complicated. The full plan is in the [childcare and education playbook](/industries/childcare-education/playbook/). This article covers the two parts that matter most: the first hour, and the waitlist.

## Parents ask when the director is home

Most tour requests arrive outside the hours anyone is at the front desk. Evenings after bedtime. Sunday afternoon. A lunch break.

The director, meanwhile, is covering a classroom, handling a licensing visit, or doing pickup. A reply the next business day is normal in this industry, and nobody is being lazy. There is just no one free.

The baseline at most centers looks like this: tour requests answered the next business day, and about 40 percent of inquiries turn into a tour. The target is a response in under 60 seconds, seven days a week, and 70 percent or more of inquiries touring.

The gap between 40 and 70 is not persuasion. It is the back-and-forth. Every extra message needed to find a tour time loses a few families.

## The two-option reply

This goes out within a minute of any inquiry, at any hour:

"Hi [Name], thanks for asking about [Center]. We have openings in the [toddler] room starting [September]. Tours this week: [Tuesday 9:30] or [Thursday 4:00]. Which works? Reply with one and we will confirm."

Two choices, not a calendar link with forty slots, and not "when are you free?" A parent can answer with one word while holding a bottle.

Before or alongside that, the intake asks what the director would ask:

- How old is your child, and when do you need care to start?
- Which days and hours: full-time, part-time, before and after school?
- Which location or program?
- Any allergies, needs, or an IEP to plan for?
- Best mobile number for confirmation and the enrollment packet?

The answers determine what the text says. If the infant room is full until January, the message says so honestly and offers a tour plus a waitlist place. Parents forgive a full room. They do not forgive finding out on the tour. The same goes for tuition: publish it or send it before the visit.

## The funnel math

A typical center gets about 25 inquiries a month. Run them through both versions.

| | Next-day reply | Same-hour reply and follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inquiries per month | 25 | 25 |
| Inquiry-to-tour rate | 40% | 70% |
| Tours | 10 | 17 or 18 |
| Tour-to-enrollment rate | 50% | 70% |
| Families ready to enroll | 5 | About 12 |

Seven more families a month from the same inquiries. At an average annual enrollment of $14,500, each one matters.

A center cannot enroll more children than its rooms hold. Massachusetts licensing through the Department of Early Education and Care sets ratios and group sizes, and those are fixed. So when the rooms are full, the extra families do not become revenue this month. They become the waitlist, and the waitlist is what fills the next opening. The second half of this article is about that.

## The evening after the tour

A tour that goes well is half the job. The baseline tour-to-enrollment rate is about 50 percent, and most of the lost half simply drift.

The same evening, this goes out:

"It was lovely meeting [Child] today. You asked about [nap schedules]; here is how we handle it: [two sentences]. The enrollment packet is here: [link]. The [toddler] spot holds for 72 hours."

The line that does the work is "you asked about." Every parent asks one question on a tour that reveals what they are really worried about. Naps. Drop-off tears. How allergies are handled at lunch. The director notes that question in thirty seconds after the tour, and writes the two-sentence answer herself. It is her answer, in her words.

The 72-hour hold gives the family a reason to decide and gives you a clean rule. A reminder goes out once before it lapses. If the hold expires, the spot goes to the next family, and the first family is offered the waitlist.

## The waitlist is an asset, not a notebook

Most waitlists are a spiral notebook or a spreadsheet nobody has opened since spring. When a spot opens, the director calls down the list and finds that eight of the first ten families enrolled somewhere else months ago.

A warm waitlist works differently. Once a month, every family gets a short update:

"Hi [Name], a quick update from [Center]. The [preschool] room is still full, and you are number 3 for a [January] start. Still interested? Reply YES to keep your place or NO and we will take you off."

The message does three jobs. It keeps the list accurate. It reminds the family you exist. And it tells you the true depth of demand for each room, which is the number you need when deciding whether to open another classroom.

Then, when a spot opens:

"Hi [Name], a [preschool] spot opened for [October]. You are next on the list. Reply YES within 48 hours to claim it and we will send the paperwork."

If 48 hours pass, the offer moves to the next family automatically. The target is half of all openings filled from the list within seven days.

## What a vacancy costs

Put a number on an empty spot. At $14,500 a year, one enrollment is about $280 a week.

Say a center has eight openings a year from moves, job changes, and mid-year transitions. Without a working waitlist, each one takes about five weeks to fill: a week to notice, weeks of calls and tours, and a start date.

- Eight openings at five weeks each: 40 vacant weeks
- 40 weeks at $280: about $11,200 a year

With a warm list, the same eight openings fill in about a week each. That is eight vacant weeks, or about $2,240. The difference is close to $9,000 a year, and the staff cost of running the room did not change during any of those empty weeks.

For multi-site operators, the monthly waitlist count by room and location is also the best evidence for where to expand.

## Where the line sits

Some things the system does not do, and the list should be written down before launch:

- It does not discuss a child's day, behavior, development, or health. That is a teacher or the director.
- It does not answer questions about incidents, staffing, or anything sensitive. It passes those to the director at once.
- It does not handle licensing or enrollment forms beyond delivering them. Your process stays yours, and your license information is linked where parents expect to find it.
- It does not promise a spot that is not open.

Tell families how it works, too. One line in the first message is enough: scheduling comes by text, and anything about your child comes from a teacher or the director.

Parents are trusting you with their child. Fast scheduling earns the tour. People earn the enrollment.
