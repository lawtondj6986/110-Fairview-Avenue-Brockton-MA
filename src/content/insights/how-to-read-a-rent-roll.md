Every income property for sale comes with a [rent roll](/glossary/rent-roll/). It is one page, it has a total at the bottom, and the asking price is built on that total. Most buyers look at the total. That is the wrong place to look.

A rent roll is almost never falsified. Outright lies are easy to catch and expensive to tell. What happens instead is omission: the page shows what is true today and leaves out what changes tomorrow.

You can read one properly in fifteen minutes. Six columns carry the facts. Five questions cover what the page does not say.

## The six columns that matter

Rent rolls vary in layout. These six are the ones to find.

1. **Tenant name.** The legal name, not the sign on the door. "ABC Pizza" might be a three-store operator or a first-time owner with no other assets.
2. **Square feet.** Check that the suite sizes add up to the building size on the assessor's card. A gap means unlisted space or generous measuring.
3. **Lease start and expiration.** The single most important column. It tells you how long the income lasts.
4. **Base rent.** Monthly and annual, and per square foot. The per-foot figure lets you compare tenants to each other and to the market.
5. **Reimbursements.** What each tenant pays toward taxes, insurance, and common area costs. Two tenants at the same base rent can be worth very different amounts if one reimburses and one does not.
6. **Options and escalations.** Renewal options, termination rights, and scheduled increases. Often missing. Ask for them.

Security deposits are a useful seventh. A deposit column that is blank for half the tenants tells you something about how the building has been run.

## A sample roll, read line by line

Here is a 12,000 square foot strip center on a South Shore state route, offered on the following roll.

| Suite | Tenant | SF | Rent/SF | Annual rent | Expires |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Pizza shop | 2,400 | $24.00 | $57,600 | Mar 2031 |
| B | Nail salon | 1,800 | $22.00 | $39,600 | Month to month |
| C | Dental office | 3,600 | $23.00 | $82,800 | Aug 2027 |
| D | Insurance agency | 2,200 | $28.00 | $61,600 | Dec 2030 |
| E | Vacant | 2,000 | $22.00 | $44,000 | Pro forma |
| | **Total** | **12,000** | | **$285,600** | |

The total says $285,600. Read it again.

- **Suite E is empty.** The $44,000 is a hope, not a rent. Remove it.
- **Suite D pays $28.00** in a building where everyone else pays $22.00 to $24.00. The agency turns out to be owned by the seller. At a market $22.00 it is $48,400, a $13,200 difference.
- **Suite B can leave in 30 days.**
- **Suite C, the largest tenant, expires in nine months.**

Rent that is actually being paid, adjusted to market on the related tenant: $228,400. Rent that is locked in beyond next summer: $106,000.

## What that does to the price

Assume the leases are triple net, and ignore the owner's carrying costs on the empty suite to keep the arithmetic simple.

At a 7.5 percent [cap rate](/glossary/cap-rate/):

| Income used | Annual | Implied value |
|---|---|---|
| Roll as presented | $285,600 | $3,808,000 |
| In place, related tenant at market | $228,400 | $3,045,000 |

The gap is about $763,000, and nothing on the page was untrue. The vacant suite was labeled pro forma. The related tenant has a real lease. The dates were printed in black and white.

That is how a rent roll misleads: it lets the reader do the adding. The rule is that you pay for [in-place rent](/glossary/in-place-rent/) at the cap rate, and you treat vacant space and expiring leases as work you will do, priced as risk. Test your own figures in the [cap rate calculator](/cap-rate/).

A lender will run the same exercise, and usually more harshly. If the bank underwrites to $228,400 and you agreed to a price built on $285,600, the loan comes in short and you make up the difference in cash.

## The five lies of omission

These are the facts a rent roll usually leaves off. Ask for each one in writing.

**1. Concessions.** Free rent, reduced rent in the first year, or a landlord-funded build-out still owed to the tenant. A lease that shows $24.00 with six months free is not a $24.00 lease this year.

**2. Delinquency.** The roll shows rent due, not rent received. Ask for a twelve-month collections report or the deposit records. A tenant three months behind is a vacancy that has not happened yet.

**3. Termination and kick-out rights.** A lease expiring in 2031 with a tenant right to cancel in 2027 is a 2027 lease.

**4. Related parties.** Any tenant owned by, related to, or guaranteed by the seller. That rent may be real, but it leaves when the seller does unless a new arm's-length lease is signed at closing.

**5. Pending changes.** A tenant who has given notice, asked for a rent reduction, or stopped operating while still paying. The manager knows. The page does not say.

Also ask for the expense side. A gross lease where the landlord absorbs a rising tax bill is worth less every year than the base rent suggests.

## How to verify what the page says

A rent roll is a claim. Three documents turn it into a fact.

- **The leases and every amendment.** Read the rent, the term, the options, and the reimbursement language yourself or have your attorney do it. Amendments are where concessions hide.
- **Collections.** Twelve months of deposits or a rent ledger, matched tenant by tenant against the roll.
- **Estoppels.** An [estoppel certificate](/glossary/estoppel-certificate/) is a signed statement from each tenant confirming its rent, its term, its deposit, and that the landlord is not in default. Make delivery of estoppels a condition of closing. Your attorney drafts that into the purchase and sale agreement.

Any line that disagrees across those three sources gets a written question. Do this inside the due diligence period, not after it.

The same reading applies to an offering memorandum, where the rent roll usually sits near the back and the pro forma sits near the front. Read the back first.

## What to do this week

1. Take the rent roll for any building you are considering and cross out every line marked vacant or pro forma.
2. Circle every lease that expires or can be terminated within 24 months, and total what remains.
3. Look up each tenant's legal entity and ask the seller directly whether any is related to ownership.
4. Request the leases, all amendments, and twelve months of collections before you make an offer, not after.
5. Recompute the price on in-place income using the [cap rate calculator](/cap-rate/), then put your requirements in a [Buyer Brief](/buyer-brief/) so sellers know what you will underwrite.
