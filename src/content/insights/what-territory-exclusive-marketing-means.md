Here's a question to ask your marketing agency this week: *"Do you work for any of my competitors?"*

Most owners have never asked. Most agencies hope they never do. Because the honest answer, more often than anyone admits, is yes — the same strategist, the same ad templates, the same "proven playbook," running for you and the business across the street, with both of you paying for the privilege of competing against your own vendor.

## The conflict nobody discloses

Agencies specialize by vertical for a reason: once the roofing playbook works, the cheapest thing in the world is to sell it to the next roofer. Nothing in a standard agreement stops them. Your competitor gets your creative, your keyword strategy, your landing-page structure — and in a small market, the two of you bid the same search terms against each other while the agency books both retainers.

That isn't malice. It's the business model. But it means "we'll help you dominate your market" is being promised to several people in the same market at once, and arithmetic says most of them were lied to.

## What exclusivity actually means

Territory-exclusive marketing is a written promise with four parts. If any one is missing, you don't have exclusivity — you have a feeling.

1. **The category, defined plainly.** "Roofing," not "home services." A roofer and a dentist can share a town; two roofers cannot.
2. **The territory, named.** Towns, listed. Not "a 15-mile radius" that moves when it's convenient.
3. **The term, and who renews first.** A year is the honest unit. You should hold first right of renewal; if you walk, the territory reopens.
4. **The consequence.** The firm *declines* competing engagements for the term — and tells the competitor why. That refusal is the entire product.

## Why most firms won't offer it

Because it caps their revenue. An agency that can sell the playbook to every roofer on the South Shore has a bigger market than one that sells it to a single roofer per town. Exclusivity means turning away money on purpose, in writing, and most firms would rather keep the clause vague and the door open.
