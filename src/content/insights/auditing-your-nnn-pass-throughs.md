Every spring, tenants in South Shore plazas and industrial parks get an envelope. Inside is a one-page statement that says last year's operating costs came in higher than estimated, and here is what you owe. Most tenants pay it. A few get angry and call a lawyer.

There is a better third path. Read the lease, ask for the backup, check five things, and write a short, civil letter. In our experience most reconciliation errors are bookkeeping, not bad faith, and most landlords fix them when shown the lease language.

ICS's founder is a licensed Massachusetts real estate salesperson, not an attorney. This note covers how to review a statement. Your attorney handles a dispute that the letter does not resolve.

## How pass-throughs work

Under an [NNN lease](/glossary/nnn-lease/) the tenant pays base rent plus its share of three things: real estate taxes, building insurance, and common area maintenance, or CAM. Together these are the [pass-throughs](/glossary/pass-throughs/).

The landlord estimates the year's costs in advance and bills the tenant monthly. After the year closes, the landlord totals actual costs, computes each tenant's share, and compares it to what the tenant paid. The difference is a bill or a credit. That annual settling up is the [CAM reconciliation](/glossary/cam-reconciliation/).

Your share is normally a fraction: your square feet over the building's leasable square feet. A 3,000 square foot tenant in a 30,000 square foot plaza pays 10 percent.

Three places an error can enter: what goes into the pool of costs, what fraction is applied, and whether your payments were credited correctly. A review checks all three.

## Start with the lease, not the statement

Before you look at a single number, read four clauses. The [lease clause library](/lease-clauses/) explains each in plain terms.

- **The definition of operating expenses.** What is included, and the list of exclusions. Capital improvements, leasing commissions, the landlord's legal fees, and costs covered by insurance are commonly excluded.
- **The pro rata share.** The stated percentage or the formula, and what the denominator is: total leasable area or only leased area.
- **Caps and limits.** Some leases cap annual increases in controllable costs, often at 3 to 5 percent. Some limit the management fee to a percentage of rent.
- **The audit clause.** Whether you may review the landlord's books, the deadline, who may do the review, and who pays for it.

Write the deadline down first. If the lease says objections must be made within 60 days of the statement, day 61 is too late no matter how right you are.

If you no longer have a clean copy of the lease and every amendment, ask the landlord for one. That is an ordinary request.

## A worked example: one statement, two findings

A 3,000 square foot retail tenant in a 30,000 square foot plaza on Route 18 gets this statement. Share: 10 percent.

| Line | Actual cost |
|---|---|
| Snow removal and landscaping | $38,000 |
| Repairs and maintenance | $21,000 |
| Parking lot repaving | $28,000 |
| Common utilities | $9,000 |
| Management fee | $24,000 |
| Administrative fee | $11,000 |
| **Total CAM** | **$131,000** |

Tenant's 10 percent share: $13,100. Estimates paid during the year: $9,600. Balance due: $3,500.

Two lines stand out.

**Repaving, $28,000.** A new parking surface lasts well over a decade. That makes it a [capital expenditure](/glossary/capital-expenditures/), not an annual operating cost. This lease excludes capital items except when amortized over their useful life. Spread over 15 years, the proper charge this year is about $1,867, not $28,000. The pool drops by $26,133. The tenant's share of that: $2,613.

**Administrative fee, $11,000.** The lease allows a management fee. It does not mention a separate administrative fee on top. Removing it lowers the pool by $11,000, and the tenant's share by $1,100.

Together: $3,713. The $3,500 balance due becomes a $213 credit. More important, the repaving does not get billed again next year at full cost.

## The five checks that find most errors

Run these on any statement.

1. **Capital versus operating.** Roofs, repaving, new HVAC units, and structural work. Check the lease's exclusion list and any amortization language.
2. **Fees on fees.** A management fee and an administrative fee both charged, or a fee calculated on a base that already includes the fee, or one above the lease's limit.
3. **The denominator.** Confirm your share uses the square footage the lease specifies. If the landlord divides by leased area and the plaza is 20 percent vacant, you are carrying part of the empty space. Some leases allow that through a gross-up clause. Many do not.
4. **Costs that are not common.** One tenant's grease trap service, one tenant's dumpster, or work on vacant suites to prepare them for new tenants. Those belong to that tenant or to the landlord.
5. **Arithmetic and credits.** Add up your twelve estimate payments from your own bank records. Check that taxes match the town's actual bill, which is public, and that any abatement the landlord won was passed through.

Then compare this year's statement to the last two. A line that jumps 40 percent deserves one question.

## How to ask without starting a war

Tone decides how this goes. The landlord's bookkeeper is a person with 40 statements to send, and your letter is either a reasonable question or a threat.

A good first letter does four things:

- States that you received the statement and are reviewing it under the lease's audit section, by number.
- Asks for specific backup: the general ledger for CAM, invoices for the two or three lines in question, and the square footage used in the calculation.
- Asks questions instead of making accusations. "Can you tell us how the repaving cost was treated under Section 6.2?" lands better than "You overcharged us."
- Proposes a date for a response and confirms you will keep paying in the meantime.

Send it well inside the deadline. Keep it to one page.

If the backup confirms an error, ask for a corrected statement and a credit against future charges. A credit is easier for a landlord to give than a check, and it settles the matter faster.

If the landlord refuses to provide backup the lease entitles you to, or the amount is large, that is when your attorney writes the next letter. Some leases bar contingency-fee auditors, so check before hiring one.

## What to do this week

1. Find your lease and every amendment, and mark the operating expense definition, the exclusions, and the audit clause.
2. Calendar the objection deadline for your most recent statement.
3. Total your own estimate payments for the year from bank records and compare them to the credit shown.
4. Pull the town's tax bill for the property and compare it to the tax line you were charged.
5. Draft a one-page letter requesting backup for the two largest or fastest-growing lines.
