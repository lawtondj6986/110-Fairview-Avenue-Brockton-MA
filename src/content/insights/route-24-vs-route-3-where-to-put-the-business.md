Every South Shore business that has outgrown its space ends up asking the same question in different words: should we be out by Route 24 or over by Route 3? The question is usually asked about rent. It should be asked about customers.

The two highways run roughly parallel, ten to fifteen miles apart, from the edge of Boston toward the Cape and the Rhode Island line. They carry different people to different places. Route 24 is the inland working corridor, with Brockton at its center. Route 3 is the coastal consumer corridor, with the Hingham and Hanover retail clusters near the top and Plymouth at the bottom. Picking the wrong one does not kill a business. It just makes the business pay more for less of the right customer for the life of the lease.

Here is how to think it through by trade and by customer.

## The two corridors in one table

| | Route 24 corridor | Route 3 corridor |
|---|---|---|
| Towns | Randolph, Avon, Brockton, West Bridgewater, Bridgewater, Raynham, Taunton | Braintree, Weymouth, Hingham, Rockland, Hanover, Norwell, Pembroke, Marshfield, Duxbury, Kingston, Plymouth |
| Population character | Denser, more renters, more multi-family, more blue-collar employment | Lower density, more owner-occupied single family, higher household income |
| Dominant commercial stock | Flex, industrial, distribution, auto, contractor yards, older strip retail | Retail plazas, medical, professional office, restaurants, marine, newer strip retail |
| Typical occupancy cost | Lower | Higher |
| Traffic pattern | Weekday commuter and truck, steady year round | Weekday commuter, heavy seasonal and weekend coastal traffic |
| Permitting | Generally faster in Brockton and Taunton, which are cities with planning staff | Town meeting towns, slower, more neighbor involvement |

The [Route 24 corridor guide](/corridors/route-24/) and the [Route 3 corridor guide](/corridors/route-3/) carry the town-by-town detail. The rest of this article is about matching your business to the right side.

## Trades and home services: follow the housing stock

A plumbing, electrical, HVAC, or roofing company sells to homeowners and property managers. The question is where the service calls come from and how far the trucks drive.

Route 24 towns have more housing units per square mile and more aging housing, which produces more repair calls per truck-hour. Route 3 towns have larger homes, higher incomes, more replacement and renovation work, and higher average tickets. A $9,000 heating system replacement is more common in Hingham than in Brockton; a $350 emergency call is more common in Brockton than in Hingham.

A worked example. An HVAC contractor with six trucks is choosing between a 6,000-square-foot flex building in Brockton near the Route 27 interchange and a comparable one in Hanover near Route 139. Brockton rents at roughly $11 per square foot triple-net, Hanover at roughly $15, in our experience. The difference is $24,000 a year. But the Hanover shop reaches Hingham, Norwell, Scituate, Duxbury, and Marshfield within 20 minutes, where the company's average replacement ticket runs 30 percent higher. If the company does 180 replacements a year at an average of $8,500 from Brockton or $11,000 from Hanover, the location difference is $450,000 in revenue against $24,000 in rent.

The arithmetic flips for a company whose work is mostly service calls and property management contracts. Then volume and density win, and Brockton or Avon is right. The [trades playbook](/industries/trades-home-services/playbook/) covers the lead-flow side of this.

## Consumer retail, restaurants, and personal services: follow the income

A restaurant, salon, fitness studio, or specialty retailer lives on foot traffic and disposable income within a ten-minute drive. Route 3 wins most of these decisions, and the rents say so. The Derby Street area in Hingham, the Route 53 and Route 139 clusters in Hanover and Pembroke, and the Kingston retail node near the Route 3 and Route 44 junction all draw from higher-income households and from the seasonal population heading to the coast.

Route 24 retail is not dead. It is a different business. Brockton supports a dense, diverse customer base that national chains underserve, and independent restaurants, grocers, and service businesses that speak to that market do well with lower rent. Randolph and Avon pick up Route 139 and I-93 traffic. Taunton has rebuilt its retail around the Route 140 and Route 44 corridor.

The question for a consumer business is simple: what does your average customer spend, and how many of them live within ten minutes of the door? The [compare towns tool](/south-shore/compare/) puts household and business counts for any two South Shore towns side by side.

## Medical, dental, and professional services: follow the referral pattern

A dental practice, physical therapy clinic, accounting firm, or law office draws from a fifteen-minute radius and from referrals. Both corridors support these, but the economics differ.

Route 3 practices charge more and see more commercially insured patients. They also pay more for space, compete with established practices that have been in Hingham and Hanover for decades, and face slower permitting for signage and parking changes. Route 24 practices, especially in Brockton and Taunton, serve a larger population with a different payer mix, pay less for space, and often find it easier to recruit staff who live nearby.

A second worked example. A two-dentist practice needs 3,000 square feet. In a Route 3 medical building in Weymouth near the Route 18 exit, rent is roughly $28 per square foot gross, about $84,000 a year. In a Brockton medical building near the hospital district, roughly $20 per square foot gross, $60,000 a year. The Weymouth location has about 40 percent more households earning over $150,000 within fifteen minutes. The Brockton location has about 35 percent more total households in the same drive time. Which one is right depends on whether the practice is built on elective and cosmetic work or on volume and family dentistry. Neither is wrong. Choosing without knowing is.

## Distribution, light manufacturing, and anything with trucks

This is the clearest call. Route 24 connects directly to I-93, I-495, and Route 140, with full interchanges that handle tractor-trailers and an industrial base in Brockton, Avon, West Bridgewater, and Taunton built for it. Route 3 has limited industrial land, interchanges designed for commuters, and towns that do not want truck traffic.

If your business receives or ships by truck more than a few times a week, you belong on the Route 24 side or along the Route 18 and Route 139 connectors in Abington, Whitman, and Rockland, which are the overlap zone. The [Route 18 guide](/corridors/route-18/) covers that stretch.

## The overlap zone

Rockland, Abington, Whitman, Hanson, and Holbrook sit between the two highways. Route 18, Route 139, and Route 123 cross between them. A business located here reaches both the Brockton market and the Hanover and Hingham market in 20 to 25 minutes, usually at rents closer to the Route 24 side than the Route 3 side. For a trade company that wants Route 3 customers and Route 24 costs, this is the answer more often than either highway.

The tradeoff is identity. A business in Rockland is not a Hingham business to a Hingham customer, and the marketing has to carry that distance.
