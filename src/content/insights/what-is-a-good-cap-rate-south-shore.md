Every conversation about an income property gets to the cap rate within two minutes. It is the number on the listing, the number in the appraisal, and the number the investor across the table will quote back at you. It is also, in our experience on the South Shore, the number most owners and first-time buyers understand least.

This article explains what the cap rate measures, what buildings here actually trade at, and the three ways the number misleads people who treat it as a verdict instead of a question.

## What a cap rate is

A capitalization rate is a property's net operating income divided by its price.

Net operating income, or NOI, is the money the building produces after the costs of running it and before the costs of financing it: rent and other income, minus taxes, insurance, maintenance, management, utilities the owner pays, and a reserve for replacements. Mortgage payments are not an operating expense. Neither are income taxes or depreciation.

Price is what someone paid, or what a seller is asking.

A building that produces $70,000 of NOI and sells for $1,000,000 traded at a 7 percent cap rate. Turn the formula around and it becomes a pricing tool: at a 7 percent cap rate, every dollar of NOI is worth about $14.29 of price. Raise the NOI by $5,000 and the building is worth about $71,000 more. Lower the cap rate to 6.5 percent and the same $70,000 supports a price of about $1,077,000.

That is the whole mechanism. Cap rate is a yield if you paid cash, and it is the exchange rate between income and value.

## What South Shore buildings trade at

Cap rates are not set by formula. They are observed from sales, and they move with interest rates, with the perceived risk of the income, and with how many buyers want that type of building.

On the South Shore, recent sales have clustered roughly like this:

- Stabilized retail or medical with a strong tenant on a long lease, on a main corridor: around 6 to 7.5 percent.
- Multi-tenant strip centers and newer flex industrial with staggered leases: around 7 to 8 percent.
- Older mixed-use downtown buildings, small industrial with an owner-user buyer pool, and anything with a lease rolling in the next year or two: around 7.5 to 9 percent, sometimes higher.

These are ranges, not rules, and they shift. The point is the pattern: the safer and more passive the income, the lower the cap rate and the higher the price per dollar of income. The more work, risk, or uncertainty, the higher the cap rate. A high cap rate is the market saying there is something here you will have to deal with.

## The three ways cap rates mislead

### 1. The NOI is somebody's opinion

Two buyers can agree completely on a cap rate and disagree by $200,000 on price, because they disagree on NOI. Sellers present trailing actuals, which may omit a management fee because the owner managed it himself, show taxes from before the reassessment a sale will trigger, and carry no reserve for the roof that is fifteen years old. Buyers underwrite with all three. The seller's $95,000 NOI becomes the buyer's $78,000, and at the same 7 percent cap rate the building is worth $1,357,000 to one and $1,114,000 to the other.

When you hear a cap rate, ask whose NOI it is dividing.

### 2. A higher cap rate is not a bargain

New investors look for the highest cap rate on the market and feel clever. The market is not that careless. A building offered at a 9.5 percent cap rate when comparable buildings trade at 7.5 is telling you something: the tenant is weak, the lease is short, the roof is failing, the location is sliding, or the seller's NOI is fiction. Sometimes the something is manageable and the deal is good. The cap rate did not tell you that; the due diligence did.

### 3. Owner-users are measuring the wrong thing

Most sub-$3M buildings on the South Shore are bought by the businesses that will occupy them. For those buyers, cap rate is a side note. The relevant comparison is the rent they are paying now versus the total cost of owning, over the period they expect to stay, including the equity they build and the control they gain. A contractor paying $7,500 a month in rent who can buy a building with 10 percent down through an SBA 504 loan at a slightly higher monthly cost is not making a cap rate decision. He is making a lease-versus-buy decision, and our calculator for that is built for exactly his situation.

Cap rate still matters to an owner-user in one way: it tells you what an investor would pay for the building later, which is the floor under your exit.

## How to use the number well

Compute it yourself, from a NOI you have rebuilt with real taxes, a management fee, a vacancy allowance, and a reserve. Compare it to recent sales of genuinely similar buildings, not to a regional average that mixes Hingham medical with Brockton industrial. When the cap rate on a listing is far from the comparable set in either direction, treat that gap as the first question of your due diligence, not the last word on the price.
