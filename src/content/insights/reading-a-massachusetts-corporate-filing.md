Before you sign a contract with a company, lease space from one, or wire a deposit to one, there is a free public page that tells you whether that company legally exists and who is allowed to sign for it. It takes four minutes to read.

Most people never look. The ones who do often read it wrong: they treat the list of officers as a list of owners, or they see "good standing" and assume the business is healthy. The record says less than that, and it also says more, if you read the filing history instead of the summary.

This is how to read a Massachusetts corporate filing, line by line.

## Search four ways, not one

The Corporations Division search accepts four kinds of input. Each answers a different question.

- **Entity name.** The obvious one. Search a fragment, not the full name, because punctuation and "LLC" versus "L.L.C." can hide a match.
- **Individual name.** The most useful and the least used. It returns every entity where that person is listed as an officer, director, manager, or agent.
- **Identification number.** The state's own ID for the entity. Use it once you have it, because names change and numbers do not.
- **Filing number.** For pulling one specific document.

If the name on the truck or the storefront returns nothing, the business may be a sole proprietorship or partnership operating under a trade name. Those file a [business certificate](/glossary/business-certificate-dba/) with the city or town clerk, not the state, and the certificate is good for four years. It may also be an out-of-state entity that never registered here, which is its own finding.

## The summary page, line by line

The top of the record carries the facts that matter most.

- **Exact name and entity type.** Domestic profit corporation, LLC, foreign corporation, nonprofit. The exact name is what belongs on your contract.
- **Date of organization.** How old the entity is. A contractor claiming twenty years in business under an LLC formed in March has an explanation to give. Often a good one: owners reorganize.
- **Jurisdiction.** A "foreign" entity was formed in another state, commonly Delaware, and is registered to do business here. Its formation documents are in the home state.
- **Principal office.** Where the company says it operates.
- **Resident agent.** The person or service that accepts lawsuits and official notices. See [registered agent](/glossary/registered-agent/). An agent who is the owner at a home address is a small operation. An agent that is a commercial service tells you nothing either way.
- **Status.** Active, or some form of dissolved, withdrawn, or revoked, with a date.

Compare the principal office and agent address with what the company told you. Mismatches are common and usually innocent. They are still worth one question.

## Officers, managers, and the owner who is not listed

What a company must disclose depends on its type.

A **corporation** lists its president, treasurer, secretary, and directors. Those are the people with authority to act. Shareholders are not listed.

An **LLC** lists its managers if it has any, the persons authorized to execute documents filed with the state, and the persons authorized to sign instruments affecting real property. Members, the actual owners, appear only if they hold one of those roles.

So the record answers "who can sign?" well and "who owns this?" poorly. That distinction matters in two directions. If the person across the table is not listed anywhere on the filing, ask for a written resolution or certificate showing their authority before you rely on their signature. And if you need to know the real owners, the filing is where the trail starts, not where it ends. The glossary entry on [beneficial ownership](/glossary/beneficial-ownership/) covers what can and cannot be established from public sources.

## Annual reports: the filing history is the real document

Scroll past the summary to the list of filings. Every Massachusetts corporation and LLC owes an annual report. A corporation's is due within two and a half months after its fiscal year ends. An LLC's is due on or before the anniversary of its formation. The LLC fee is currently $500 a year, and a corporation's is roughly a quarter of that. Check the current fee schedule, because fees change.

Read the dates, not just the count. A worked example, a composite LLC formed in June 2019:

| Report year | Filed |
|---|---|
| 2020 | June 2020 |
| 2021 | June 2021 |
| 2022 | February 2023 |
| 2023 | Not filed |
| 2024 | Not filed |
| 2025 | Both back years and 2025 filed the same day, September 2025 |

Three reports in one day is $1,500 in fees paid at once. That pattern almost always means an outside event forced the cleanup: a lender, a buyer, or a title company asked for a certificate of good standing and the company could not produce one. So something was closing or refinancing in September 2025. That is a useful thing to know about a counterparty, and it is sitting in plain view.

Also compare one annual report with the next. A manager who appears in 2021 and is gone in 2022 left, or was removed. The [annual report filing](/glossary/annual-report-filing/) entry explains what each report must contain.

## What a lapse means, and what it does not

When reports go unfiled for two or more years, the Secretary of the Commonwealth can dissolve the entity administratively. The status changes to involuntary dissolution or revocation.

What that means in practice:

- The entity cannot get a [certificate of good standing](/glossary/certificate-of-good-standing/), so it cannot easily close a loan, sell real estate, or pass a careful counterparty's review.
- Its authority to enter new contracts is open to challenge, and the individuals who keep operating may be taking on personal exposure. How far that goes is a legal question: talk to counsel before you sign with a dissolved entity.
- It can usually be revived by filing the missing reports and paying the back fees.

What it does not mean: that the business is broke, closed, or dishonest. Plenty of profitable South Shore companies lapse because the bookkeeper left and the reminder went to an old address. A lapse is evidence of inattention. One lapse is a question. A pattern is an answer.

Here is the pattern test. Search the manager's name as an individual. A composite result: seven entities over twelve years, three active, four involuntarily dissolved, two of the dissolved ones formed within a year of a predecessor with a nearly identical name. That is no longer one missed reminder. That is how this person runs companies, and it belongs in the file next to the court search described in our guide to [vetting a business partner](/insights/how-to-vet-a-business-partner-before-a-deal/).

## What the filing does not show, and where ICS stops

The corporate record does not show revenue, debts, tax status, lawsuits, or, for most LLCs, owners. A current filing is not an endorsement. A clean record on a six-week-old entity is a blank page, not a clean history.

ICS reads these filings as public documents and follows the entity trail through other public records. We do not pretext: nobody calls a bank, an accountant, or the company itself under a false identity to learn who the owners are. We do not surveil private individuals or research anyone's personal life. We do not obtain medical, credit, or private financial data. And ICS does not produce reports for employment, credit, or residential tenant decisions, which belong to licensed consumer reporting agencies.

## What to do this week

1. Look up your own entity and confirm the status, the principal office, and the agent address are current.
2. Count your annual reports against the years you have existed and file anything missing.
3. Look up the three counterparties you rely on most: landlord, largest customer, largest vendor.
4. Search each of their listed managers or officers by individual name and note every dissolved entity.
5. Put the exact legal name from the record, not the trade name, on your next contract.
6. For a counterparty in a real estate deal, add the findings to a free [Buyer Brief](/buyer-brief/) before you make an offer.
