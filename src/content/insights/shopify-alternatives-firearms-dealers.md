If you hold an FFL and you've tried to put your store online, you already know the punchline: the platforms that power the rest of retail don't want you.

Shopify's acceptable-use rules prohibit or restrict firearm sales, and its built-in payment processing won't touch the category. The story repeats across most mainstream site builders — and even when a workaround exists, you're building a business on a platform that has already told you, in writing, that it doesn't want your category. That's not a foundation; that's a countdown.

So what actually works? We've shipped in this category — [a live-inventory storefront for Outback Arms in Plympton, MA](/case-studies/outback-arms-retail-platform/), a family-run Massachusetts dealer whose store reports business up 300% since launch — and our founder works that counter. Here's the honest landscape.

## Option 1: Turnkey gun-store platforms

There's a small industry of category-specific platforms built for firearms retail — distributor catalog feeds, FFL-aware checkout flows, compliance-shaped templates. They're real options, and for some shops the right one.

**The honest trade-offs:** you rent. Monthly platform fees plus transaction cuts, templates that make your store look like every other store on the same platform, catalog data shared with every competitor pulling the same feed, and your storefront's rules set by someone else's roadmap. You're no longer on a platform that *prohibits* you — but you're still a tenant.

## Option 2: General builders with workarounds

Some dealers run a mainstream builder for the "brochure" part of the site and push transactions elsewhere, or lean on third-party plugins to thread the policy needle. It can limp along. But the arrangement sits on terms-of-service goodwill, and categories like this one don't get the benefit of the doubt when policies tighten. The checkout that works today can vanish on a compliance sweep — we've watched it happen to shops mid-season.

## Option 3: Custom open code you own

This is what we built for Outback Arms, and the architecture is simple to say and hard to evict:

- **The website shows the real shelf.** One live inventory database drives the site and the admin panel — stock, pricing, photos, synced in real time. The customer forty minutes away checks what's actually in the case before driving.
- **The owner runs it from the counter.** List a product, change a price, mark it sold — in minutes, between customers, no developer, no ticket queue.
- **Reserve online, complete in store.** Customers commit on the website; background checks, transfers, and paperwork happen at the counter, per your license and your process — the site accelerates the sale without ever shortcutting compliance.
- **Payment architecture that serves the category** — processors that genuinely work with firearms merchants, so checkout doesn't evaporate mid-stream.
- **Owned visibility.** Meta and Google won't run your product ads, which makes local SEO, structured product data, review velocity, and answer-engine visibility the whole game. A custom build lets us engineer all of it at the product level — so "who has one in stock near me" finds your live shelf.

The trade-off here is honest too: custom costs more upfront than a template. What you get for it is an asset instead of a rent bill — code you own outright, on infrastructure nobody can pull out from under you, shaped to how your counter actually runs.

## How to decide

- **Testing whether online matters for your shop?** A turnkey platform is a reasonable first experiment.
- **Already know your customers check stock online, and tired of tenant risk?** Own the storefront. The math on a custom build against years of platform fees and transaction cuts is closer than most dealers expect — and only one of those paths ends with an asset.
