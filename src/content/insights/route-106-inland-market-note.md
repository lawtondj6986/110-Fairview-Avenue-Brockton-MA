Route 106 does not have a nickname and does not need one. It leaves Mansfield as Eastman Street, crosses Easton, meets Route 24 and Route 28 at the West Bridgewater crossroads, runs through East Bridgewater's center, and continues through Halifax and Plympton to Kingston and Route 3A. It is an east-west road through working towns, and the stock is what working towns build: yards, small industrial, auto and truck repair, and the plaza at each center.

Almost nothing on it lists. When a building changes hands it usually goes to a tenant, a neighbor, or the buyer who wrote a letter two years ago. The portals show what the relationship market passed on.

This note covers what the product costs, why it moves this way, and how a buyer gets into the conversation first. Numbers are typical bands from our reads, not named transactions.

## The road and its three crossings

Route 106 is best read by its crossings, because each is a different market.

The west end is [Mansfield](/south-shore/mansfield/) and [Easton](/south-shore/easton/). Mansfield's stretch sits between Cabot Business Park and the downtown, with the deepest industrial inventory south of Boston minutes away. Easton's passes the town's industrial pockets on the way to Five Corners, in a town that trades at a premium.

The middle is the crossroads. [West Bridgewater](/south-shore/west-bridgewater/) is where 106 meets Route 24 and Route 28; the Manley Street corridor and the land around the interchange hold one of the densest runs of industrial, contractor, and distribution property on the South Shore. [East Bridgewater](/south-shore/east-bridgewater/) is the hold-forever town just east, where 106 crosses Route 18 and the stock is small plazas, service buildings, and contractor properties on the side roads.

The east end is [Halifax](/south-shore/halifax/), [Plympton](/south-shore/plympton/), and [Kingston](/south-shore/kingston/). Halifax clusters along 106 as it crosses town: small plazas, auto, and contractor properties with yard space at prices the inner towns cannot offer. Plympton is the quietest market we cover. Kingston is where 106 ends, at the Route 3 retail district and the rail.

The [Route 106 corridor guide](/corridors/route-106/) covers who does business on each stretch, and the [Route 24 guide](/corridors/route-24/) explains the interchange.

## Why nothing lists

Three reasons, and a buyer should know them before complaining about inventory.

**The owners do not need to sell.** A contractor who bought a yard in 2003 owns it outright, runs the business from it, and pays modest taxes relative to what the land is worth. The decision, when it comes, is retirement, health, or an estate, on the family's schedule.

**The buyer is already known.** By the time the owner decides, the tenant in the back bay has asked twice, the excavator next door has mentioned it, and someone has sent a letter. The owner picks from people they trust. A listing is what happens when none of those conversations closed.

**The product is specific.** A fenced yard with a steel building is worth more to the contractor who needs exactly that than to the general market. Listing it broadly invites buyers who want something else there and brings the zoning question to town meeting. Selling quietly to another contractor keeps the use and the price.

A buyer who starts looking when they need the building is two years late.

## The yard: the product everyone wants

The contractor yard is the corridor's defining product: two to four acres, fenced, a steel building of 3,000 to 6,000 square feet with drive-in doors, a small office, and zoning that still permits outdoor storage.

A worked example. A two-acre fenced yard with a 4,000 square foot steel building and two drive-in doors near the West Bridgewater crossroads sits, in our reads, in a band of roughly $900,000 to $1.3 million. The same spec in Halifax or Plympton runs roughly $650,000 to $950,000; highway minutes set the spread. Renting a fenced acre with a small shop costs roughly $3,000 to $5,000 a month where it can be found.

Take the crossroads at $1.1 million. Under a typical SBA 504 structure, 10 percent down and roughly 7 percent blended over 25 years, the payment on the $990,000 financed is about $7,000 a month. A contractor renting a yard at $4,000 and a separate shop at $2,500 is already at $6,500. The owner pays slightly more, controls the yard, and stops explaining the equipment to a landlord. Terms vary by lender and business; talk to your CPA and lender first.

What moves the price inside the band: outdoor storage by right or by special permit, sewer or septic, wetlands, and the state of the gravel. A yard with a wetlands line through the back acre is a one-acre yard with a two-acre tax bill.

## The small flex building at the west end

The west end carries a second product: the 10,000 to 20,000 square foot flex building from the 1980s or 1990s, two or three tenants, drive-in doors, a fenced rear lot, and an owner who built it for their own business and rents the other half.

A worked example. A 12,000 square foot flex building in an Easton industrial pocket, two tenants on triple net leases at roughly $10 to $12 per foot, grosses about $132,000. After the landlord's share of insurance, management, and reserves, net operating income lands near $105,000. In our reads this product trades at cap rates of roughly 7.5 to 8.5 percent, a band of about $1.24 million to $1.4 million.

The owner-user angle is the half the owner occupies. A buyer who takes those 6,000 feet and keeps the other tenant owns a building that pays half its own mortgage from day one. That is why they go to the tenant or the neighbor first.

Easton prices at a premium to the towns east of it, so underwrite with discipline. Mansfield's park rents set the top of the regional industrial band, which makes older 106 product look cheap and keeps the competition for it steady.

## The east end and the distribution neighbor

East of the Bridgewaters the road quiets down, and quiet is the advantage.

Halifax's commercial property clusters where 106 crosses town: small plazas and auto buildings serving a regional radius, and contractor properties at prices that keep pulling trades operators out of the inner towns. Plympton is rural-zoned land, contractor yards, and the occasional flex or agricultural-commercial property. A buyer wanting acreage for equipment or a build-to-suit at sane pricing should have Plympton on the list before the market finds it.

The distribution activity on the corridor matters in two ways. It brings truck traffic, which towns manage with road and hours restrictions that vary parcel by parcel. And it sets a floor under land values, because a large user that needs more yard will pay. An owner who has not re-read their assessment in a decade may be sitting on more than they think.

Rules on outdoor storage, truck hours, and agricultural-commercial uses differ by town bylaw and by board. Confirm with the planning office and conservation commission before you price a parcel.

## Finding the building before the sign goes up

The off-market method is public records and patience. It is lawful, and it works.

1. **Assessor database.** Every town publishes its parcel data. Filter by zoning district and land use code along the corridor and pull owner, assessed value, and building size for every parcel that fits.
2. **Registry of deeds.** The Plymouth and Bristol County registries show when each owner bought, what they paid, and what liens sit on the parcel. Holding period is the best predictor of a willing seller: twenty years or more is the list.
3. **Corporations database.** When the owner is an LLC, the Secretary of the Commonwealth's filings name the manager and the registered agent. That is who gets the letter.
4. **The letter.** Short, specific, and honest: who you are, what you do, why their parcel fits, and that you are financed. No pressure, no price. A phone number that gets answered.
5. **The follow-up.** Once a year, every year, until the answer changes. The buyer still writing in year three is the one the family calls.

This is the work behind an ICS Buyer Brief: assessor, deed, zoning, and environmental flags on one page for every parcel on your list.

## What to do this week

1. Write your spec in three lines: acres, building size and door count, and the towns you work most.
2. Pull the assessor data for every parcel that fits and sort by how long the owner has held.
3. Check each candidate's zoning for outdoor storage and truck hours, and confirm sewer or septic, before you fall for the yard.
4. Have a lender frame the SBA 504 structure so the letter can say you are financed and mean it.
5. Send the first ten letters this week and calendar the follow-up a year out.
6. Order a [Buyer Brief](/buyer-brief/) on the top three parcels to see deed history, zoning, and environmental flags before you call.
