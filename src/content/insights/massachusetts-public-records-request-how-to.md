The Massachusetts Public Records Law, M.G.L. chapter 66, section 10, is simple in principle: every record made or received by a state agency or a city or town is public unless a specific exemption applies, and anyone may inspect or copy it. In practice, most requests die of bad wording, missed deadlines nobody enforced, or a fee estimate that was never challenged.

The law was tightened in 2017 with real deadlines, fee caps, and a presumption that a requester who has to sue and wins gets attorney's fees. Those teeth only matter if you use them. This is how we write requests at ICS, why they get answered, and what to do when they do not.

## Find the Records Access Officer

Since the 2017 reform, every state agency and every municipality must designate a Records Access Officer and post the RAO's name and contact information. In most South Shore towns the RAO is the town clerk or an assistant in the clerk's office; in Brockton the city posts a records access page. Departments with heavy request volume, such as police and building, often have their own.

Send the request to the RAO by email, and copy the department that holds the records if you know it. A request need not be in writing to be valid, but an oral request cannot be appealed, so always write. You do not need a form, you do not need to cite the statute, and you do not need to say why you want the records. Keep your own copy of exactly what you asked for and when.

One caution: in some towns, the request log is itself a public record. Do not assume the subject of your research will never learn that someone asked. The [Massachusetts Public Records Law](/glossary/massachusetts-public-records-law/) protects access, not anonymity.

## Word it for documents, not answers

The single most common failure is asking a question instead of describing a record. An RAO is not obliged to answer "why was the roof permit never closed." An RAO is obliged to produce "the permit file, including inspection records and correspondence, for the roof permit issued in 2023 for 00 Main Street." Describe records a clerk could find.

A request that gets answered usually has these parts:

- **Record type.** Permits, inspection reports, complaints, meeting minutes, contracts, invoices, emails.
- **Identifiers.** Address, parcel ID, permit number, business name, docket, meeting date.
- **Date range.** "January 1, 2022 to the date of this request." Open-ended requests get estimates designed to make you go away.
- **Format.** "Electronic copies in the format in which the records are maintained, by email." Records kept electronically should be produced electronically.
- **Fee language.** "If the cost will exceed $25, please provide an itemized estimate before proceeding." You are entitled to the estimate anyway; asking shows you know.
- **Exemption language.** "If any record or portion is withheld, please identify the specific exemption and the record withheld." Also required of them, also worth stating.
- **Narrowing offer.** "I am willing to discuss narrowing the request if it is burdensome." This is what turns a hostile estimate into a phone call.

Keep it to one page. A narrow request answered in a week beats a sweeping request appealed for three months.

## The ten-day clock

The RAO has ten business days from receipt to do one of two things: produce the records, or send a written response. If the response is not production, it must identify the records being withheld and the exemption for each, give a good-faith fee estimate, and state a reasonable date by which production will finish. An agency may set that date up to 15 business days from receipt; a municipality up to 25. Anything longer requires the RAO to petition the Supervisor of Records, who may grant an agency up to 20 more business days and a municipality up to 30, and only for cause.

A response that says "we are working on it" with no date, no exemption, and no estimate is not a compliant response, and the clock does not stop for it. Silence past day ten is a denial you can appeal immediately. Write the receipt date on your copy and count business days, skipping state holidays.

## Fees under 950 CMR 32

The fee rules live in the Supervisor of Records' regulations, 950 CMR 32.00, and the current limits are roughly these. A municipality may charge for search and segregation time only after the first two hours, at a rate not exceeding $25 per hour, or the hourly rate of the lowest-paid employee capable of the work if lower. A town with fewer than 20,000 residents may charge from the first hour. A state agency may charge after four free hours. Black-and-white copies are capped at five cents a page. There is no charge for producing records that already exist electronically, beyond the time to find and review them. An RAO who wants to charge more must petition the Supervisor first. Rules change; check the current regulation.

An estimate of 14 hours at $25 is $300 for a small town's time, and it is almost always a sign the request was too broad, not that the records are too deep.

## A worked example: the Rockland plaza

A client was buying a 12,000-square-foot retail plaza in [Rockland](/south-shore/rockland/) and wanted the town's full file. The first draft asked for "all records relating to" the address. The estimate came back at 11 hours, less two free, at $25: $225, plus a 25-business-day timeline.

The rewrite asked for four specific things: all building permits and inspection records for the parcel from 2015 forward; Board of Health complaints and orders for the same period; fire department inspection reports for the same period; and the two most recent municipal lien certificates issued for the parcel. Electronic copies, by email. The RAO's revised estimate was under two hours. Cost: $0. Production took eight business days. The file showed an open electrical permit from 2019 and a Board of Health order about a grease trap, and the seller credited $14,000 at closing to resolve both.

Same parcel, same town, same law. The difference was the wording.

## Appeals: the Supervisor of Records

If the RAO misses the deadline, denies the request, claims an exemption you think is wrong, or quotes a fee you think is inflated, you may appeal to the Supervisor of Records in the Secretary of the Commonwealth's office within 90 days. The appeal is a short letter with a copy of your request and the response, if any. The Supervisor issues a written determination within 10 business days and may order the agency or town to produce the records or revise the fee.

If the custodian ignores the Supervisor, the next step is Superior Court, where the 2017 reform created a presumption that a requester who prevails recovers attorney's fees, and allows punitive damages up to $5,000 for bad faith. Talk to counsel before filing suit; the Supervisor's order alone resolves most disputes.

## What the law does not reach, and where ICS stops

The exemptions are listed in chapter 4, section 7, clause 26, and the ones you will meet most are personnel and medical files, investigatory materials, records whose release would be an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy, and records specifically protected by other statutes, including criminal offender record information. The law also does not cover the Legislature, the judiciary, or the Governor's office; court records have their own public access rules, which is why dockets come from the courts, not from an RAO.

ICS uses this law for what it is: a tool to obtain public records about properties, businesses, licenses, contracts, meetings, and public officials acting in public roles, read alongside registry and court records. We do not use it to assemble files on private individuals' personal lives. We do not pretext, impersonate, or surveil. We do not seek medical, credit, or private financial data by any method, and nothing we produce is used for employment, credit, or tenant decisions governed by the FCRA. A public records request gathered that way can be handed to anyone, which is the point. The [Open Meeting Law](/glossary/open-meeting-law/) is the companion statute for minutes and recordings, and the two are usually requested together.

## What to do this week

1. Find the RAO for the town or agency that holds the record you need, and note the email address.
2. Write a one-page request: record type, identifiers, date range, electronic format, fee estimate over $25, exemption language, offer to narrow.
3. Send it, save a copy, and write the receipt date and the ten-business-day deadline on it.
4. If the estimate is high, call and narrow before you pay or appeal.
5. If day ten passes in silence, appeal to the Supervisor of Records the same week.
6. If the records are for a deal with money on it, run the [60-second scoper](/intelligence/scope/) to see whether a full file is warranted.
