Ask a South Shore business owner who owns their building how it happened, and you will rarely hear a story about a plan. You will hear about a landlord who called one day, a building down the street that came up, a lease clause someone insisted on years earlier, or a retirement that created an opening. The paths are different, but there are only four of them, and a business that understands all four can position for whichever one arrives.

This is written for the contractor, the practice owner, the shop owner, or the small manufacturer who pays rent every month and has started to wonder what that money could be building instead.

## First, the honest arithmetic

Owning is not automatically better than renting. It fixes your occupancy cost, builds equity, and gives you control of your premises for as long as you want them. It also concentrates your capital in one asset, makes moving expensive, and puts the roof on your side of the ledger.

The comparison that matters is total cost over the period you expect to stay, including the equity you build and the rent increases you avoid, against the flexibility you give up. Our free Lease vs. Buy tool runs that comparison with your rent, a candidate price, your down payment, and today's rates over ten years. Run it before you fall in love with a building. For many South Shore businesses paying $5,000 to $10,000 a month in rent, the result is closer than they expect in year one and decisively in favor of owning by year five, because the rent would have escalated and the loan would not.

## Path one: the right of first refusal

The cleanest path is the one you set up before you need it. A right of first refusal in your lease gives you the chance to match any offer your landlord receives before they can accept it. A right of first offer, which landlords grant more readily, requires them to offer the building to you first, at a stated or negotiated price, before marketing it.

Either clause turns the day your landlord decides to sell into your acquisition rather than your new landlord's. We have seen it change the trajectory of a business: a Stoughton tenant holding a right of first refusal matched a $1,400,000 third-party offer and bought the building it had operated from for twelve years, financed at 90 percent through an SBA 504 loan.

If you are negotiating a lease or a renewal today and the business is rooted in the location, ask for it. It costs the landlord little to grant and costs you nothing to request. The time to ask is when you have leverage, which is before you sign, not after.

## Path two: the building down the street

The most common path is not buying your current building at all. It is buying a different one, usually nearby, usually because the business has outgrown the space it rents or because a building with the right configuration came up.

What makes this path possible for small businesses is SBA financing. A conventional commercial loan wants 25 to 35 percent down. The SBA 504 program, which pairs a bank first mortgage with a fixed-rate debenture through a certified development company, lets an established business occupying at least 51 percent of an existing building buy with roughly 10 percent down. The 7(a) program does similar work with more flexibility and can fold in equipment or working capital.

The practical consequence is that an owner-user can pay more for a building than an investor can and still win, because the owner-user's financing is cheaper and their return is measured in rent avoided rather than cap rate. On the South Shore, where sub-$3M industrial and flex buildings are scarce and contested, this is why owner-users win most of them.

The constraint is inventory. The right building, with the right doors, yard, clear height, parking, or frontage, in the right towns, rarely hits a listing service before it sells. A buyer's broker who knows the owners on the corridors you care about hears about it first. That is not a sales pitch; it is how the market works here, and it is why we represent buyers as a core service.

## Path three: the quiet seller

Many South Shore commercial buildings are owned by people who built a business in them decades ago and are now thinking about retirement. Some have a child who will take over. Many do not. A meaningful share would sell to the right buyer, on the right terms, without ever listing, if someone asked at the right moment.

Sometimes that buyer is the tenant. A landlord who has had the same reliable tenant for ten years, who is tired of the roof and the snow and the phone calls, may prefer a clean sale to that tenant over the uncertainty of marketing the building. The conversation is delicate and the timing matters, and it is usually better started by an intermediary than by the tenant directly.

Sometimes that buyer is a stranger with a good introduction. An owner of a flex building in Avon or a mixed-use block in Whitman who is not ready to list may still be ready to talk to a qualified buyer who shows up with financing in place and a reasonable number. We maintain those relationships continuously, which is how a client ends up under agreement on a building that was never for sale.

Seller financing sometimes enters this path. A retiring owner with no mortgage and a desire for income may carry a note for part of the price, which can bridge a down-payment gap or ease a timing problem. It is negotiated case by case and reviewed by counsel on both sides.

## Path four: the reverse sale-leaseback

The fourth path runs backward. A business owner who already owns their building and needs capital, for growth, a partner buyout, or retirement, sells the building to an investor and signs a long lease to stay. That is a sale-leaseback, and it is the mirror image of a tenant buying.

Why mention it here? Because the investor on the other side of that transaction is sometimes another local business owner building a portfolio, and because understanding it clarifies the decision. If you would consider selling your building and leasing it back someday, buying it now is a way of banking that option. If you would never want to be a landlord, the current tenancy may suit you after all.

## Positioning for whichever path arrives

A business that wants to own its building someday can do four things today, regardless of which path shows up.

Get the right of first refusal or first offer into the current lease at the next renewal. It is the only path you can create by asking.

Clean up the financials. Every path runs through a lender, and SBA lenders in particular want two to three years of tax returns that show the business can carry the payment. The year you decide to buy is too late to start looking profitable.

Know your number. Run the lease-versus-buy comparison, know what monthly cost you can carry, and know what that supports in price at today's rates and a 10 percent down payment. A buyer who knows their ceiling can act in a week; one who does not needs a month, and the building is gone.

Tell someone who hears about buildings. Owner-users win on speed and certainty, and both depend on knowing about the opportunity early. A buyer's broker who knows what you need, in which towns, with what doors and parking, is the difference between reading about a sale and making one.

## What it looks like when it works

An HVAC company in Bridgewater paying $7,500 a month in rent engaged us with a simple brief: a building with yard space and two drive-in doors within fifteen minutes, under $1,500,000. Three weeks later an owner conversation in Avon surfaced a 5,200 square foot flex unit whose owner was thinking about retiring. The company went under agreement before it was listed, financed 90 percent through an SBA 504 loan, and now pays slightly more per month than it paid in rent, on a fixed schedule, for an asset it owns.

That is not an unusual story on the South Shore. It is the usual one. The only unusual part is that the owner knew which path he was on before it arrived.
