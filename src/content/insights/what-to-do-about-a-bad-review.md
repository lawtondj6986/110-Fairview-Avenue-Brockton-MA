It is 7:15 on a Thursday morning. A Quincy restaurant owner opens her phone and sees it: one star, two hundred words, posted at 11:40 last night. Cold food, rude server, the owner "clearly doesn't care." Her stomach drops. Her thumb is already on the reply button.

The next hour decides whether this review costs her a bad morning or a bad year. Handled well, it is a blip that twenty new reviews bury by Christmas. Handled badly, it sits at the top of her listing for a year with her angry reply attached.

Here is the hour, in order, and the short list of things never worth doing no matter how it goes.

## Minutes 0 to 15: read it twice and touch nothing

Do not reply. Do not flag. Do not text the server. Read the review twice, then put the phone down and make coffee.

The first read tells you how you feel. The second tells you what the review actually says. These are usually different. "The server was rude" might mean the server was rude. It might mean the kitchen was forty minutes behind and the server was the only face the customer saw. The job in the first fifteen minutes is to separate the claim from the sting.

Write down, privately, the two or three factual claims: cold food, rude server, waited forty minutes. Those you can check. "Doesn't care" is not a claim. It is a feeling, and you cannot rebut a feeling, so stop trying.

Then pull last night's tickets. Was there a table that matches: two guests, 8:30 seating, a sent-back entree? If yes, you have a real customer with a real complaint. If no, you have a different problem, which the next fifteen minutes are for.

## Minutes 15 to 30: figure out what kind of review this is

Not every bad review is the same thing, and the right response depends on which one it is.

- **A real customer, a real bad night.** The most common. Your tickets match. The complaint is specific. This is feedback, delivered rudely, and it gets a reply.
- **A real customer, a wrong fact.** They say they waited an hour; your system shows twenty-two minutes. Still a reply, with the fact corrected gently.
- **Wrong business.** They describe a dish you do not serve or a location on Route 3 when you are on Hancock Street. This gets flagged to Google as not about this business.
- **A former employee or a competitor.** The review knows things a customer would not, or the reviewer's other reviews are all for your competitors. Flag for conflict of interest, and document why.
- **Extortion.** A message arrives: pay me, refund me, and it comes down. Do not negotiate. Screenshot everything, flag it, and if money is demanded, talk to your attorney.
- **Fake, in bulk.** Three one-stars in a day from accounts with no other reviews. Flag each one and report the pattern.

Most owners reply to all six kinds the same way, which is why so many replies read as defensive. The [Google Business Profile](/glossary/google-business-profile/) entry lists the policy categories Google actually enforces.

## Minutes 30 to 60: the reply, written for the next hundred readers

The reply is not for the reviewer. They have moved on. It is for the hundred people who will read the review in the next month and watch how you handled it. That changes the tone.

The structure that works, in every reply we have written for a client:

1. **Thank them, by first name if shown, in one sentence.** "Thanks for letting us know about Wednesday night, Karen."
2. **Own what is true.** "A forty-minute wait is not what we want for anyone, and I am sorry you had one."
3. **Correct one fact only if it matters,** without arguing. "Our records show your entree went back and a replacement came out in nine minutes, but that is still too long."
4. **Say what you did.** "I spoke with the kitchen this morning about Saturday staffing."
5. **Take it offline.** "I would like to make it right. Please call me at the restaurant and ask for Maria."

Four or five sentences. No exclamation points. No "we strive to." No details about the customer's bill, guests, or behavior. A medical or dental office goes further: never confirm the person was a patient; acknowledge feedback and offer a phone number, nothing more.

Post it once. Never reply a second time, even when they respond. One reply is a business handling feedback. Two is an argument on your own storefront.

## When to flag to Google, and what happens when you do

Flagging is for policy violations, not for reviews you dislike. Google's list is short: off-topic content, spam or fake accounts, conflicts of interest (former employees, competitors), harassment, personal information, illegal content, and reviews about a different business. "Unfair" and "exaggerated" are not on it.

If the review fits one of those, flag it from your profile manager and write down the date and the reason. Expect a few days to a few weeks, and expect the first answer to be no; the first pass is largely automated. Appeal with a one-paragraph explanation and evidence: the ticket log showing no matching visit, the profile showing the reviewer worked for you until June. Documented appeals succeed at a reasonable rate. Undocumented ones do not.

Two things never to do while flagging. Do not reply saying you have reported it as fake; if Google leaves it up, you have called a customer a liar in public. And do not threaten legal action, in the reply or privately. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews now treats unfounded legal threats used to suppress reviews as a violation in itself.

## What never to do, and why the FTC now cares

Some responses feel natural in the first hour and are poison. Most are also, since late 2024, potential federal violations under the FTC's rule on fake reviews and testimonials, with civil penalties that run past fifty thousand dollars per violation.

- **Never gate.** Sending a survey first and asking only the happy ones for a Google review is [review gating](/glossary/review-gating/). Google removes reviews, and sometimes profiles, for it.
- **Never fake.** Not one review from a cousin, not a batch from a vendor, not a staff member posting as a customer. The rule bans fake reviews and undisclosed insider reviews.
- **Never buy or trade.** No discount for a five-star, no gift card for a review. The rule bans paying for reviews conditioned on sentiment.
- **Never pay to have it removed.** Removal services either fail or use methods that put your profile at risk.
- **Never threaten or intimidate.** Covered above, and now explicitly covered by the rule.
- **Never pretend the bad ones do not exist.** Showing only positive reviews on your own site while implying that is all of them is also in the rule's scope.

## How velocity dilutes, with the arithmetic

The real cure is arithmetic, and it starts the same morning.

Take the Quincy restaurant: 30 reviews, 4.7 average. One one-star arrives. The new average is (30 times 4.7 plus 1) divided by 31, about 4.58, which Google displays as 4.6. To get back to a displayed 4.7, she needs roughly eight new five-star reviews. At her current pace of two a month, that is four months with the one-star near the top of her list the whole time.

Now give her an automated request after every paid check, which lifts her to fifteen or twenty reviews a month. The average is back to 4.7 within three weeks. More important, the one-star has fifteen newer, more detailed reviews above it and drops off the first screen in about ten days. By the second month it is the twenty-eighth review down. Nobody scrolls that far.

The same one-star on a profile with 200 reviews at 4.7 moves the average by about 0.018, invisible. Count protects the number. Only [review velocity](/glossary/review-velocity/) protects the first screen, where the next hundred customers decide.

## What to do this week

1. Write your reply template now, while calm, in the five-sentence structure above, and keep it where you can reach it at 7:15 a.m.
2. Read your last ten reviews from a customer's phone and check that every one has a reply.
3. Pull your review count for the last 90 days; under ten means one bad review sits on top for months.
4. Confirm your review request goes to every customer, with no survey in front of it, and remove any incentive language.
5. Check whether your website's review section shows only the good ones and, if so, link to the full profile instead.
