An owner walks into a zoning board hearing with an engineer, a site plan, and a prepared statement. The board has been sitting for years. Its members have heard forty applications like this one, they have habits, and they have already said, on the record, what they think about traffic on that corridor and parking on lots that size.

All of it is written down. The Massachusetts [Open Meeting Law](/glossary/open-meeting-law/) requires every public body to keep minutes, and those minutes are public records. The applicant who read them knows what the board will ask before the board asks. The one who did not is hearing it for the first time at the podium.

The same is true before a ballot vote. Here is what minutes contain, how to read them, and what they leave out.

## What the law requires the minutes to contain

Minutes are not optional and not free-form. For every open meeting they must set out:

- The date, time, and place.
- The members present and absent.
- A summary of the discussion on each subject.
- A list of the documents and exhibits used at the meeting.
- The decisions made and actions taken, including a record of all votes.

Two of those items do most of the work for a researcher. The **vote record** tells you who voted which way, by name. The **document list** tells you what the board had in front of it: the traffic study, the peer review letter, the revised plan dated a certain day. Every document on that list is itself a public record you can ask for.

Boards are generally expected to approve minutes within the next three meetings or thirty days, whichever is later. In practice some South Shore boards post within two weeks and others run months behind. Detail varies as much as timing: one board writes four pages per hearing, another writes four lines.

## Where to find them, and what to ask for when they are missing

Most towns post agendas and minutes on the board's page of the town website. Go back at least two years. Then widen the net:

- **Agenda packets.** Many boards post the full packet, with staff memos and applicant submissions. This is often better than the minutes.
- **Recordings.** Local cable access stations record most select board, planning board, and zoning board meetings, and many keep an online archive. A recording is the transcript the minutes are not.
- **Decisions.** Zoning and planning decisions are filed with the town clerk as written documents with findings and conditions.
- **Draft minutes.** If the approved minutes are not posted, ask for the draft. A board must generally produce minutes, approved or draft, within ten days of a request.

A short written request to the board's clerk or the town's records officer is enough. Our guide to a [Massachusetts public records request](/insights/massachusetts-public-records-request-how-to/) covers the wording and the clock.

## Before a zoning hearing: read the board, not just the bylaw

The bylaw tells you what the board may do. The minutes tell you what it does. Before a hearing in front of a [zoning board of appeals](/glossary/zoning-board-of-appeals/) or a planning board, pull every hearing in the past two years for a similar use, and for your corridor. Build a simple table: applicant, use, date opened, date decided, number of sessions, vote, conditions.

Then read for five things:

1. **The count.** A [special permit](/glossary/special-permit/) on a five member board usually needs four votes. One reliable no and one absence can sink an application that has a majority. Know who the reliable no is.
2. **The standing questions.** Each member tends to ask the same thing every time. Traffic at the driveway. Lighting at the lot line. Hours.
3. **The standard conditions.** What the board attaches to nearly every approval. Offer them first.
4. **The continuances.** How many sessions approvals take, and what triggers a continuance.
5. **The peer review.** Whether the board routinely requires the applicant to fund an outside review, and what that has cost others.

Read every earlier hearing on your own parcel too. A prior owner's withdrawn application, and the reasons the board gave, is the best preview you will get.

## A worked example: a drive-through on Route 18

A composite. An owner in a South Shore town wants a special permit for a drive-through at a pad site. The engineer says it is routine.

Two years of zoning board minutes show nine special permit applications for drive-through or auto-oriented uses:

| Outcome | Count |
|---|---|
| Approved 5 to 0 | 4 |
| Approved 4 to 1 | 2 |
| Denied 3 to 2 | 1 |
| Withdrawn after a continuance | 2 |

Six of nine approved. But the detail matters more than the rate. The same member cast the dissent in both 4 to 1 votes and was one of the two no votes in the denial, each time citing queue length backing onto the road. The denial happened on a night when a usually favorable member was absent and an alternate sat. Every approval took at least three sessions. Five of the six approvals carried the same three conditions: a stacking lane for at least ten vehicles, no speaker operation after 10 p.m., and a traffic peer review paid by the applicant, recorded in the minutes at between $6,500 and $12,000.

So the owner now knows four things the engineer did not say. Budget four months and about $10,000 for peer review. Design ten stacking spaces before filing, not after the first hearing. Answer the queue question in the opening presentation, with a number. And count the members present before agreeing to open the hearing on a night when the full board is not seated.

Add the carrying cost. At $9,000 a month in taxes, insurance, and debt service on the pad, a four-month process is $36,000 before the peer review. That belongs in the offer price, not in a surprise after closing.

## Before a ballot vote: follow the number across the boards

A debt exclusion, an override, or a zoning article reaches the ballot or town meeting after months in front of several boards. Each one kept minutes. Our article on [ballot question research](/insights/ballot-question-research-what-it-looks-like/) covers the full file. The minutes technique is narrower: pick one number and follow it.

A composite fire station project, traced through the building committee, select board, and finance committee minutes:

- March 2025: feasibility estimate, $9.8 million.
- September 2025: schematic design, $11.6 million. Minutes cite a larger apparatus bay.
- February 2026: revised estimate, $13.1 million. Minutes cite escalation and site work.
- May 2026: final figure for the warrant, $14.2 million. Finance committee recommends 6 to 3.

That is a 45 percent increase in fourteen months, with each step explained on the record by the people responsible. The three dissenting finance committee members stated their reasons, and the minutes preserve them. A voter, a reporter, or a campaign on either side can now argue about the real history instead of a rumor. The dissents in the minutes are usually the strongest version of the opposing case, written by people who read the whole file.

## What minutes do not tell you, and where ICS stops

Minutes are summaries written by a board's own clerk. They compress, they omit tone, and they sometimes get things wrong. They do not record conversations outside the meeting. Executive session minutes may be withheld while the reason for secrecy lasts. Where precision matters, check the recording. For what a decision means legally, including appeal periods and vote thresholds, talk to counsel.

Minutes also name residents who spoke at public comment. What a person said on the record at a public hearing is public. It is not a reason to research that person. ICS notes abutters' stated positions as the minutes report them and goes no further: no files on private individuals, no surveillance, no pretexting to learn what a board member or a neighbor thinks privately. We do not obtain medical, credit, or private financial data, and nothing ICS produces is used for employment, credit, or residential tenant decisions.

## What to do this week

1. Find the board that will hear your matter and download two years of its minutes and agendas.
2. Build the table: applicant, use, sessions, vote by member, conditions.
3. Read every prior hearing on your own parcel and the parcels beside it.
4. Request, in writing, the documents listed in the minutes for the two most similar cases.
5. Watch the recording of the most recent comparable hearing start to finish.
6. If the hearing is part of a purchase, put the timeline and conditions into a [Buyer Brief](/buyer-brief/) before you set your price.
