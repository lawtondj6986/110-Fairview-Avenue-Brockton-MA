In Brockton, Quincy, or downtown Weymouth, nobody thinks about where the water goes. The building is on sewer. Drive twenty minutes to Hanover, Norwell, Pembroke, Hanson, or Halifax and much of the commercial stock sits on private septic systems, including plazas, office buildings, and restaurants along Route 53 and Route 139.

On those properties, a state regulation called [Title V](/glossary/title-v/) sits in the middle of every sale. Handled early, it is a one-week line item. Handled late, it is the reason a closing moves from March to July.

This note covers the general rule and the timeline. ICS's founder is a licensed Massachusetts real estate salesperson, not an attorney or an engineer. Local Boards of Health can be stricter than the state, so the town's rules are the ones that count.

## The general rule

Title V is the state environmental code for on-site sewage disposal. For a sale, the core requirement is simple: a septic system generally has to be inspected within two years before the transfer of title. That window extends to three years if the system was pumped every year and the owner has the records.

The inspection is done by a state-approved system inspector. The report goes to the local Board of Health, generally within 30 days, and a copy goes to the buyer.

There are exceptions. Certain transfers between family members and some changes in the form of ownership are treated differently. When weather prevents an inspection, the rule allows it to happen within a set period after the transfer. Large systems and shared systems, which some plazas and office parks have, follow their own inspection schedules. An inspection can also be triggered without any sale, for example by a change of use or an expansion of the building.

Do not guess which rule applies. One call to the Board of Health in the town where the property sits answers it.

## Pass, conditional pass, fail

The inspector reaches one of a few outcomes.

- **Pass.** The system functions and shows no signs of failure. The report is good for the sale, within its time window.
- **Conditional pass.** The system will pass once a specific component is repaired or replaced, such as a broken distribution box or a cracked tank cover. These are usually fixed in days for a modest sum.
- **Fail.** The system shows backup, breakout to the surface, a leaching area sitting in groundwater, or another failure criterion. It has to be repaired or upgraded, generally within two years of discovery unless the Board of Health orders it sooner.
- **Further evaluation.** The inspector cannot decide, and the Board of Health makes the determination.

A failure does not legally stop a closing. The obligation to upgrade can pass to the buyer by agreement. What stops closings is the buyer's lender, which usually will not fund against a failed system without a repair or money held back to pay for one.

## The second question: is the system big enough for the buyer's use?

This is where commercial deals differ from houses, and where buyers get hurt.

A pass says the existing system works for the existing use. Title V also assigns each use a design flow in gallons per day. At a careful generalist level, the state figures run roughly 75 gallons per day per 1,000 square feet of office, about 50 per 1,000 square feet of dry retail, and about 35 per seat for a restaurant. Confirm the current numbers with an engineer.

Now the arithmetic. A 6,000 square foot office building in Hanover has a design flow of about 450 gallons per day. A buyer wants to put a 40-seat cafe on the first floor. Forty seats at 35 gallons is 1,400 gallons per day for the cafe alone, more than three times what the whole system was built for.

The inspection passed. The deal still does not work without a new, larger system, and the lot may not have room or soil for one. Medical offices, daycares, salons, gyms with showers, and food uses of any kind all raise the same question. See [septic vs sewer](/glossary/septic-vs-sewer/) for the wider comparison.

## What a failure does to the calendar

Here is a realistic sequence on a failed system, with typical ranges. Towns vary.

| Step | Typical time |
|---|---|
| Schedule and complete inspection | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Soil evaluation and percolation test | 2 to 6 weeks, longer in winter |
| Engineer designs the new system | 2 to 4 weeks |
| Board of Health review and permit | 2 to 6 weeks |
| Installation and final sign-off | 1 to 3 weeks |

End to end, that is two to five months. Soil testing is the wild card. Many towns restrict when a [perc test](/glossary/perc-test/) can be witnessed, and frozen or saturated ground can push it out. If wetlands are near the leaching area, the Conservation Commission adds its own hearing.

Cost is just as variable. A commercial replacement on the South Shore typically runs from the tens of thousands into six figures, depending on flow, soil, and whether the site needs a raised or engineered system. Get a written estimate from an installer before anyone negotiates.

## How the money gets handled

When a system fails mid-deal, there are three common paths.

**The seller repairs before closing.** Cleanest for the buyer and the lender. It costs the seller time, and the closing date moves.

**A price reduction.** The buyer takes on the work. This suits a buyer who is redeveloping the site anyway and wants to design the system for the new use. It only works if the lender agrees.

**An escrow holdback.** Money is held from the seller's proceeds at closing to pay for the repair afterward. Lenders often want more than the estimate held, commonly around one and a half times. On a $60,000 estimate, that is $90,000 sitting in escrow until the Board of Health signs off. The exact multiple is the lender's call.

Whichever path, the terms belong in the purchase and sale agreement, drafted by the attorneys. Talk to your attorney before you agree to carry a failed system, because the upgrade deadline follows the property.

Sellers should model the hit before listing. The seller net sheet in the [deal tools](/deal-tools/) shows what a repair or a holdback does to proceeds.

## What to do this week

1. Confirm in writing whether the property is on sewer or septic. The town's Board of Health or sewer department has the record.
2. If you are selling, order the inspection now, before the listing, and pull every pumping receipt you have.
3. If you are buying, ask for the as-built plan and the design flow, then compare it to your intended use.
4. Call the Board of Health and ask about local rules, testing seasons, and current review times.
5. Have an installer or engineer price any repair before price negotiations start.
6. Sellers: put the property facts, including the Title V status, into a [Seller Brief](/seller-brief/) so buyers see them on day one.
