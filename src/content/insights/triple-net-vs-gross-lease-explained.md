Two listings arrive in the same week for the same kind of space in the same town. One says $16 per square foot NNN. The other says $23 per square foot gross. Most tenants look at $16 and $23 and feel they have learned something. They have learned the names of two different accounting systems, and almost nothing about which space costs less.

This is the explanation we give every tenant we represent on the South Shore before they look at a single listing.

## Three structures, one question

Every commercial building has operating costs: property taxes, insurance, maintenance of the parking lot and roof, snow removal, landscaping, common area utilities, management. The lease structure is simply the answer to one question: are those costs inside the rent or on top of it?

**Gross lease.** Inside the rent. The tenant pays one number and the landlord pays the building's operating costs out of it. If taxes rise, the landlord absorbs it, at least until the next lease. Pure gross leases are most common in small office buildings and older multi-tenant properties. Many include a base-year clause, under which the landlord covers expenses up to the first year's level and the tenant pays increases above it, which is a gross lease that quietly behaves like a net one over time.

**Triple net lease.** On top of the rent. The tenant reimburses the landlord for taxes, insurance, and common area maintenance, the three Ns, typically as a per-square-foot estimate billed monthly and trued up against actual costs each year. The landlord's rent is nearly all net. Investors prefer NNN buildings for exactly that reason, and most South Shore retail and medical leases are NNN or close to it.

**Modified gross lease.** Somewhere in between, by negotiation. Commonly the landlord pays taxes, insurance, and exterior maintenance while the tenant pays utilities, interior upkeep, and sometimes a share of common area costs. The term has no fixed meaning. The lease's expense definitions are the only truth, and two modified gross leases in the same industrial park can differ by several dollars a foot in what the tenant actually pays.

## The arithmetic that makes them comparable

Back to the two listings. Both are 2,000 square feet.

The NNN space is $16 base rent plus, the landlord estimates, $7 in NNN charges: $4.20 for taxes, $0.80 for insurance, $2.00 for common area maintenance. The tenant pays its own utilities, estimated at $2.50 per square foot. Total: $25.50 per square foot, or $51,000 a year.

The gross space is $23 and includes taxes, insurance, and maintenance. The tenant pays utilities, also about $2.50. Total: $25.50 per square foot, or $51,000 a year.

They cost the same. The $16 space was not cheaper, and the $23 space was not more expensive. Change the NNN estimate to $8.50, which is what the building's actual history might show, and the triple net space becomes the expensive one by $3,000 a year. Change the gross lease to include utilities and it becomes the cheaper one by $5,000.

The rate on the listing told you nothing. The all-in annual number told you everything.

## The questions that reveal the real number

On a NNN or modified gross space, ask for three years of actual operating expense history for the building, not the landlord's estimate for next year. Estimates run low; history does not. Ask specifically what the snow budget has been, because on the South Shore it is the line that swings.

Ask what is excluded. A lease that passes through roof and structural repairs is a very different deal from one that reserves them to the landlord. A lease that passes through capital improvements in the year they occur, rather than amortizing them, can produce a reconciliation bill that exceeds a month's rent.

Ask for a cap on controllable expenses, usually 3 to 5 percent a year, and the right to audit the annual reconciliation. Landlords who keep clean books agree readily.

On a gross space, ask whether there is a base-year clause and what the base year's expenses were. If taxes rise 8 percent after a reassessment, a base-year gross lease passes that increase to you.

On either, ask who pays utilities, whether they are separately metered, and what the last tenant's bills looked like. Utilities are the fourth N nobody names.

## Which structure is better for a tenant

Neither, inherently. Gross leases buy predictability at a higher base rent and less visibility into what the landlord spends. Net leases buy a lower base rent and full transparency at the cost of carrying expense risk. A business with thin margins and no appetite for surprises may rationally prefer gross; a business that reads its reconciliations and negotiates caps may do better on net.

What matters is that you compare on the same footing, which means converting both to total annual cost, then to net effective rent once free rent, improvement allowances, and escalations are spread over the term. A five-year NNN lease with three free months and a $15 allowance can have a lower effective rent than a gross lease at a lower headline, or a higher one. The structure does not tell you. The arithmetic does.

## In practice

A Hanover tenant we represented was comparing a $14 modified gross flex bay against a $12 NNN bay a mile away. The modified gross lease had the tenant paying utilities only. The NNN bay's pass-throughs came back at $5.10 based on three years of actuals. The $12 space cost $17.10 before utilities; the $14 space cost $14. The cheaper rate was the more expensive space by $7,400 a year on 2,400 square feet, before a single escalation.
