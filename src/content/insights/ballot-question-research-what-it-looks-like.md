A Proposition 2 1/2 debt exclusion for a new school. A zoning article that would allow mixed-use buildings along a state highway. A charter change that replaces an open town meeting with a council. On the South Shore these questions are decided by a few hundred votes on a Tuesday in spring, and the people on either side usually walk in knowing little about the record.

Research on a ballot question is not the same thing as opposition research on a candidate. There is no opponent with a career. There is a question with a history, a set of people and organizations spending money on it, and a room full of people who have been saying things on the record for years. This is what a real file on one looks like, section by section, using only lawful public records.

## First, how did the question get here

Every local question arrived by a path, and the path tells you who wants it. In an open town meeting town like Hanover, Rockland, or Whitman, an article reaches the warrant by the select board's vote or by citizen petition with ten signatures, and a debt exclusion needs both a two-thirds town meeting vote and a ballot majority. In Plymouth, a representative town meeting votes. In Brockton, Weymouth, or Braintree, a council and a mayor put questions on the ballot.

The file begins with that path, documented: the warrant, the vote that placed it, the dates, and the names of the public bodies and officials who moved it, in their public capacity. For a zoning article, add the planning board's required public hearing and its report. For a school debt exclusion, add the feasibility study and the state school building authority filings that fix the number everyone is arguing about. [Ballot question research](/glossary/ballot-question-research/) is the discipline of reading all of that before anyone writes a lawn sign.

## The record: warrants, minutes, and prior votes

Three sets of documents do most of the work.

**The warrant and the committee recommendations.** The finance or advisory committee's written recommendation on each article, with its reasoning, is usually the most careful analysis anyone in town has done.

**Open Meeting Law minutes.** Under the state's [Open Meeting Law](/glossary/open-meeting-law/), every select board, school committee, planning board, and capital committee meeting must have minutes, and most are posted. Executive session minutes must be released once the reason for secrecy has passed; ask for them. Read two years of minutes for the question's keyword and build a dated chronology of who said what, when, and how the numbers moved. A project that was $54 million in 2024 and $78 million in 2026 has a paper trail explaining why, and it is in the minutes.

**Prior votes.** The town clerk certifies every election result by precinct. A 2019 override that failed by 212 votes, with precinct 3 turning out at 31 percent against 52 percent in precinct 1, is the single most useful page in the file for anyone planning a campaign. Request it in writing; most clerks will email a spreadsheet.

## The money: ballot question committees

Anyone raising or spending money to influence a ballot question must generally organize a ballot question committee and file disclosure reports. For state questions, the committee files with the Office of Campaign and Political Finance and the reports are searchable online. For municipal questions, committees in most towns file with the city or town clerk, on paper or by email, and you request the reports from the clerk. Check the current filing rules for the specific town, because they vary.

Two things about ballot question committees differ from candidate committees under [OCPF](/glossary/ocpf/) rules. There are no contribution limits, and corporations may give. So the architect, the construction manager, and the developer whose project a zoning change enables can all write checks, lawfully, and the report will show it. A "no" group that prints $900 of signs without registering may be out of compliance, and that is a fact too.

The money section is a table: donor, employer where listed, amount, date, side, then the expenditures. What it does not contain is a judgment about anyone's motives. A vendor donating to a project it will build is a disclosure fact, not a scandal, and the file says so.

## The room: public comment and who shows up

Hearings are recorded. Local cable access stations keep video of planning board and select board meetings, often for years, and written public comment submitted to a board is a public record. The file lists the organizations and officials who have spoken on the record, the positions they took, and the dates.

This is where the line matters most. The file tracks organizations, elected and appointed officials in their public roles, and anyone who has chosen to campaign publicly on the question. It does not track residents who spoke once at a hearing and went home. A neighbor's three minutes at a microphone does not make their mortgage, their divorce, or their employer part of anyone's research. At ICS it never does.

## A worked example: a $78 million school

A composite from a South Shore town of about 18,000. The question: a debt exclusion for a $78 million school building project, with state reimbursement of roughly 46 percent, leaving about $42 million to the town. The finance committee's recommendation estimated the peak annual tax impact at $412 on a median single-family home assessed at $585,000.

The file found, in the public record:

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Town meeting warrants, 2019 and 2026 | The 2019 version was a $54 million project that failed at the ballot by 212 votes |
| School committee minutes, 2024 to 2026 | Enrollment projections revised downward twice while the square footage stayed constant |
| Clerk's certified results, 2019 | Turnout 31 percent in the two precincts with the most seniors, 52 percent in the two with the most young families |
| Yes committee report, filed with clerk | $14,300 raised from 61 donors; $5,000 from principals of the project architect, $2,500 from a construction firm |
| No side | Roughly $900 in signs, no registered committee on file |
| Planning board minutes | A separate zoning article for a mixed-use overlay on the state highway corridor, scheduled for the same town meeting |

What the file concluded, with confidence levels stated: the enrollment question was the real vulnerability for the yes side (high confidence, three sets of minutes), the money pattern was lawful and disclosed and would not move votes on its own (moderate confidence), and the 2019 precinct turnout was the whole campaign for either side (high confidence, certified results). It did not conclude that anyone was corrupt, because nothing in the record said so.

## The second question on the same warrant

The zoning overlay deserves its own numbers. The article proposed a mixed-use overlay along roughly a mile of a state highway corridor, the kind of stretch covered in the [Route 18 corridor guide](/corridors/route-18/). The planning board's hearing minutes recorded eleven speakers over two nights, four parcels totaling 23 acres owned by three LLCs, and a town counsel memo on whether the article qualified for the simple-majority threshold under the 2021 state housing law or needed two-thirds.

That memo was the file's most important document. If two-thirds applied, the 2019 vote pattern said the article would fail by about 40 votes. If a simple majority applied, it would pass by more than 100. Talk to town counsel, or your own, about which applies to your article before you spend a dollar.

## What ICS does and does not do on a ballot question

The political desk's [Issue Landscape](/political/) is this file: the record, the money, and the room, with a chronology, a sourced table for each, and a bottom line up front with confidence levels. One client per question. Every claim footnoted to a document a reporter could pull tomorrow, because it will be checked.

Where the work stops: no pretexting or impersonation to obtain anything, no surveillance of anyone, no research into private individuals' personal lives, no medical, credit, or private financial data, and no disinformation at any price. The ballot question file is never used for employment, credit, or tenant decisions, which are governed by the FCRA and are not what this is.

## What to do this week

1. Pull the warrant article, the committee recommendation, and the vote that placed the question on the ballot.
2. Request two years of minutes from every board that discussed it, including released executive session minutes.
3. Ask the town clerk for certified results by precinct on the last two related votes.
4. Request the ballot question committee reports from the clerk, or pull them from OCPF for a state question.
5. Watch the recorded hearings and list the organizations and officials who have taken a public position.
6. If the question is yours to win, the [political desk](/political/) scopes an Issue Landscape in writing before any commitment.
