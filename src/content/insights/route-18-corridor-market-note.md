Route 18 is Bedford Street for most of its length, and it does not pretend to be anything grand. It runs south from the Route 3 interchange in South Weymouth, past the hospital cluster, through Abington and Whitman, into East Bridgewater, and ends this note at Campus Plaza in Bridgewater. The buildings on it are strip retail, service garages, dental offices in converted houses, contractor shops, and the plaza at each town center.

That plainness is why it matters. This is the road where a plumber, a dentist, or a landscaper buys the first building they ever own. Inventory with real frontage is thin, the owners are patient, and the best product changes hands between people who already knew each other. The listing portals show what everyone else passed on.

This note covers what is trading on the road, what is sitting, and where we think the next owner-user purchase actually gets made. The numbers are typical bands from our reads, not transactions.

## Five towns, one road, three different markets

The corridor reads as three segments, and a buyer should pick one before touring anything.

The north end is [Weymouth](/south-shore/weymouth/): Route 18 as Main Street through South Weymouth, heavy Route 3 commuter traffic, and a dense medical cluster anchored by the hospital. Medical and professional space moves fastest here, and the Union Point redevelopment of the former naval air station keeps every South Shore investor watching. Pricing is the highest on the road.

The middle is [Abington](/south-shore/abington/) and [Whitman](/south-shore/whitman/): dense small-business towns where the stock runs small and practical. Strip retail and service buildings on Bedford Street, converted houses holding professional offices near the centers, contractor yards off the side roads. Abington is a drive-by market where Route 18 visibility does real work. Whitman adds a walkable downtown of storefronts with apartments above, held for decades and traded quietly.

The south end is [East Bridgewater](/south-shore/east-bridgewater/) and [Bridgewater](/south-shore/bridgewater/). East Bridgewater is a hold-forever town, relationship-driven, where pricing rewards a buyer who arrives with financing framed. Bridgewater brings the university, Campus Plaza, and a genuine flex base off Route 24 that pulls every growing contractor within twenty minutes.

The [Route 18 corridor guide](/corridors/route-18/) covers who does business on each stretch. This note is about the buildings.

## What trades: the small service building with a curb cut

The product that moves on Route 18 is the 2,500 to 6,000 square foot freestanding service building on a half acre to an acre, with its own curb cut, a dozen parking spaces, and a use that does not need a special permit. Auto service, a trade shop with a small showroom, a dental or physical therapy practice, a daycare that already holds its license.

In our reads these buildings sit in a band of roughly $200 to $300 per square foot in Abington and Whitman, higher in South Weymouth near the hospital, lower in East Bridgewater. A 4,000 square foot building on Bedford Street in Abington, in decent shape, is typically a $900,000 to $1.2 million purchase. Lease rates for the same product run roughly $16 to $24 per foot triple net, with frontage and condition setting the spread.

What makes it trade fast is not the building. It is a left turn in, a curb cut the state already approved, and a use the town has already blessed. A buyer who needs a new or widened curb cut on a state highway is in a slower conversation. Each town also sets its own site plan thresholds and parking minimums, so the same use can be by right in one town and a special permit in the next.

These buildings almost never list. The owner decides to retire, a tenant or a neighbor hears first, and the deal is done before a sign goes up.

## What sits: the tired plaza priced like it is not

The product that sits is the 10,000 to 20,000 square foot plaza from the 1980s or 1990s with a few vacancies, the original pylon sign, a roof the seller would rather not discuss, and an asking price built on a cap rate the building has not earned.

A worked example from the middle of the corridor. A 14,000 square foot plaza with six units, two of them vacant, collects $165,000 in gross rent. After taxes, insurance, and the landlord's share of maintenance, net operating income is roughly $120,000. The seller asks $1.85 million, which is a 6.5 percent cap rate. Buyers in our reads underwrite this kind of plaza at 8 to 8.5 percent given the vacancy and the capital work, which puts the value at $1.4 to $1.5 million. The gap is $350,000 or more.

So the listing sits. It gets reduced twice over a year, the vacant units stay vacant because prospective tenants read the for-sale sign as uncertainty, and it eventually sells near where the first serious buyer offered.

What would have moved it: fill one vacancy at a market rent before listing, price the roof honestly in the package so the buyer is not discovering it, and show the signage and parking math up front. A seller on this road who does that work first typically gets closer to the number they wanted than one who starts high and waits.

## Where the next owner-user buys

Our read is that the next wave of owner-user purchases on Route 18 is trades and small medical practices in Abington, Whitman, and East Bridgewater, buying the building they already rent or the one two doors down. The math is why.

Take a trades operator renting 4,000 square feet on Bedford Street at $20 per foot triple net: $80,000 a year, plus roughly $3 per foot in taxes and insurance passed through. All in, about $92,000 a year, or $7,700 a month.

The same building is available at $1.1 million. Under a typical SBA 504 structure the buyer puts down 10 percent, a bank holds a first mortgage for about 50 percent, and a certified development company debenture covers the remaining 40 percent. Call the blended rate roughly 7 percent over 25 years. The $990,000 financed costs about $7,000 a month in principal and interest. Add the same $12,000 a year in taxes and insurance the tenant was already paying, and the owner is at roughly $8,000 a month.

A few hundred dollars more per month than renting, and the operator is paying down their own building, controls the yard, and can add the second bay without asking a landlord. Rates, terms, and eligibility vary by lender and by the business, so talk to your CPA and your lender before you set the budget.

The buyers who get these buildings had the loan framed before the owner decided to sell. Where the good product never lists, preparation is the edge.

## The hedges on this road

Every Route 18 deal we have seen go sideways went sideways on one of these:

- **Access.** Much of the road is a state highway. A new curb cut, a wider one, or a change in use that adds trips can trigger a MassDOT access permit. Ask before you fall for the facade.
- **Septic versus sewer.** Parts of the corridor, especially toward the south end, are on septic. A restaurant, a daycare, or a medical practice changes the design flow, and Title 5 rules plus local board of health requirements decide whether the system can handle it. Confirm which side of the line the parcel is on.
- **Environmental history.** Auto service, fuel, and old dry cleaning uses are common on Route 18. Chapter 21E exposure follows the land, and a Phase I is read before the offer.
- **Nonconforming uses.** Many converted houses holding offices predate current zoning. The use may be grandfathered for the current owner and not transferable to a different use without a special permit. Each town's bylaw reads differently.
- **Union Point.** Watch it, enjoy it, and do not underwrite a South Weymouth purchase on its timeline. Large redevelopments move on their own schedule.
- **Hospital traffic.** The medical cluster in Weymouth brings volume and congestion in equal measure. A practice benefits; a retailer that needs a quick left turn may not.

## What to do this week

1. Pick your segment: north for medical and volume, middle for drive-by trades and services, south for value and patience.
2. Pull the assessor records for the ten buildings you would buy and note how long each owner has held; the twenty-year holders are the list to write to.
3. Have a lender frame an SBA 504 pre-approval before you tour anything, so you can move when an owner says yes.
4. Ask the town planning office whether your use is by right, site plan review, or special permit on each parcel's zoning.
5. Confirm sewer or septic and whether the existing curb cut is state-permitted for your use.
6. Run your current rent against the purchase with the [lease versus buy tool](/lease-vs-buy/).
