Listen to how people give directions on the South Shore. Nobody says "the dentist in the northeastern part of Brockton." They say "on Belmont Street past the hospital," or "Route 28 by the Avon line," or "off 18 in Abington near the plaza." The road comes first, the town second, and the landmark third. Customers search the way they talk, and the assistants that now answer their questions are trained on the same language.

## Towns organize the results; corridors organize the customers

Search engines and AI assistants organize local results by town, because that is how business profiles, addresses, and service areas are structured. That is why every town you serve needs its own page, written for that town, naming its corridors, its neighborhoods, and the kind of work you do there. A single "areas we serve" list does not do it.

But customers organize themselves by road. A homeowner on Route 139 in Rockland will drive to a shop on Route 139 in Abington without thinking about the town line, because it is the same road and the same ten minutes. A contractor who works the Route 24 exits serves five towns from one yard. A business whose pages, profile, and reviews all mention the corridor matches the way its customers already think.

## The South Shore's grammar

Seven roads carry most of the sentences. [Route 28](/corridors/route-28/) is Main Street for Randolph, Avon, Brockton, and West Bridgewater: freestanding retail, auto, restaurants, converted-house offices. [Route 18](/corridors/route-18/) is Bedford Street, the practical trades-and-services road from South Weymouth through Abington and Whitman to Bridgewater. [Route 24](/corridors/route-24/) is the industrial expressway: flex, yards, logistics, the contractor economy. [Route 3](/corridors/route-3/) is the commuter spine and the exits where the regional retail and office markets were built. [Route 3A](/corridors/route-3a/) is the coast road: village centers, harbors, marine. [Route 139](/corridors/route-139/) ties the inland towns to the coast east to west. [Route 106](/corridors/route-106/) is the crossroads road the trades know.

In Broward the equivalents are [University Drive](/corridors/university-drive/), [441](/corridors/us-441/), [the Sawgrass](/corridors/sawgrass-expressway/), and [I-595](/corridors/i-595/); in Palm Beach County, [Military Trail](/corridors/military-trail/), [the Turnpike](/corridors/floridas-turnpike/), and [Southern Boulevard](/corridors/southern-boulevard/) out to Wellington. Different roads, same grammar.

## What this means for your pages

**Name the roads on the town pages.** "Serving Abington" is weak. "Serving Abington along Route 18, Route 123, and Route 139, from North Abington center to the Rockland line" is how a local reads it and how an assistant confirms you are real.

**Put the corridor in your profile and your reviews.** When the system asks a finished customer for a review, the request can mention where the job was: "the water heater on Route 28 in Avon." Reviews that name roads and towns are the ones that get your business recommended for the next person on that road.

**Think in territories, not radiuses.** A corridor crosses several towns, which means several territories. Under Market Lock, one business per category per town, a Rockland plumber and an Abington plumber can both hold their ground on the same Route 139. The question is which towns on your road you want to own, and whether they are still open. The [Territory Board](/territories/) answers that town by town.

## Where to start

Read the guide for your road, then the market page for each town on it, then decide which two or three towns to lock first. If you are hunting for a building rather than customers, the Massachusetts guides open a [buyer brief](/buyer-brief/) with the whole road's towns pre-selected. Either way, start with the sentence your customers already use.
