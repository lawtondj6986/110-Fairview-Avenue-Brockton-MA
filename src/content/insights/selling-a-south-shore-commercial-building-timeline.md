The most common way a South Shore commercial sale goes long is not a difficult buyer. It is a seller who starts marketing before gathering the paperwork a buyer will ask for in week six. The leases are in a drawer, the septic has never been inspected, nobody knows where the survey is, and the deal that should have closed in four months closes in seven or falls apart in month five.

This is the sequence that avoids that. It assumes a stabilized property between roughly $750,000 and $5 million, which covers most owner-user and small investor buildings from Quincy to Plymouth. Larger or distressed assets run on different clocks.

One note before the timeline. Several steps below touch tax and legal questions. Talk to your attorney and CPA before you engage a broker, not after you have an offer, because the answers shape the price and the structure.

## Days 1 to 21: preparation

The first three weeks are spent assembling what the buyer's lender and attorney will demand later. Doing it now shortens due diligence and removes the surprises that cost money at the negotiating table.

- **Leases.** Every lease, every amendment, every side letter, with a current rent roll that matches them. Note options, termination rights, and security deposits held.
- **Financials.** Three years of income and expense statements and a trailing twelve months, with the tax bill, insurance declarations, and utility history.
- **Title and survey.** Pull your deed and any recorded easements from the Registry. Locate the last survey. If none exists, order one; a buyer's lender will want it and you control the timing if you order it first.
- **Physical.** Roof age, HVAC age, paving condition, and any reports you have. Fix what is cheap and obvious.
- **Septic.** If the property is not on municipal sewer, order the [Title V](/glossary/title-v/) inspection now. A failed system discovered in week ten is a renegotiation.
- **Environmental.** Gather any prior Phase I reports and check for [Chapter 21E](/glossary/chapter-21e/) filings on the parcel. A seller who knows the history prices it; a seller who learns it from the buyer gets priced.
- **Zoning.** Confirm the current use is permitted or lawfully pre-existing. A zoning letter from the building department is cheap insurance.

## Days 7 to 21: valuation

Valuation runs alongside preparation because the financials feed it. Three approaches, weighed by property type.

Income: net operating income capitalized at a market rate for that asset class and location. Sales comparison: what similar buildings sold for per square foot in the last 18 months, adjusted. Replacement cost: what it would cost to build it today, less depreciation, which sets a ceiling more than a price.

A worked example. A 9,000-square-foot owner-occupied flex building in Abington. The owner is retiring and will sell vacant. Market rent for comparable space is about $12 per square foot triple-net, so a hypothetical NOI is roughly $108,000 less a 7 percent vacancy allowance and modest owner expenses, call it $96,000. At an 8 percent cap rate for a vacant flex building in that location, the income approach gives $1.2 million. Comparable sales of small flex buildings along Route 18 have run roughly $125 to $150 per square foot, which brackets $1.125 million to $1.35 million. Replacement cost for new flex construction on the South Shore is well above $200 per square foot before land, so cost is not the constraint. A list price around $1.3 million with a floor near $1.2 million is defensible. The [building worth tool](/building-worth/) on this site runs this same three-way check.

## Days 21 to 60: marketing and the letter of intent

With the package built and the price set, marketing begins. For most South Shore buildings this means a listing on the commercial platforms, direct outreach to owner-users in the trade that fits the building, a call to every broker active in that submarket, and a sign. The offering memorandum should be honest about the building, because every claim in it gets tested in due diligence.

Expect four to eight weeks to an accepted letter of intent. The LOI is non-binding on the sale itself but sets price, deposit, due diligence period, financing contingency, and closing date. Push for a short due diligence period, 30 to 45 days, because you have already done the seller's share of the work. Ask for proof of funds or a lender pre-qualification with the LOI. A buyer who cannot produce one in 48 hours is a buyer who will not close in 60 days.

## Days 60 to 75: the purchase and sale agreement

The purchase and sale agreement converts the LOI into a binding contract. Your attorney drafts or reviews it. Key terms: deposit amount and when it goes hard, the due diligence and financing contingency dates, representations about leases and environmental condition, what happens if a tenant defaults before closing, and the closing date with any extension rights.

Ten to fourteen days from LOI to signed P&S is normal. The deposit, typically 5 to 10 percent, goes into escrow with one of the attorneys. Deliver the full due diligence package the day the P&S is signed. You built it in week one.

## Days 75 to 110: due diligence and financing

The buyer inspects, orders a Phase I, reviews title, and asks tenants to sign estoppel certificates confirming their lease terms. Their lender orders an appraisal and reviews everything the buyer did. On the South Shore, bank financing for a small commercial purchase runs 45 to 60 days from application to commitment, and SBA loans can run longer. The financing contingency date in the P&S should reflect that.

Your job during this stretch is to answer quickly and keep tenants calm. Tell tenants the building is under agreement, tell them who will contact them for estoppels, and tell them nothing changes in their lease. A tenant who hears about the sale from an inspector is a tenant who starts calling other landlords.

Expect one renegotiation request. The appraisal comes in light, the Phase I flags a former fuel tank, or the roof inspector finds what you already knew. Having priced the building honestly, you can hold or concede from a position of knowledge.

## Days 110 to 120: closing at the Registry

The buyer's attorney orders the [municipal lien certificate](/glossary/municipal-lien-certificate/) from the town collector, which states outstanding taxes, water, sewer, and betterments. Final utility readings are scheduled. Rents and taxes are prorated to the closing date. Tenant security deposits transfer to the buyer with a written assignment. Your lender issues a payoff statement.

The deed is recorded at the county Registry of Deeds: Plymouth County in Plymouth with a Brockton satellite office, Norfolk County in Dedham, or Bristol County North in Taunton, depending on the town. The Massachusetts [deeds excise](/glossary/deeds-excise-tax/) of $4.56 per $1,000 is paid on recording and comes out of the seller's proceeds by custom.

A second worked example, a seller net sheet on a $1.5 million sale:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Sale price | $1,500,000 |
| Commission at 5 percent | ($75,000) |
| Deeds excise at $4.56 per $1,000 | ($6,840) |
| Seller attorney | ($5,000) |
| Title V inspection and septic pumping | ($1,200) |
| Survey and zoning letter | ($3,500) |
| Mortgage payoff | ($620,000) |
| Tax and rent prorations, net to buyer | ($4,800) |
| Security deposits transferred | ($18,000) |
| Estimated net proceeds | $765,660 |

Commission varies by deal and by agreement. Capital gains, depreciation recapture, and whether a 1031 exchange makes sense are CPA questions, and the 45-day identification clock on an exchange starts at closing, so decide before then.

## What to do this week

1. Gather every lease, amendment, and the trailing twelve months of income and expenses into one folder.
2. Pull your deed and recorded easements from the Registry and find or order a survey.
3. Order a Title V inspection if the property is on septic, and check the parcel for 21E filings.
4. Schedule a call with your attorney and CPA about structure, timing, and whether an exchange fits.
5. Request a zoning compliance letter from the building department.
6. Run a seller net sheet in the [deal tools](/deal-tools/) and compare it against the [building worth](/building-worth/) estimate before you set a price.
