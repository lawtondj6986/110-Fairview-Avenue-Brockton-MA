The call comes in at 6:40 on a Friday. An office manager in Braintree needs lunch for 60 people on December 11. The host is seating a party of eight. The phone rings six times and goes to voicemail. The office manager does not leave a message. She calls the next place on her list, and somebody there picks up.

Nobody at your restaurant knows that call happened. It will not show up in the POS, the reservation book, or the weekly numbers. It was worth more than every table in the dining room that hour.

This is the most expensive leak in a restaurant, and it is fixable with three changes: a separate line, a 30-second text, and a proposal that goes out within the hour. The rest of the operating detail lives in the [restaurant playbook](/industries/restaurants-food/playbook/).

## Why catering calls land at the worst time

People plan events when they have a free moment: late afternoon, after work, on a weekend. Those are the hours a restaurant is least able to answer. A kitchen that can answer the phone at 2:30 on a Tuesday gets few catering calls at 2:30 on a Tuesday.

The seasons make it worse. Corporate holiday parties book in October and November. Graduation orders arrive in March through May. Summer on the coast, from Hull down through Plymouth, is when staffing is thinnest and the phone is busiest.

Today's baseline at most restaurants is a reply the next business day. By then the order is placed elsewhere. The target is a first response in under five minutes, whatever the dining room is doing.

## The math on a normal month

A typical single-location restaurant that does catering sees about 22 inquiries a month across phone, web form, and social messages. In our installs, roughly half go unanswered or get answered too late to matter.

- 22 inquiries a month
- 11 missed or answered late
- 45 percent close when answered promptly: about 5 orders
- $1,850 average catering order
- Roughly $9,150 a month that never reaches the kitchen

Over a year that is about $110,000 in orders. Catering margins are better than dine-in margins, because the labor is planned, the menu is limited, and the food is prepped in batches.

Your inquiry count may be lower or higher. The honest answer is that most owners do not know, because the missed calls leave no trace. The first job is to count. The [Payback Calculator](/payback/) will take your own numbers once you have them.

## Split the line first

Before any automation, separate catering from everything else. One mistake we see constantly is catering inquiries landing in the same inbox as reservation spam and vendor pitches.

A split can be simple:

- A dedicated catering number on the website, the menu, and the Google Business Profile.
- A catering form with five fields: date, headcount, town, service style, and a mobile number.
- A phone menu with one option only: "For catering and private events, press 1."

Now every catering contact is tagged from the first second. You can count them, time the response, and treat them as a priority. A reservation question can wait three minutes. A 60-person order should not.

Make sure the profile, the website, and the printed menu all show the same catering number. A customer who finds two different numbers calls neither.

## The 30-second text and the one-hour proposal

When a catering call is missed during service, this goes out within 30 seconds:

"Thanks for calling [Restaurant]. We are mid-service but we want your event. Reply with the date, headcount, and town and you will have a proposal within the hour."

That message does three things. It tells the caller she reached a real business that wants the work. It asks for the three facts needed to price the job. And it makes a promise with a clock on it.

When she replies, the system asks the remaining intake questions: drop-off, buffet, or full service. Dietary needs. Rough budget per person. Where the event is, so delivery radius can be checked. Who gets the proposal and by when.

Then it fills your proposal template. Your packages, your per-person prices, your delivery fee, your deposit terms. Nothing is invented. The pricing rules are ones you set in advance, and the proposal goes out under your name.

## A worked example: the December office lunch

Back to the Braintree office manager. Here is the same Friday with the system in place.

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 6:40pm | Call missed during service. |
| 6:40pm | Text-back sent. |
| 6:43pm | She replies: December 11, 60 people, Braintree, drop-off. |
| 6:44pm | Two follow-up questions: dietary needs and budget. |
| 6:47pm | She answers: four vegetarian, around $30 a head. |
| 7:05pm | Proposal sent: hot buffet package at $29 a person, delivery $60. |
| 7:06pm | Manager's phone gets a summary to review after the rush. |

The proposal totals $1,800 before tax: 60 people at $29 is $1,740, plus $60 for delivery. She has it in 25 minutes, while the other two restaurants on her list have not called back. At 9:15 the next morning the manager calls to confirm details and take the deposit.

Nobody left the line. The human did what the human is good at, which is the confirming phone call and the relationship.

## What stays with a person

The system should know its limits, and you should set them in writing:

- Full-service events with staff on site go to the owner or manager for pricing.
- Parties above a headcount you choose, often 100 or 150, get a personal call before any number is sent.
- Bar service is never quoted automatically. Rules on serving alcohol off premises depend on your license and the town, so those inquiries are flagged for you.
- Allergies are recorded and passed to the kitchen. The system never makes a safety promise.
- Dates you have blocked return an honest "we are booked that day" with two alternatives.

The goal is narrow. Standard drop-off and buffet orders, which are most of the volume, get a fast and accurate proposal. Everything else gets a fast acknowledgment and a person.

## What to do this week

1. Check last month's missed calls on your phone bill or carrier portal, and note how many came between 4pm and 9pm.
2. Put a separate catering number or a "press 1 for catering" option on the main line.
3. Write your three most-ordered catering packages on one page, with per-person prices and a delivery fee by town.
4. Cut your catering form to five fields and make sure it sends an alert to a phone, not just an inbox.
5. Decide your limits: the headcount and service styles that always go to a person.
