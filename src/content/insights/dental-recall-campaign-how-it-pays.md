Every dental practice has a second practice hiding inside its software: the patients who came once or twice a year, liked the hygienist, and then stopped. Nobody fired them. Nobody lost them to a competitor. A reminder postcard went out, a voicemail was left, and the desk got busy.

Those patients are the cheapest production a practice will ever book. They already trust you. Their records are on file. Their insurance, in most cases, covers the visit. The only thing missing is a message that makes rebooking take ten seconds.

That is why recall sits in the first thirty days of every medical and dental install we do, ahead of advertising and ahead of the website. The full sequence is in the [medical and dental playbook](/industries/medical-dental/playbook/). This article covers the recall piece alone.

## Two different jobs with one name

Practices use the word recall for two things, and they should be set up separately.

The first is ongoing recall. Thirty days before a patient's hygiene due date, a text goes out with a link to pick a time. The target is 70 percent of due patients booked within 30 days. Most practices cannot tell you their current number, which is the first finding.

The second is the reactivation campaign. This is a one-time push to everyone who is already 60 or more days overdue. It is the part that pays immediately, because the backlog has been building for years.

Ongoing recall keeps the bucket from leaking. Reactivation refills it. Run the reactivation first, because it funds everything else, then turn on the ongoing sequence so the list never gets that long again.

## What the list looks like when you pull it

Export every active patient with a last hygiene date and no future appointment. In a single-dentist practice with 1,800 active patients, a list of 350 to 500 overdue names is typical. Before anything is sent, clean it:

- Remove patients who have asked not to be contacted, and anyone deceased or transferred.
- Remove anyone with a balance in collections. That conversation belongs to a person.
- Flag patients with no mobile number on file. They get a call from the desk or a letter.
- Sort by how long they have been gone: 2 to 6 months, 6 to 18 months, and longer.

The sort matters. Someone three months overdue just needs a nudge. Someone gone two years needs a warmer message and may need new patient forms and updated insurance. Send the recent group first. They book fastest and they give the schedule an early win.

## The message, and what it never says

The ongoing recall text in our installs reads like this:

"Hi [Name], you are due for a cleaning in [March]. Tap to pick a time: [link]. Most insurance covers two per year; yours resets in [January]."

Three things make it work. It names the patient. It puts the booking one tap away, writing to the practice management system or a synced calendar, with no "we will call you." And it gives a reason to act now.

What the message never contains: a diagnosis, a treatment note, a balance, or anything a stranger reading the lock screen should not see. Scheduling only. Consent is captured, every opt-out is honored the first time, and the vendor signs a business associate agreement where it touches protected information.

For the long-overdue group, change one line: "It has been a while and we would like to see you again. No lecture, just a cleaning and a check." Patients who have stayed away often stay away out of embarrassment. Say the thing that removes it.

## A worked example: 400 overdue patients

Take a practice in a town like Whitman or Hanover with 400 patients on the cleaned list. At a 15 percent booking rate, the first campaign produces 60 appointments.

Value each at roughly $200 for a cleaning, exam, and X-rays where due. Your fee schedule and payer mix will move that number, so use your own.

| Line | Number |
| --- | --- |
| Overdue patients messaged | 400 |
| Booked at 15 percent | 60 |
| Hygiene production at about $200 | $12,000 |
| Advertising spend | $0 |

That $12,000 leaves out two things. Some long-overdue patients will need more than a cleaning, and that treatment gets diagnosed in the chair, by the dentist, where it belongs. And each reactivated patient is back on a six-month cycle, which is worth more than the first visit.

Compare that with a new patient. The playbook values a new patient at about $1,400 in the first year, but you pay to acquire each one. Recall patients cost a text message.

## Send in waves the schedule can absorb

Sixty appointments is seven or eight full hygiene days. If the hygiene schedule is booked three weeks out, a single blast of 400 texts creates a problem: patients tap the link, see nothing until next month, and close it.

So send in waves. One hundred messages on a Tuesday morning. Watch what books. Send the next hundred a week later. If there is open chair time this week, say so in the message: "We have openings Thursday and Friday." Specific openings book better than a calendar.

Then protect what you booked. Reminders go out at 48 hours and at 2 hours, with reply C to confirm or R to reschedule. Practices on phone reminders typically see an 80 to 85 percent show rate. Two-touch text reminders move that to 93 percent or better, and a reactivated patient is the most likely no-show you have.

One follow-up to non-responders, ten days later, is enough. After two messages with no reply, the patient goes to the desk's call list or gets left alone. Three texts about a cleaning is one too many.

## How to tell whether it worked

Count four numbers at 30 days:

1. Messages delivered, out of messages sent. A low delivery rate means old phone numbers, which is its own cleanup job.
2. Appointments booked from the campaign link.
3. Appointments kept.
4. Production from those visits, hygiene and restorative reported separately.

If booked appointments come in under 8 percent, the cause is almost always the list, the booking link, or the schedule. Either the list was mostly patients gone three years, or the link opened a form asking for a callback, or there were no openings worth choosing.
