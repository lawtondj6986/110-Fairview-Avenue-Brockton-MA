A landlord with a vacant storefront gets an offer. The rent is right, the use fits the center, and the prospect is pleasant on the walkthrough. The broker sends over a one-page application and a business plan.

Then the landlord commits real money: a tenant improvement allowance, a leasing commission, months of free rent, and the opportunity cost of taking the space off the market. On a typical South Shore inline unit that is $100,000 or more out the door before the first full rent check. Almost none of it comes back if the tenant fails in month fourteen.

The public record on a business tenant is free and takes an afternoon. Here is what to read, and one line to be clear about first.

## The line: business tenants only

This article is about vetting a **business** that wants **commercial** space. It is not residential tenant screening, and nothing here should be used to decide whether to rent an apartment to a person.

Residential screening, and any report used to make a housing, employment, or credit decision about an individual, is a consumer report under the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act. That is a regulated activity with licensed providers, consent requirements, and adverse action notices. ICS is not one of those providers and does not do that work. See [the FCRA line](/glossary/fcra-line/).

The line still applies inside a commercial deal. The tenant entity is a business, and its public filings are fair to read. The individual who signs a personal guaranty is a person. Their public business record, such as the companies they manage and the suits those companies were in, is open. Their personal credit is not ours to touch.

## Who is signing, and can they

Start with the entity on the letter of intent. Look it up with the Secretary of the Commonwealth and confirm five things:

- The exact legal name, which is what goes on the lease.
- The entity type and the state where it was formed.
- The formation date.
- The status, and whether annual reports have been filed on time.
- Who is listed as manager, officer, or authorized signatory.

For an LLC, the filing lists the persons authorized to execute instruments affecting real property. A lease is one. If the person negotiating with you is not on that list, ask for a certificate or resolution confirming their authority, and have your attorney review it.

An out-of-state entity should be registered to do business in Massachusetts before it signs. And if the tenant is operating under a trade name, confirm who holds it. The name on the sign should trace back to the entity on the lease.

## Where they have been before

A business with prior locations has a history a landlord can read.

**Prior landlords.** Search the entity, and any affiliated entity run by the same principals, in the trial court's public case system. Commercial evictions in Massachusetts are generally filed in District or Superior Court. A summary process case from a prior landlord is the single most relevant finding there is. So is its absence across ten years and three locations.

**Suppliers and lenders.** Collection suits tell you how the business behaves when cash is short.

**Liens.** Run the [UCC filing](/glossary/ucc-filing/) search on the entity. A bank or SBA lender with a blanket lien is normal for an operating business. It also matters to you directly: that lender has first claim on the equipment and fixtures in your space, and may ask you to sign a landlord waiver. Three recent blanket filings from short-term funders is a different picture. Check for recorded tax liens as well.

**Licenses.** A restaurant, salon, daycare, dispensary, or medical office cannot open without licenses. Confirm the applicant or its principals hold them now, or have held them elsewhere without discipline. License status is a public lookup for most professions and trades.

## A worked example: a 2,400 square foot unit in Weymouth

A composite. An inline space in a neighborhood center, offered at $28 per square foot triple net on a five-year term. A fitness franchisee wants it.

The landlord's commitment:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base rent, annual | $67,200 |
| Base rent, five years | $336,000 |
| Tenant improvement allowance at $40 per square foot | $96,000 |
| Three months free rent | $16,800 |
| Leasing commission, approximate | $17,000 |
| Landlord's cash and concessions before stabilized rent | about $130,000 |

The public record on the tenant:

1. **The entity on the LOI** is an LLC formed five weeks ago. No filings beyond formation. No liens. No suits. A blank page.
2. **The manager** is listed on two other LLCs, found by searching the individual name. Both were formed in 2019, both are active, and both have filed every annual report on time.
3. **Those two LLCs** operate the same franchise in Braintree and Quincy. Each shows one UCC filing, to an SBA lender, and nothing else.
4. **Courts.** No cases against either operating entity or the manager in a business capacity in three counties.
5. **Registry.** A notice of lease recorded for the Braintree location in 2019, ten-year term. Still there.

The finding is not "approve" or "decline." It is that the tenant on paper has no substance and the operator behind it has a clean seven-year record. So the landlord's ask writes itself: a [corporate guaranty](/glossary/corporate-guaranty/) from the two operating entities, a security deposit of three months' base rent, which is $16,800, and the allowance paid in stages against lien waivers from the contractor. The operator agreed to two of the three. That is a negotiation grounded in the record instead of in a feeling about the walkthrough.

## The guaranty, and the individual behind it

Most small-business leases come down to the guaranty, because a new LLC has nothing to collect from. The options, roughly from strongest to weakest for a landlord: a full [personal guaranty](/glossary/personal-guaranty/), a guaranty from an established operating company, a capped or burn-down guaranty, and a [good guy guaranty](/glossary/good-guy-guaranty/) that covers rent only until the tenant hands back the keys.

How to evaluate an individual guarantor is where landlords get into trouble. The lawful route is direct and consensual. Ask the guarantor for a signed personal financial statement. If you want a credit report, it comes from a licensed consumer reporting agency, with the written authorization the law requires. Talk to counsel about the right forms and about what you may and may not do with the result.

What you may read without anyone's permission is the guarantor's public business record: the entities they have managed, whether those entities stayed in good standing, and whether those entities were sued by landlords. That is usually the more informative record anyway.

## What ICS does and does not do here

For a landlord, ICS produces a short brief on a prospective business tenant: the entity, its affiliates, its liens, its litigation, its licenses, and its prior locations, each finding cited to a public source.

The limits apply to every brief. No pretexting: nobody calls a prior landlord, a bank, or a franchisor under a false identity. No surveillance of private individuals. No medical, credit, or private financial data, which means no credit reports and no bank records. And nothing ICS produces is used for employment decisions, credit decisions about individuals, or residential tenant decisions. If a request is really about an apartment or a hire, we decline it and explain why.

## What to do this week

1. Get the tenant's exact legal entity name in writing before you spend time on terms.
2. Look up the entity and note the formation date, status, and listed signatories.
3. Search the manager's name as an individual and list every affiliated entity.
4. Run court and UCC searches on the tenant entity and each operating affiliate.
5. Decide what guaranty and deposit the record supports, and ask your attorney to draft it.
6. Model the rent, free rent, and allowance in the [Lease Analyzer](/lease-analyzer/) so you know your exposure by month.
