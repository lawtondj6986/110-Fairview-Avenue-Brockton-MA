The hardest building to find on the South Shore is a 5,000-square-foot flex building with a drive-in door, decent power, and a zoning district that allows a contractor to park trucks. Owner-users want them because the alternative is renting a bay in someone else's building at rents that have climbed every year. Investors want them because the tenants never leave. Almost nobody builds them because the land and construction cost more than the rent supports.

So the buildings that come to market are older, imperfect, and gone in weeks. The owner-user who wins is the one who knows exactly what the trade needs before the tour and can say yes or no in the parking lot.

This is the checklist we walk with contractors, installers, and small manufacturers before they look at anything. It covers the building, the zoning, the clusters, and the financing. Where tax or legal structure comes up, talk to your attorney and CPA; the entity that owns the building and the lease back to your company are decisions with consequences.

## Clear height, measured to the lowest thing

[Clear height](/glossary/clear-height/) is the distance from the floor to the lowest overhead obstruction, usually a sprinkler main, a truss, or ductwork. Listings quote height to the roof deck, which is often two feet higher than what you can use.

What the number means by trade:

- **12 to 14 feet:** a van and shelving. Fine for a service plumber, electrician, or cleaning company. A box truck will not fit, and a mezzanine is tight.
- **14 to 16 feet:** the South Shore standard for older flex built in the 1970s and 1980s. A box truck fits, a storage mezzanine is realistic, and a two-post vehicle lift works for cars but not vans.
- **18 to 20 feet:** rack storage three pallets high, a bucket truck, a van on a lift. Most newer small flex along Route 139 is in this range.
- **24 feet and up:** true warehouse. Rare under 10,000 square feet on the South Shore and priced accordingly.

Bring a laser measure. Point it at the sprinkler line, not the ceiling.

## Drive-in versus dock

A drive-in door is a grade-level door, typically 10 by 12 or 12 by 14 feet, that a truck pulls through onto the floor. A loading dock is a raised platform, about 48 inches, that a tractor-trailer backs against. A 5,000-square-foot owner-user almost always wants drive-in.

Contractors load their own trucks inside, out of the weather. Installers stage material on the floor. A dock is only useful if you receive tractor-trailer freight regularly, and a dock without a drive-in means you cannot pull a vehicle inside at all. The ideal small building has one drive-in door wide enough for your largest truck and, if you take freight, a second door with a dock or a leveler.

Check the apron: a short, sloped approach with a tight turn is a door your drivers will hit. An insulated door with a working opener costs $6,000 to $12,000 to replace, and older buildings often need it.

## Power

Power is the quiet deal-killer. A building with 200-amp single-phase service runs a shop with hand tools and a compressor. A fabrication shop, a commercial kitchen prep operation, a vehicle shop with lifts and a large compressor, or any CNC equipment wants three-phase, and the question is what size.

Look at the meter and the main panel and photograph the label. Typical small flex has 200 to 400 amps at 208 volts three-phase. Heavy power, meaning 600 amps or more or 480-volt service, is uncommon in buildings this size and adds real value if you need it. Bringing three-phase to a building that lacks it depends entirely on what is in the street. If the utility has three-phase at the pole, a service upgrade might run $15,000 to $40,000. If it does not, the upgrade may be impossible at any price you would pay. Call the utility before you make an offer, not after.

## Zoning and the yard

A flex building in an industrial district can do almost anything. The same building in a business or highway commercial district may allow a contractor's office but not outdoor storage of material, equipment, or more than a few commercial vehicles. Several South Shore towns treat a contractor's yard as a use requiring a special permit, with conditions on screening, hours, and vehicle count.

Before touring, pull the town's zoning map, find the parcel's district, and read the use table for your business. Ask the building department whether the current use is permitted or pre-existing nonconforming, and whether a change of occupant triggers a new certificate of occupancy or site plan review. In Rockland, Abington, Hanover, and Brockton the industrial districts are reasonably clear. In the Route 3 towns farther south, read carefully.

## The clusters

Three corridors hold most of the small flex stock:

**Route 18 through Weymouth, Abington, and Whitman.** Older industrial parks and standalone buildings, many from the 1970s and 1980s, with 14 to 16-foot clear heights. Good access to both the Route 3 and Route 24 customer bases.

**Route 139 through Rockland, Hanover, Pembroke, and Marshfield.** Newer stock, higher clear heights, higher prices, and the best access to the coastal customer. Hanover's industrial area near Route 53 and Rockland's park near the former air station are the deepest pockets. See the [Route 139 guide](/corridors/route-139/).

**Route 24 through Avon, Brockton, West Bridgewater, and Raynham.** The largest industrial base on the South Shore, the widest price range, and the best truck access. Brockton's industrial zones are extensive and permitting in a city with professional planning staff is faster than in a town-meeting town. The [Route 24 guide](/corridors/route-24/) has the detail.

Pricing has varied widely. In our experience, small flex buildings have traded from roughly $150 per square foot for older, lower-clear stock on Route 24 to $250 or more for newer buildings in Hanover or Pembroke, and rents from roughly $10 to $16 per square foot triple-net across the same spread. Treat those as ranges to be checked against current sales, not quotes.

## The SBA 504 math

An [SBA 504 loan](/glossary/sba-504-loan/) is the reason owner-users can compete with investors for these buildings. The structure: a bank first mortgage for about 50 percent of project cost, an SBA-backed debenture for up to 40 percent at a fixed rate for 20 or 25 years, and roughly 10 percent down from the borrower. The business must occupy at least 51 percent of the building, and the remaining space can be leased to someone else.

A worked example. A 5,000-square-foot flex building in Abington at $220 per square foot is $1.1 million. Add $40,000 in closing and soft costs, for a project of $1.14 million. The 504 structure puts about $114,000 down, a bank first of about $570,000, and a debenture of about $456,000. At representative rates, call it 7.25 percent on the bank piece over 25 years and 6 percent fixed on the debenture over 25 years, the combined monthly payment is roughly $7,060, or about $84,700 a year. Add taxes, insurance, and maintenance at roughly $3.50 per square foot, $17,500, for a total occupancy cost near $102,000.

The same building rents at about $13 per square foot triple-net, $65,000 a year, plus the same $17,500 in pass-throughs, for $82,500. Owning costs about $19,500 more per year in cash. Against that, roughly $25,000 of the first year's payments is principal, the business controls its location, and the building's value no longer belongs to a landlord. The [lease versus buy tool](/lease-vs-buy/) runs this with your own rent, rate, and holding period.

A second example that often changes the decision. The same company buys a 7,500-square-foot building for $1.5 million, occupies 5,000 feet, and leases 2,500 feet to a neighbor at $13 triple-net. The tenant's $32,500 a year covers about a third of the debt service, and the 504 still works because the owner occupies more than 51 percent.

Startups under two years old and special-purpose buildings need more equity, often 15 to 20 percent. Bring two years of tax returns and a current year-to-date statement to the first lender meeting.

## What to do this week

1. Write your building spec on one page: minimum clear height, door size and count, amperage and phase, yard needs, and office square footage.
2. Pull the zoning use table for your top three towns and confirm your trade is permitted in their industrial and business districts.
3. Call the utility about three-phase availability on any street you are considering.
4. Meet an SBA lender and get a letter stating what project size your business supports.
5. Walk the Route 18, Route 139, and Route 24 clusters on a weekday and note every building with a for-sale or for-lease sign.
6. Order a [Buyer Brief](/buyer-brief/) on the first building that passes the spec so you tour with the zoning, assessor data, and deed history already in hand.
