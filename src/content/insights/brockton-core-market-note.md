Brockton is the commercial center of the inland South Shore and the deepest sub-$3 million market on it: downtown mixed-use blocks, freestanding retail along Route 28, exit retail at Westgate, auto and light-industrial buildings through Montello and Campello, and trades buildings on nearly every secondary street. Much of it changes hands before it is marketed, because the national firms do not work these blocks.

It is also our home market. This note reads the core by district, puts typical numbers on each, and ends with a question more owners should ask: what the MBTA Communities Act does to the parking lot three blocks from the station.

Numbers are typical bands from our reads, not named transactions. Avon, Stoughton, and Randolph appear where they change the math.

## Four markets inside one city

[Brockton](/south-shore/brockton/) is really four commercial markets sharing a city line.

**Main Street** is Route 28 north to south: North Main through Montello, downtown around the station and the civic blocks, Main Street again through Campello. Mixed-use buildings with storefronts below and apartments above, retail pads, auto repair, restaurants, and converted houses holding offices.

**Westgate** is the Route 27 and Route 24 interchange on the west side: regional retail, pads, second-generation boxes, and the medical and professional uses that moved into former retail.

**Route 27**, Pleasant Street toward Stoughton, and Route 123, Belmont Street toward Easton, are the auto and service strips: dealers, repair, tires, body shops, and the retail between them.

**The light-industrial seams** run along the rail line through Montello and Campello toward the Route 24 exits: older manufacturing buildings cut into contractor space, distribution, and fabrication, at the lowest basis on the South Shore near a highway.

The [Route 28 corridor guide](/corridors/route-28/) and the [Route 24 guide](/corridors/route-24/) cover who does business on each. The three commuter rail stations, Montello, Brockton, and Campello, sit on the Main Street spine and matter for the Section 3A question below.

## Main Street: the mixed-use block

The downtown mixed-use building is Brockton's defining investor product, and often a first-time owner's.

A worked example. A three-story building of about 9,000 square feet downtown holds two 1,500 square foot storefronts and six two-bedroom apartments. In our reads the storefronts rent at roughly $14 to $20 per foot and the apartments at about $1,700 to $2,200 a month. Call it $17 per foot and $1,950 a month: $51,000 from the commercial and $140,400 from the residential, about $191,000 gross. An older downtown building with landlord-paid heat runs expenses near 40 percent of gross once reserves are honest, so net operating income lands near $115,000.

Downtown mixed-use trades in our reads at cap rates of roughly 7.5 to 8.5 percent, a band of about $1.35 million to $1.55 million with everything leased.

The apartments carry the building. The storefronts carry the risk: a vacant ground floor downtown can sit a year, and every month it sits the building prices lower. The owners who do well treat the storefront like a business they are in, not a space they are waiting to rent, and the city's downtown investment has made that a better bet than ten years ago.

Montello and Campello carry the same product at a slightly lower basis with more auto and service on the ground floor; a Phase I is read before any offer on a building with a repair history.

## Westgate, Route 27, and the exit retail

Westgate is where Brockton meets Route 24: regional retail, pads with drive-throughs, and large-format boxes, built for the car.

What trades is the pad with full access and the second-generation box a medical, fitness, or institutional user takes at a rent well below the original retail number. What sits is inline space in the older plazas behind the anchors, where the traffic is real but the visibility is not.

Route 27 toward [Stoughton](/south-shore/stoughton/) and Route 123 toward Easton are the auto strips. A 5,000 square foot repair building on three quarters of an acre with Route 27 frontage trades in our reads at roughly $1 million to $1.4 million, below the same building on Route 53 in Hanover, with the environmental file deciding which end. Stoughton's Route 138 retail gravity pulls shoppers off Brockton's west side, so the Route 27 operators who win are the ones a customer finds first.

[Randolph](/south-shore/randolph/), on the north edge where Route 24 begins, competes with Westgate for the same large-format tenants from its Route 139 pocket, at pricing below Braintree.

## The light-industrial seams and the Avon edge

The cheapest industrial basis near Route 24 on the South Shore is in the Montello and Campello seams: older manufacturing buildings along the rail line cut into contractor bays, distribution, and fabrication.

In our reads, a 10,000 square foot older light-industrial building in the seams sits in a band of roughly $90 to $130 per square foot, or $900,000 to $1.3 million. The same footage in the [Avon](/south-shore/avon/) industrial park next door, newer and with immediate Route 24 access, prices meaningfully higher, and the West Bridgewater interchange higher still. Rents follow: roughly $8 to $11 per foot triple net in the seams against the low teens in Avon.

The basis is lower for real reasons: older buildings, lower clear heights, uneven floors, thin power, and longer environmental files. A buyer is trading diligence for price. The ones who do well are contractors and fabricators who need the footage more than the finish and read the Phase I before the walk-through.

Avon is the premium alternative: the South Shore's purest industrial play, chronically scarce, traded to whoever was positioned when a building came up. Priced out of Avon, an operator lands in the seams; outgrowing the seams, it moves to Avon or the West Bridgewater crossroads. That ladder is the Brockton industrial market in one sentence.

## What Section 3A does to the parking lots

Section 3A of Chapter 40A, the MBTA Communities Act, required Brockton to zone a district where multifamily housing is allowed by right. The city already permitted dense residential downtown, so the headline effect was smaller than in towns that rezoned from scratch. The law and its hedges are in [the Section 3A article for commercial owners](/insights/mbta-communities-act-south-shore-commercial-owners/); this section is about Brockton's lots.

Around each of the three stations are surface parking lots, single-story buildings on large lots, and underused yards that earn little as income and could hold housing by right: parcels with two values now.

A worked example. A half-acre paved lot three blocks from a downtown station earns $18,000 a year from monthly parking. As an income property at a 9 percent cap rate it is worth about $200,000. If the adopted district allows 25 units per acre, the parcel supports roughly 12 units. South Shore builders in our reads have paid roughly $40,000 to $60,000 per buildable unit for clean sites with utilities, which puts the land at $480,000 to $720,000. The owner has done nothing. The zoning moved.

The hedges: confirm the parcel is inside the adopted district and read the density, height, and parking text; check sewer capacity; pull the environmental history, because a downtown lot was often something else first; and remember a district can be amended. A developer sale also has tax consequences that differ from an investor sale, so talk to your attorney and CPA before structuring anything.

## What sits in Brockton and why

Brockton product sits for three reasons, and none of them is the city.

**The storefront is empty and the price assumes it is not.** Buyers underwrite the vacancy. A seller who fills the ground floor first, even at a modest rent, sells at a better cap rate than one who asks the buyer to imagine it full.

**The package is two phone photos and a line of text.** Sub-$3 million listings here still go to market with almost no marketing, so the best buyers never see them.

**The environmental file was a surprise.** An auto, dry cleaning, or manufacturing history that turns up in the buyer's Phase I instead of the seller's package costs weeks and credibility.

The buildings that do not sit never list; they move owner to owner, to the buyer who was in the conversation early and already financed.

## What to do this week

1. Decide which of the four Brockton markets fits your use and budget; the price per foot differs by half between Westgate and the seams.
2. If you own downtown, request the adopted 3A district map and text from the city planning department and check your parcel ID against it.
3. If you are buying a mixed-use building, underwrite the storefronts at a vacancy you can live with, not the rent roll the seller shows.
4. Pull the environmental history on any auto, manufacturing, or rail-adjacent building before the offer.
5. If you are selling, fill the vacancy and build the package before you list.
6. Check what the building is worth on both bases, income and land, with the [building worth tool](/building-worth/).
