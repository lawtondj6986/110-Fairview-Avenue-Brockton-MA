The Fort Lauderdale International Boat Show closed about three weeks ago. If you run a yard, a mobile mechanic operation, a detailing crew, an electronics shop, or a canvas loft anywhere from Dania Beach to Lighthouse Point, there is a stack of business cards somewhere in your office right now, and a list of names in someone's phone.

That stack is where the show's value is. The week on the docks is expensive: booth or no booth, it costs staff time, missed shop hours, and a week of dinners. The return is decided afterward, by who follows up and how fast. Most shops follow up when the yard quiets down, which in November it does not, because splash and commissioning season has just started.

This article is about fixing that for the next two shows, Miami in February and Palm Beach in March, and for next October.

## Three surges in five months

The South Florida marine calendar hands a service business a schedule most industries would envy.

- **Late October.** The Fort Lauderdale show. The industry's biggest week, centered on the SE 17th Street marine district and the Intracoastal.
- **November.** Season opens. Seasonal boats come back from the north or out of storage and need to be splashed, commissioned, and detailed.
- **December and January.** Peak use. Every slip is full and every mechanic is booked.
- **February.** The Miami show. Repowers and electronics upgrades get decided.
- **March.** The Palm Beach show, then spring break charters and a detailing backlog.

Each surge produces a wave of inquiries in a short window, and each arrives when the shop is at its busiest. That is the structural problem. The leads come when nobody has time to answer them. The month-by-month version is in the [marine and boat services playbook](/industries/marine-boat-services/playbook/?market=fl).

## What happens to a show lead

Follow one conversation. A captain running a 58-foot sportfish stops at your display on Friday and talks for ten minutes about a generator that will not hold load and a bottom job due before the Bahamas in January. He takes a card. You take his.

By Sunday night he has had the same conversation with three other outfits. On Monday he flies to meet the owner. From here it usually goes one of two ways.

**The common way.** Your office manager types up the cards the following week. Someone calls on day nine and leaves a voicemail. The captain already has a haul-out date at another yard.

**The better way.** That Friday evening he gets a text: good talking today about the generator and the bottom job on the 58, here is the direct line for our service manager, what dates is the boat in Fort Lauderdale? On Tuesday he gets a written range for both jobs. He books the haul-out for the first week of December.

The difference is not salesmanship. It is that the details were captured on the dock, in a form on a phone, in the thirty seconds after he walked away.

## Worked numbers: sixty cards

Take a mid-size service yard in [Fort Lauderdale](/broward/fort-lauderdale/) that comes out of show week with 60 real conversations. Not brochure collectors: people with a boat and a job.

Assume an average job value of $9,000, which is modest for a mix of bottom work, mechanical, and electronics.

- Followed up in week two, by phone, when someone gets to it: perhaps 6 of 60 turn into work. That is $54,000.
- Followed up the same day by text, with a written range inside two business days and two more touches over three weeks: 12 to 15 of 60 is a reasonable expectation. That is $108,000 to $135,000.

Same show, same staff, same conversations. The second version costs about one minute per lead on the dock and a pricing sheet the service manager already carries in his head.

There is a second number that matters more over time. A captain who has a good first job with you manages that boat for years, and his next boat too. In marine work the first job is the cost of entry to the relationship.

## Show week: nobody is at the desk

During show week the owner, the service manager, and the best technician are on the docks. The office is thin. Meanwhile your existing customers still have boats, and boats still break.

An existing customer who calls on the Thursday of show week with a dead bilge pump and gets voicemail is at real risk. A response plan for that week has three parts:

1. **Every call gets answered or texted back within a minute.** By a person, or by an automated agent that takes the vessel, location, and problem and says when a human will call.
2. **Emergencies route to a named technician.** Taking on water, no power at the dock, no steering: those ring a phone, they do not sit in a queue.
3. **Everything else gets a time.** We are at the show through Sunday, your request is logged, the service manager will call Monday before noon. And then he does.

The early call matters too. A yacht owner's inquiry at 7 a.m. is worth more than any dockside banner, and the shops in [Dania Beach](/broward/dania-beach/) and [Pompano Beach](/broward/pompano-beach/) that serve the whole county from one address live on those calls.

## Owners, captains, and managers

A marine inquiry comes from one of three people, and each wants something different.

- **The owner** of a 28-foot center console behind a house in [Lighthouse Point](/broward/lighthouse-point/) wants a price, a date, and someone who shows up. He decides alone and decides fast.
- **The captain** of a larger yacht wants competence, a schedule he can plan a crew around, and paperwork he can send to the owner. He decides, but someone else pays.
- **The management company** wants a vendor file: insurance certificate, rates, payment terms, and one contact who answers email.

The intake form should ask which one is calling, in the first question. Then the reply can fit. The owner gets a range and two dates. The captain gets the service manager's direct line and a yard schedule. The manager gets the vendor packet the same day. Sending all three the same generic thanks for your inquiry wastes the best information you had.

North of the county line the pattern holds. The yards in Riviera Beach and the marine shops in Lake Park and Jupiter see the same three callers around the Palm Beach show.

## Quote the range the same week

Marine work is hard to quote sight unseen, and that is the usual reason for the delay. But the customer is not asking for a fixed price on day two. He is asking whether you are in the right neighborhood and when you can start.

A written range does that. Bottom job on a 45-foot boat, sand and two coats: a range per foot and the haul-out fee. Generator service: diagnostic fee and the hourly rate. Electronics refit: equipment list price plus an installation range by the hour. State what would move the number. Give the next available yard date.

A calculator on the website can produce these ranges without the service manager typing them, which is what [QuoteBrain](/quotebrain/) is built for. The rule is the same either way: a range inside two business days, a firm quote after inspection.
