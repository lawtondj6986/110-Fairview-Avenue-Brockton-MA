A commercial lease on the South Shore runs thirty to sixty pages. Most tenants read two of them: the page with the rent and the page with the signature line. The other pages are not boilerplate. They are where the actual cost of the space is decided, where your ability to grow or leave is decided, and where the landlord's lawyer has had decades to get comfortable.

This is a plain-English walk through the twelve clauses that matter most, in the order you will meet them, with what each one is doing and what to ask for. It is written for the owner of a trade, a practice, a shop, or a restaurant signing their first or second lease. It is not legal advice; it is the briefing you should have before you pay for legal advice, so the lawyer's hour goes to risk rather than vocabulary.

## 1. Premises and square footage

The lease will state a square footage. Ask how it was measured and whether it is rentable or usable. In a multi-tenant building the rentable figure includes your share of hallways, lobbies, and restrooms, and it can run 10 to 20 percent above the space inside your walls. Rent is quoted per rentable foot. If the number is wrong or inflated, every rent and expense calculation downstream is wrong with it.

Ask for: the measurement standard used, and a right to remeasure before the first rent payment.

## 2. Term, commencement, and rent commencement

Three dates matter: when the lease is signed, when the term begins, and when rent begins. They are often different. A lease that commences on delivery of the space but begins charging rent sixty days later gives you a build-out window. One that begins both on signing, while the landlord still has work to do, does not.

Ask for: rent commencement tied to the later of a fixed date or the landlord's completion of its work, with a per-day credit if the landlord is late.

## 3. Base rent and escalations

Base rent is the headline. The escalation clause is the plot. Three percent a year compounds to a 34 percent increase over ten years; two percent compounds to 22 percent. Some leases tie increases to the consumer price index, which is fine with a cap and dangerous without one.

Ask for: a fixed percentage rather than an uncapped index, the first increase deferred to year two or three, and a cap on any index-linked clause.

## 4. The expense structure: gross, modified gross, or NNN

This is the clause that decides what you actually pay. In a gross lease the landlord's operating costs are inside the rent. In a triple net lease you reimburse taxes, insurance, and common area maintenance on top of rent. Modified gross is anything in between, and the term has no fixed meaning, so the definitions in the lease are the only truth.

A listing at $18 per square foot NNN with $7 of pass-throughs is a $25 space. A listing at $23 gross is a $23 space. The lower number was the more expensive one.

Ask for: three years of actual operating expense history for the building, the exact definition of what is reimbursable, and a cap on controllable expense increases, typically 3 to 5 percent per year.

## 5. Common area maintenance and its reconciliation

If you are paying CAM, you are paying an estimate monthly and a true-up once a year. The reconciliation is where disputes live. Snow is the South Shore's wildcard; a hard winter can add dollars per square foot. Capital items, such as a new roof or a repaved lot, should be excluded or amortized over their useful life, not dumped into one year's CAM.

Ask for: the right to audit the reconciliation, exclusion of capital expenditures and landlord overhead, and a management fee cap.

## 6. Permitted use

The use clause defines what business you may operate. A narrow clause blocks you from adding a product line, changing your concept, or assigning the lease to a buyer with a slightly different business. A salon lease that permits only hair services cannot add a medspa partner. A bakery lease that permits only a bakery cannot be assigned to a coffee shop.

Ask for: the broadest use you can plausibly need over the term, phrased as a category plus related uses, and the right to any lawful use for an assignee with landlord consent not unreasonably withheld.

## 7. Exclusive use

The mirror image of the use clause. An exclusive prohibits the landlord from leasing other space in the property to a competitor as defined in your clause. It protects your investment in the location. Small landlords often grant it for a well-defined use, especially to an early or anchor tenant.

Ask for: an exclusive for your core category, and a remedy if it is violated, such as a rent reduction while the violation continues and a right to terminate.

## 8. Tenant improvements and the work letter

Who builds what, to what standard, by when, and who pays. A tenant improvement allowance is a landlord contribution toward your build-out, recovered through your rent. A work letter is the attached document that specifies the landlord's work versus yours. Vague work letters produce disputes on move-in day.

Ask for: a detailed work letter with a delivery date, the allowance paid on a clear schedule rather than only at completion, and a right to use unspent allowance against rent.

## 9. Assignment and sublease

Businesses grow, shrink, and get sold. Without assignment and sublease rights, your lease can trap you or kill a sale of your business. Most leases require landlord consent; the question is whether that consent can be unreasonably withheld, whether the landlord can recapture the space instead, and whether you are released after an assignment.

Ask for: consent not unreasonably withheld or delayed, no recapture right, no profit-sharing on a sublease, and a release from liability after a successful assignment to a qualified assignee.

## 10. Options to renew

An option to renew is the tenant's right to extend on terms set in advance. It is worth more than a dollar off the rent, because it protects the location your customers know. Renewal rent can be fixed, formula-based, or fair market; fixed or formula is certain, fair market is a negotiation.

Ask for: two options of equal length to the initial term, with rent set by a formula or capped fair market, and a notice window you can actually remember, with the deadline in three calendars.

## 11. Personal guaranty

The landlord will ask you to personally guarantee the lease, because your LLC has no credit history. You can usually limit it: a cap at a fixed number of months' rent, a burn-off after a period of on-time payments, or a good-guy guaranty that ends when you vacate in good standing with notice.

Ask for: a cap, a burn-off, or a good-guy structure. Signing an unlimited guaranty for a ten-year term is the single most expensive sentence most tenants ever sign.

## 12. Holdover, default, and remedies

The end of the lease and the bad days in the middle. Holdover rent at 150 to 200 percent of the last month is standard and punishing if your next space runs late. Default clauses define how quickly a missed payment becomes a lost lease; cure periods of five to ten days are common and negotiable.

Ask for: a holdover rate of 125 percent for the first sixty days, written notice and a cure period before default, and no acceleration of all remaining rent on a single missed payment.

## What this looks like in a real negotiation

A Whitman tenant signing a five-year lease on 2,200 square feet of retail space at $20 NNN was quoted $6 in pass-throughs. The expense history showed $7.40 the prior year, with snow at $1.10 of it. She negotiated a cap of 4 percent on controllable costs, an exclusive for her category, two five-year options at the lesser of 3 percent escalation or fair market, a guaranty capped at six months that burns off after year two, and a holdover rate of 125 percent for sixty days. None of it changed the headline rent. All of it changed what the space will cost her and what she can do with it.

That is the job of reading the lease. The headline is the easy part. The forty pages are the deal.
