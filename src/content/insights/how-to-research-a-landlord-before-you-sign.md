A five-year lease at $3,200 a month is a $192,000 commitment. Most tenants sign it after two walkthroughs and a handshake with a property manager who may not own the building. The landlord, meanwhile, pulled your credit, your financials, and your references before agreeing to anything.

You can do the same thing in the other direction, and you do not need anyone's permission. Everything that matters about a landlord sits in records any tenant can read: who holds title, what they owe on it, whether the town has a lien, whether the LLC is in good standing, and how often they end up in court. This article walks through those records in the order we pull them, what each one means, and where lawful research stops.

## Start at the Registry of Deeds, not the listing

The sign on the plaza names a management company. The deed names the owner. Massachusetts records land by county: Plymouth County (Brockton, Rockland, Hanover, Whitman, Abington, Plymouth, Marshfield, Bridgewater) in Plymouth, Norfolk County (Quincy, Weymouth, Braintree) in Dedham, Bristol County (Taunton) with offices in Taunton. All of them are searchable online for free by owner name, and the town assessor's database gets you from a street address to the owner name in one step.

Pull the current deed first. It tells you the grantee (owner of record), the grantor (who sold it), the date, and the consideration, which is usually the price. If the price is blank, the excise stamps give it away: Massachusetts charges $4.56 per $1,000 of consideration, so $5,016 in stamps means a $1.1 million sale. Then pull every document recorded against the property since that deed: mortgages, assignments, discharges, liens, easements, and notices. The [Registry of Deeds](/glossary/registry-of-deeds/) index is the spine of the whole exercise.

Two things to check on the deed itself. Does the owner on the deed match the name on the lease? If the lease is signed by a management company or a different LLC, you want to know who actually has authority to lease the space. And is the owner an individual, a trust, or an LLC?

## The mortgage, the assignments, and the warning notices

A mortgage recorded the same day as the deed tells you the leverage. A $1.1 million purchase with a $950,000 mortgage is 86 percent financed. That is a landlord with thin margins who cannot easily absorb vacancy, a new roof, or your tenant improvement allowance.

Watch for these documents recorded after the mortgage:

- **Assignment of mortgage.** Routine when loans are sold. An assignment to a special servicer or a debt buyer often means the loan is in trouble.
- **Lis pendens.** A notice that litigation affecting title is pending. A Massachusetts judge must endorse it before it is recorded, so it is never idle.
- **Order of notice, or a Servicemembers Civil Relief Act complaint in Land Court.** These are the opening steps of a Massachusetts foreclosure.
- **Instrument of taking.** The town has recorded a lien for unpaid property taxes. Check whether it has been redeemed.

Why a tenant cares: a lease signed after a mortgage was recorded is usually junior to that mortgage. If the lender forecloses, the lease can be terminated unless you hold a subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreement, an [SNDA](/glossary/snda/), from the lender. A landlord with a distressed loan and no interest in getting you an SNDA is a different risk than the one in the brochure. Talk to counsel about the lease language. The research tells you which questions to bring.

## Who is behind the LLC

Most South Shore commercial property is held in a single-purpose LLC. The Secretary of the Commonwealth's Corporations Division search, free and online, shows the entity's status (active, or involuntarily dissolved for failing to file annual reports), the date of organization, the registered agent, and the managers listed on each annual report. The [corporate registry](/glossary/corporate-registry/) is where a brochure name turns into people.

Then run those people back through the registry of deeds and the assessor databases of nearby towns. One manager appearing on eight LLCs that each own one building is a portfolio owner. The same manager on an LLC that was dissolved with judgments outstanding, and a new LLC organized the following month, is a pattern worth understanding before you hand over a deposit.

An LLC that has not filed its annual report in three years is not automatically a bad landlord. But it is a landlord who does not do paperwork, and a lease is paperwork.

## What the court dockets show

Massachusetts trial court dockets are searchable by party name through the public case-access system. Search the LLC, each manager, and the management company, in Plymouth, Norfolk, and Bristol counties at a minimum.

For a commercial lease, read the District Court and Superior Court civil dockets. Cases where the landlord is the defendant tell you about contractors who were not paid, tenants who sued over a deposit or a roof, and lenders. Cases where the landlord is the plaintiff tell you how fast they sue tenants. Six summary process filings in a year on a twelve-unit plaza is a landlord who litigates first.

Residential tenants should search the Housing Court; the Southeast division covers Brockton and most of Plymouth County. A 2024 state law allows some eviction records to be sealed, so a clean docket is not proof of a clean history.

Read a docket as a list of questions, not verdicts. A suit filed is not a suit won. Open the case, read the complaint, and see how it ended.

## Code violations, permits, and the other tenants

The building department's permit history, online in many towns or available through a short written public records request, shows whether the roof that "was redone in 2021" has a permit and a final inspection. Board of Health orders and inspectional services complaints show how the landlord responds when something breaks. Fire department inspection records show whether the sprinkler and alarm have been maintained.

Then walk the property on a weekday and talk to the tenants who are already there. This is lawful, it is the single best source on how a landlord behaves, and it costs nothing. Ask how long repairs take, whether CAM reconciliations arrive on time, and why the last tenant left. Count the dark storefronts and compare the number with the listing's "fully leased."

## A worked example: a Whitman storefront

A client was offered 1,600 square feet on [Route 18](/corridors/route-18/) in Whitman at $3,200 a month for five years. The records, pulled in one afternoon:

| Record | What it showed |
|---|---|
| Deed, 2022 | LLC bought the plaza for $1.1 million |
| Mortgage, same day | $950,000, assigned twice since, most recently to a servicer |
| Instrument of taking, 2025 | $18,400 in unpaid taxes to the town, not yet redeemed |
| Corporations Division | LLC involuntarily dissolved after two missed annual reports |
| District Court | Two suits by contractors for unpaid work, one defaulted |
| Building department | Roof permit open since 2023, no final inspection |

None of this made the space a bad space. It made the deal a different deal. The client signed a three-year term instead of five, with an SNDA from the lender as a condition, a $4,800 deposit instead of $9,600, the improvement allowance paid up front rather than as a rent credit, and a termination right if the property was foreclosed or sold. The landlord agreed to all of it, because a landlord in that position needs a tenant more than the tenant needs that space.

## What a tenant can lawfully learn, and where ICS stops

Everything above is public and open to anyone. What is not: the landlord's personal credit, bank balances, medical history, or anything you would need to lie to obtain. ICS does not pretext, does not impersonate, does not surveil private individuals, and does not touch medical, credit, or other private data. We research the property, the entity, and the people behind it in their business capacity, and nothing else.

We also do not do the reverse. Screening tenants for a landlord is a consumer report governed by the Fair Credit Reporting Act, and ICS does not produce reports for employment, credit, or tenant decisions. That is the [FCRA line](/glossary/fcra-line/), and it does not move.

## What to do this week

1. Pull the deed and every document recorded against the property at the county registry, and write down the owner of record.
2. Check the owning LLC's status and managers at the Corporations Division.
3. Search the LLC, its managers, and the management company in the trial court public case-access system for Plymouth, Norfolk, and Bristol counties.
4. Ask the building department, in writing, for the permit and complaint history on the address.
5. Walk the property and talk to two existing tenants.
6. Run the proposed rent, term, and deposit through the free [Lease Analyzer](/lease-analyzer/) before you negotiate.
