The call comes from the property manager or the landlord's broker. The building is under agreement. The buyer's attorney will be sending an estoppel certificate, and the buyer's lender needs an SNDA signed. Can you turn both around by Friday?

Most tenants sign both without reading them, because the lease says they have to and because the documents look like paperwork. They are not paperwork. The estoppel is a sworn snapshot of your lease that you will be held to. The SNDA decides whether you still have a business location if the new owner defaults on the mortgage. Both are also the one moment in a lease term when the tenant has something the landlord needs on a deadline.

Here is what the two documents do, and the five things to get done in the week you have.

## What the estoppel certificate does

An [estoppel certificate](/glossary/estoppel-certificate/) is a statement signed by the tenant confirming the facts of the lease: the parties, the premises, the commencement and expiration dates, current base rent and any additional rent, the security deposit held, any renewal or purchase options, any prepaid rent, whether any amendments exist, and whether either side is in default. The buyer and the buyer's lender rely on it in place of trusting the seller's rent roll.

The word estoppel is the point. Once you sign it, you are stopped from later asserting anything inconsistent with it. If the certificate says no landlord defaults exist and you sign it while the roof has leaked onto your inventory twice this year, you have a much weaker claim against the new owner for the roof.

Most leases require the tenant to deliver the estoppel within a set period, commonly 10 to 20 days, and some say that failure to respond means the tenant is deemed to have confirmed everything in the landlord's draft. Read your lease's estoppel clause first. The deadline is real, but so is your right to correct the draft.

## What the SNDA does

A subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreement, or [SNDA](/glossary/snda/), is a three-party contract among you, the new landlord, and the new landlord's lender. Each word is a promise.

Subordination: you agree your lease sits behind the mortgage in priority. Without this, a lease signed before the mortgage would survive foreclosure automatically, which lenders will not accept.

Non-disturbance: the lender agrees that if it forecloses, it will not evict you or terminate your lease as long as you are not in default. This is the tenant's protection, and it is the only reason to sign.

Attornment: you agree to recognize the lender, or whoever buys at foreclosure, as your new landlord and to keep paying rent to them.

The trap is an SNDA that is heavy on subordination and attornment and light on non-disturbance. Common lender carve-outs: the lender is not bound by rent you prepaid more than a month ahead, not liable for the old landlord's defaults, not obligated to complete the old landlord's unfinished build-out, not bound by lease amendments made without the lender's consent, and not responsible for returning a security deposit it never received. Each of those can be negotiated, and a lender that wants its loan to close will usually give on two or three.

## Why this matters in dollars

A worked example on the estoppel. A tenant in a Weymouth retail plaza has paid CAM charges for four years based on estimated budgets. The lease requires an annual reconciliation. The landlord never produced one. The tenant's own reading of the plaza's actual expenses suggests about $18,000 in overpayments. The estoppel draft arrives stating that all additional rent has been paid and that the landlord is not in default. If the tenant signs as drafted, the new owner inherits a clean slate and the $18,000 claim is practically gone. If the tenant adds one line, "Tenant has not received CAM reconciliations for 2022 through 2025 and reserves all rights to any resulting credits," the claim survives and the buyer, who now knows about it, usually makes the seller resolve it before closing.

A second example on the SNDA. A dental practice in a Hanover medical building invested $240,000 in a fit-out with six years left on the lease. The building sells with new financing. Two years later the new owner defaults and the lender forecloses. With a signed SNDA containing non-disturbance language, the practice stays, pays rent to the lender or its buyer, and the fit-out keeps earning. Without it, the lease is subordinate to the mortgage and a foreclosure can wipe it out, leaving the practice negotiating a new lease from zero with $240,000 of improvements as leverage for the other side.

## The five things to do that week

**1. Pull your complete file.** The signed lease, every amendment, the commencement letter, the security deposit receipt, rent ledgers, every email about repairs or disputes, and any notice you sent or received. Compare the estoppel draft against the lease, not against memory. Check the expiration date, the option deadlines, and the deposit amount to the dollar.

**2. Correct the estoppel before you sign it.** Cross out what is wrong. Add what is missing: unresolved repair requests, unreconciled CAM, unreturned deposits, tenant improvement allowances still owed, rights of first refusal or offer. Keep it factual and short. You are documenting, not fighting. Send it back within the lease deadline with a cover email noting the changes.

**3. Read the SNDA for the non-disturbance paragraph and the carve-outs.** If the lender refuses to honor your prepaid rent, your deposit, or an unfinished build-out, ask for the new owner to confirm those in writing separately at closing. Ask for the lender to be bound by amendments that were disclosed in your estoppel. Talk to your attorney before signing an SNDA on any lease with significant tenant improvements or more than three years left.

**4. Check your own rights in the lease.** Some leases carry a [right of first refusal](/glossary/right-of-first-refusal/) or right of first offer on a sale, which the landlord may have skipped. Look for relocation clauses, redevelopment termination rights, and demolition clauses the new owner could use. If the building is in a zone where redevelopment is likely, the buyer may be a developer, and the clock on your location just started. The [how to read a commercial lease](/insights/how-to-read-a-commercial-lease/) piece covers where these clauses hide.

**5. Decide whether you want to be the buyer.** A tenant who has been in the space for years, knows the building, and has a bank relationship can sometimes match the buyer's price, especially if the sale is to an investor rather than an owner-user. Even if you do not buy, knowing the sale price tells you the new owner's rent expectations at renewal. Run the numbers in the [lease versus buy tool](/lease-vs-buy/) before the week is out.

## After the closing

Keep paying the old landlord until you receive written direction, signed by both parties, naming the new owner and the payment address. Confirm your security deposit transferred and get the new owner's written acknowledgment of the amount. Update your insurance certificate to name the new landlord and lender as required. Meet the new owner or property manager in the first month and raise, in writing, anything that was open on the estoppel.

Then read your lease again with a new question in mind: what does this owner want from this building, and when does my term give them the chance to get it? Commercial tenants in Massachusetts have few protections outside the lease. The lease is the protection, and the week the building sells is the week to make sure it says what you need it to.

## What to do this week

1. Read the estoppel and SNDA clauses in your lease and note the response deadline.
2. Assemble the full lease file and compare every line of the estoppel draft against it.
3. Return a corrected estoppel that documents every open item in one sentence each.
4. Send the SNDA to your attorney with the non-disturbance carve-outs flagged.
5. Check the lease for first refusal, relocation, and redevelopment clauses.
6. Run your current rent against ownership in the [lease versus buy tool](/lease-vs-buy/) so you know your position before the new owner introduces themselves.
