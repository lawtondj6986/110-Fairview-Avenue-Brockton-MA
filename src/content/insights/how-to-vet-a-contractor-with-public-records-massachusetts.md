A $62,000 addition in Hanover, a $35,000 roof in Abington, a $90,000 build-out for a leased storefront in Brockton. The homeowner or business owner asks three friends, reads some reviews, and hands over a deposit. Then the contractor disappears for six weeks, a lumber supplier's lien shows up at the registry of deeds, and the building inspector says no permit was ever pulled.

Almost every one of those stories was predictable from records that were public before the deposit cleared. Massachusetts keeps more of them than most states. Here is how to read them in about an hour, and what each one actually tells you.

## Two licenses, two databases

Massachusetts regulates residential contracting through two separate systems that people confuse constantly.

**The Construction Supervisor License (CSL)** is a competency license issued by the state's Board of Building Regulations and Standards, now administered under the Division of Occupational Licensure. A CSL holder is the person allowed to pull a building permit for structural work on one- and two-family homes and smaller buildings. There are unrestricted licenses, restricted licenses for one- and two-family work, and specialty licenses for things like roofing, windows and siding, and demolition. Look it up in the Division of Occupational Licensure's license lookup. Check the status, the expiration, the license type against the job you are hiring for, and whether any discipline is listed.

**The Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration** is a consumer-protection registration with the Office of Consumer Affairs and Business Regulation. It is not a skills test. It is required for contractors doing work on existing one- to four-unit residential property, and it is what makes the Guaranty Fund available to you. The registration number must appear on your contract. Look it up in the OCABR registration search.

Electricians, plumbers, and gas fitters hold separate trade licenses, also searchable through the license lookup.

For a commercial build-out in leased space, the HIC law generally does not apply, but the CSL and trade licenses do, and most leases require the landlord's approval of your contractor and proof of insurance. Read your lease before you sign a construction contract.

## The database most homeowners never check

Here is the one most people skip. The OCABR registration record is not just a yes-or-no on whether a contractor is registered. The office keeps the complaint history, arbitration outcomes under the HIC program, and payments made from the Residential Contractor's Guaranty Fund on that contractor's account.

The Guaranty Fund pays a homeowner up to $10,000 when they hold an unpaid court judgment or arbitration award against a registered contractor. A contractor whose account shows a fund payout is a contractor who lost to a homeowner and then did not pay. That is a state agency writing a check because the contractor would not.

What is published online varies and changes, so check the registration record first, and if the detail is not there, ask OCABR in writing under the public records law for the complaint and fund history on that registration number. The same request to the Attorney General's consumer protection division will tell you whether consumer complaints have been filed. A short email, ten business days, usually no fee.

## Court records: read the docket, not the count

Search the contractor's business name, the owner's name, and any prior business names in the Massachusetts trial court public case-access system. Pull Plymouth, Norfolk, and Bristol counties, and whichever county the contractor lives in.

What you are looking for:

- **Small claims and District Court civil cases** where the contractor is the defendant. Homeowners, suppliers, and subcontractors suing for money.
- **Cases where the contractor is the plaintiff.** A contractor who sues customers for the final payment on every third job is telling you something about how jobs end.
- **Default judgments.** The contractor did not show up to defend. That is a stronger signal than a case that was contested and settled.
- **The pattern across names.** A dissolved LLC with four judgments and a new LLC with the same owner, same truck, and no history.

A contractor who has been in business twenty years and has two small claims cases is a normal contractor. Open each case and read how it ended before you decide anything.

## Mechanic's liens at the Registry of Deeds

Massachusetts mechanic's liens are recorded at the county [Registry of Deeds](/glossary/registry-of-deeds/), first as a Notice of Contract and then as a Statement of Account. Search the contractor's name as a party, both as the one filing and the one named.

Liens filed **by** the contractor against customers' homes show how often jobs end in a payment fight. Liens filed by **suppliers and subcontractors** on jobs the contractor ran are the more important signal: the lumber yard or the drywall sub was not paid, and they protected themselves by liening the homeowner's property. If that happened to the last three customers, it will happen to you, and under Massachusetts law a sub who was not paid can lien your property even if you paid the general in full. A basic [lien search](/glossary/lien-search/) takes twenty minutes.

While you are at the registry, check the contractor's own property for tax takings and multiple mortgages. A contractor under financial pressure is a contractor tempted to use your deposit to finish the last job.

## Corporate standing and the contract itself

Search the business in the Secretary of the Commonwealth's [corporate registry](/glossary/corporate-registry/). You want an active entity whose exact legal name matches the name on the contract, the HIC registration, and the insurance certificate. A mismatch is not always fraud, but it is always a question.

Then make the contract do the rest. Under the Massachusetts home improvement law, a contract over $1,000 must be in writing, the deposit may not exceed one-third of the price unless special-order materials require more, and the contract must carry the registration number, start and completion dates, and a three-day cancellation notice. Add progress payments tied to inspections, lien waivers from every sub and supplier with each payment, and a certificate of insurance issued to you directly by the insurer, including workers' compensation. The state's Department of Industrial Accidents publishes stop-work orders and a coverage verification tool. Talk to counsel if the job is large enough that a lien would hurt.

## A worked example: the $62,000 addition

A Hanover homeowner brought us a bid for a $62,000 addition. The contractor wanted a $31,000 deposit, half the job, and had excellent reviews. One hour of records:

| Record | Result |
|---|---|
| CSL lookup | Active, unrestricted, no discipline |
| HIC registration | Active, one Guaranty Fund payout of $8,200 in 2024 |
| District Court | Two small claims cases, one default judgment for $4,100 |
| Registry of Deeds | Three Notices of Contract by the same lumber supplier on his jobs in 18 months |
| Corporations Division | LLC active, organized 2023; prior LLC under same owner dissolved 2022 |

The homeowner did not walk. She restructured. Deposit capped at one-third ($20,667), four progress payments tied to the framing, rough, insulation, and final inspections, a lien waiver from the lumber supplier with each check, and a workers' compensation certificate from the carrier before the first day. The addition finished eleven days late and lien-free.

The second owner that month, a Brockton shop tenant with a $90,000 build-out, found that the contractor's entity had been involuntarily dissolved and the HIC registration had lapsed. He hired someone else. The free [due-diligence checklist](/intelligence/due-diligence-checklist/) covers both cases in one page.

## Where the research stops

This is a vendor decision: you are hiring a business to do a job. Public-record research on a business and its principals in their business capacity is lawful and appropriate for that. It is not a background check on a person, and if you are hiring someone as an employee, the rules change completely. Employment screening is a consumer report governed by the Fair Credit Reporting Act, and ICS does not do it, just as we do not do credit or tenant screening.

ICS also does not pretext, does not impersonate a customer to extract information, does not surveil private individuals, and does not obtain medical, credit, or private financial data. The records above are enough. If you are the contractor reading this, the [trades playbook](/industries/trades-home-services/playbook/) covers how a clean record becomes a selling point.

## What to do this week

1. Look up the CSL and the HIC registration, and confirm both match the name on the bid.
2. Ask OCABR in writing for the complaint and Guaranty Fund history on the registration number.
3. Search the business, the owner, and any prior business names in the trial court public case-access system.
4. Search the contractor's name at the Registry of Deeds for Notices of Contract, filed by and against.
5. Rewrite the payment schedule: one-third deposit, inspections, lien waivers, insurance certificate from the carrier.
6. Print the [due-diligence checklist](/intelligence/due-diligence-checklist/) and keep it with the contract.
