Here's the uncomfortable pattern in most bad deals: the warning was public the whole time. A judgment in a county docket. A lien recorded eleven days before the handshake. An LLC that dissolved with debts still attached. Nobody hid it — nobody *looked*. So the question isn't whether to vet a counterparty. It's how far, and what it costs to go that far.

## What to check (all of it is public)

Vetting the other side of a deal comes down to six categories, every one of them in the lawful public record:

1. **The entity.** Is it real, active, and in good standing? Who are the officers and the registered agent — and who *actually* owns it, through however many layers?
2. **The people.** The principals in their business capacity: litigation history, judgments, prior ventures and how they ended, regulatory or licensing actions.
3. **The money signals.** Tax liens, UCC secured filings, bankruptcies, unsatisfied judgments — the public footprints of solvency and obligation.
4. **The asset.** Title and transfer history, encumbrances, permits, code and environmental flags.
5. **Court history.** Civil and criminal dockets, in every jurisdiction where they've operated — not just the convenient one.
6. **Reputation across time.** The open web, adverse media, and public web archives — including what was quietly deleted.

We publish a free, printable [due-diligence checklist](/intelligence/due-diligence-checklist/) with each item, where it lives, and what a red flag looks like. Start there; it's genuinely enough for small, reversible deals.

## What it costs, honestly

Due diligence scales with depth, and the market roughly tiers like this:

- **Do it yourself — nearly free.** Secretary of State registries, court dockets, registries of deeds, and web archives are public and mostly free beyond filing fees. The checklist above is the map. The limit isn't access; it's knowing where to look and what the record means.
- **A basic check — a few hundred to about $1,500.** Corporate status, litigation search, and a liens-and-judgments pull from an investigative firm. Useful, and usually shallow on ownership structure.
- **Comprehensive counterparty diligence — commonly $2,500–$10,000.** Multi-jurisdiction records, beneficial-ownership mapping, financial-exposure signals, adverse media, and a written, sourced report.
- **Complex investigations — $10,000–$25,000 and up.** Multiple entities and jurisdictions, opaque ownership, sanctions and watchlist screening, web-archive forensics, and findings that have to hold up in front of counsel.

What drives the number: how many **jurisdictions** the subject crosses, how **opaque the ownership** is, how **deep the record** goes, and how **defensible** the output needs to be — a note to yourself, or a document a lawyer or board will rely on.

## The question under the question

"Is it worth it?" has an honest formula: **the amount at stake, times how much you still don't know, times how bad it would be if the unknown turns out to matter.** Run your own numbers in the free [Cost of Not Knowing](/intelligence/cost-of-not-knowing/) calculator. For a six-figure transaction, a partnership, a board seat, or financing, a few thousand dollars of sourced research is cheap insurance against the one fact that changes everything. For the small and reversible, Google and the checklist will do.

## Where the work stops — and why it matters to you

This is the part most buyers skip, and it's the part that determines whether the research is *usable*. Legitimate diligence reads the lawful public record and nothing else. No pretexting or impersonation for private records. No access to private bank or financial accounts. No surveillance, device access, or location tracking. And nothing for hiring, tenant-screening, or credit decisions governed by the FCRA — those are regulated instruments that belong with licensed providers.

A file gathered cleanly can be used anywhere: in a negotiation, in front of counsel, in a boardroom. A file gathered improperly is worthless at best and a liability at worst. Ask any investigator where their work stops. If they can't answer in one breath, keep looking.
