It is 9:40 on a Tuesday night. A dog in Abington has eaten something off the kitchen floor and his owner is scared. She calls her vet. The recording says the clinic is closed and gives the hours. It mentions an emergency number somewhere in the middle. She did not have a pen. She hangs up and searches for it.

Twenty minutes later a different owner calls the same line. Her cat is due for vaccines and she just remembered. She hears the same recording, hangs up, and forgets again for a month.

Those two calls need completely different handling, and most after-hours setups give both the same recording. This article covers how to separate them. The broader system is in the [veterinary and pet services playbook](/industries/veterinary-pet-services/playbook/).

## Two jobs, in a fixed order

The after-hours line has two jobs.

The first is emergency routing. Any owner who says it is an emergency gets the name and phone number of an open emergency hospital, right away, before any other question. The clinic is alerted that it happened.

The second is capture. Everyone else gets a way to say what they need in a sentence, and that request is waiting for the desk in the morning, ready to book.

The order is not negotiable. Emergency first, every time, on every channel. The first thing a caller hears and the first line of every text is the emergency instruction. A worried owner should never have to listen through hours and holiday closures to reach it.

## Route, never triage

This is the line that matters most, so it is worth stating plainly.

The system does not decide whether something is an emergency. It does not ask about symptoms to sort urgent from routine. It does not say "that can probably wait until morning." It does not offer home care, dosing, or any opinion about an animal's condition. Not once, not even when asked directly.

What it does:

- If the owner says emergency, it gives the emergency hospital's name and number.
- If the owner is unsure, it treats that as a yes and gives the same information.
- If the owner asks a medical question, it answers: "I cannot give medical advice. If you are worried right now, call [Emergency Hospital] at [number]."
- It alerts the clinic's designated person that an emergency contact came in, with the client's name if known.

A veterinarian triages. A phone system routes. The reason is simple: the cost of a wrong reassurance is an animal's life, and no convenience is worth that. Every clinic we work with reviews and signs off on this wording before launch.

## The text that goes out

When an after-hours call is not answered live, this goes out within 30 seconds:

"Thanks for calling [Clinic]. If this is an emergency, call [Emergency Hospital] at [number] now. Otherwise, tell us your pet's name and what is going on and we will get you the first available time."

Look at the structure. The emergency sentence is first and complete on its own. An owner who reads only the first line has what she needs, with a number she can tap.

For the non-emergency reply, the intake is short:

- What kind of pet, and the name.
- Wellness visit, a specific concern, grooming, boarding, or daycare.
- Existing client or new.
- For boarding: dates, and whether vaccines are current.
- Best mobile number.

If anything in the reply sounds urgent to the owner herself, such as "he is really not doing well," the system repeats the emergency number. It does not weigh the words. It just makes sure the number is in front of her again.

## A worked example: one holiday night

After-hours calls peak over the Fourth of July, between fireworks and heat. Here is a realistic log for a two-doctor clinic on that night, from 6pm to 7am.

| Calls | What they were | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Owner said emergency | Hospital name and number sent at once, clinic alerted |
| 4 | Appointment requests | Details captured, first available times held |
| 2 | Boarding for August | Dates captured, vaccine status noted |
| 2 | Prescription refill requests | Logged for technician review |
| 1 | No reply to the text | Nothing further sent |

Eleven calls. Under the old recording, the clinic would have known about none of them.

The next morning the desk confirms four appointments and two boarding stays. At an average visit or stay of about $310, that is $1,860 from one night. The two emergency clients get a follow-up call from the clinic asking how the pet is doing. That call costs three minutes and is remembered for years.

## The monthly math

One night is an illustration. The monthly numbers are what justify the work.

A typical clinic sees about 180 inquiries a month across calls, texts, and web requests. Roughly 35 percent are missed or answered too late. Of those who get a prompt answer, about 60 percent book.

- 180 inquiries
- 63 missed
- 60 percent would have booked: about 38 visits
- $310 average visit or stay
- Roughly $11,700 a month

Not all of that is after-hours. A large share is the lunch hour and the 8am drop-off rush, when two people are at the counter and the phone rings out. The same routing and capture logic runs then.

Put your own call volume into the [Payback Calculator](/payback/). If you do not know your missed-call count, your phone carrier's portal usually does.

## What the morning looks like

Capture is only useful if the desk can act on it in ten minutes. At 7:30am the lead receptionist gets one summary:

- Emergencies routed overnight, with client names, for a follow-up call.
- Appointment requests, sorted by type, with tentatively held times to confirm.
- Boarding and grooming requests with dates.
- Refill requests for the technician.
- Anything unclear, flagged for a call.

Online bookings for wellness, grooming, and daycare land on a synced calendar that the desk confirms. Nothing clinical is booked without a person looking at it.

The first week, the desk will find the summary longer than expected. That length is the calls you were missing. By week three it is part of the opening routine, like counting the drawer.

## Details that keep it safe

A few maintenance habits matter more than any feature:

- Confirm the emergency hospital's number and overnight hours every quarter. Hospitals change hours and some stop taking walk-ins. A wrong number in this message is the worst failure the system can have.
- Name a backup hospital if your primary one is ever at capacity.
- Place a test call after hours once a month and read what comes back.
- Keep the spoken greeting under twenty seconds, emergency instruction first.
- Review every message for anything that could be read as medical guidance, and remove it.
