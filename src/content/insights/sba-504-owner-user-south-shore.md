Most owners who rent their space assume buying the building takes 25 percent down. On a $1.8 million purchase that is $450,000 in cash, which is the number that ends the conversation for a healthy machine shop, dental practice, or contractor with money tied up in trucks and receivables.

The SBA 504 program exists for that exact owner. It splits the financing into two loans and cuts the down payment to roughly 10 percent. The trade is real: more total debt, two lenders, more paperwork, and a prepayment penalty on one of the loans.

This note explains the general structure. ICS's founder is a licensed Massachusetts real estate salesperson, not a lender, so treat every rate, fee, and limit here as something that changes and that a 504 lender confirms before you rely on it.

## The three pieces: 50, 40, and 10

A [504 loan](/glossary/sba-504-loan/) is really two loans and a check.

- **About 50 percent: a bank.** A conventional lender makes a first-mortgage loan for half the project. The bank sets the rate and the term. Because it holds the first lien at half the value, its risk is low, and that is why banks like these deals.
- **Up to 40 percent: a Certified Development Company.** A CDC is a nonprofit licensed by the SBA. Its loan sits in second position and is funded by a debenture the SBA guarantees. This piece carries a fixed rate, commonly for 20 or 25 years on real estate.
- **About 10 percent: you.** The borrower's injection. It rises to roughly 15 percent for a business under two years old or for a special-purpose building such as a car wash or a bowling alley, and to roughly 20 percent when both apply.

The "project" is broader than the purchase price. It generally can include renovations, eligible equipment, and soft costs such as the appraisal, environmental report, and legal fees. That matters, because cash you would otherwise spend on the build-out gets financed at the same 90 percent.

## A worked example on a $1.8 million project

Take a Brockton HVAC contractor paying $11.00 per square foot triple net on 12,000 square feet: $132,000 a year, or $11,000 a month, plus taxes, insurance, and maintenance. A 12,000 square foot building near Route 24 comes up at $1,600,000 and needs $200,000 of work. Total project: $1,800,000.

| | SBA 504 | Conventional at 75 percent |
|---|---|---|
| Bank first mortgage | $900,000 | $1,350,000 |
| CDC second | $720,000 | none |
| Cash down | $180,000 | $450,000 |
| Total debt | $1,620,000 | $1,350,000 |

The 504 structure keeps $270,000 in the business.

Now the payment. Rates move, so these are illustrations only. If the bank piece were at 7.00 percent on a 25-year amortization, it would run about $6,360 a month. If the CDC piece were at 6.25 percent over 25 years, about $4,750. Together: roughly $11,110 a month. The conventional loan at 7.00 percent would be about $9,540.

So the 504 buyer pays roughly $1,570 more per month and keeps $270,000. And the combined payment lands almost exactly on the rent the contractor already pays, except that part of every payment is now principal. Run your own numbers in the [lease vs buy calculator](/lease-vs-buy/).

## Who qualifies, in general terms

The program is for operating businesses that will occupy the property. The broad tests:

- **For-profit and within the SBA size limits.** The 504 program uses a net worth and net income test that most South Shore small businesses pass easily. A CDC confirms the current thresholds.
- **Owner-occupancy.** An [owner-user](/glossary/owner-user/) generally has to occupy at least 51 percent of an existing building. In the example, the contractor could use 8,000 square feet and lease 4,000 to a tenant. New construction requires more.
- **Ability to repay.** Lenders look at debt service coverage from the business's cash flow, usually on two to three years of tax returns. Rent you already pay gets added back, which helps.
- **Personal guaranties.** Expect anyone owning 20 percent or more to sign one.
- **A public purpose.** The program has job creation and policy goals. Most ordinary deals meet one. The CDC handles that paperwork.

There is also a cap on the CDC portion, in the range of $5 million for most projects and higher for certain manufacturers. For a typical South Shore building it never comes into play.

## What the 504 costs you beyond the rate

Four things an owner should know going in.

**Fees.** The CDC loan carries fees that are usually a few percent of that loan and are typically financed into it rather than paid in cash. The bank may charge its own.

**A prepayment penalty.** The CDC loan generally has a declining [prepayment penalty](/glossary/prepayment-penalty/) for the first ten years. If you expect to sell or refinance in year four, price that in before you sign.

**Time.** Two lenders and an SBA authorization take longer than one bank. Sixty to ninety days from a complete package is a fair planning number. A seller with a second, faster offer may take it.

**Reports.** Expect a full appraisal and at least a Phase I environmental assessment. On older industrial property in Brockton, Avon, or Taunton, the environmental report is where deals slow down. Order it early.

None of these are reasons to avoid the program. They are reasons to start the lender conversation before you tour buildings, not after.

## When 504 fits and when it does not

It fits when cash is the constraint and the business is stable. A practice or a trade business that has paid rent on time for five years, wants to stay put for ten more, and would rather keep working capital than sink it into a down payment is the textbook case.

It fits less well in three situations:

1. **You plan to sell or move within five years.** The prepayment penalty and the closing costs do not have time to earn out.
2. **You need less than half the building.** Below the occupancy threshold it is an investment property, and the program does not finance those.
3. **The seller needs speed.** In a competitive sale, a conventional buyer who can close in 45 days has an edge. A strong pre-approval letter from both lenders narrows the gap.

Some owners also compare the SBA 7(a) program, which can cover a building plus working capital in a single loan, often at a variable rate. A lender who does both will tell you which fits.

## What to ask before you make an offer

Bring these to the first lender call:

- What is today's fixed rate on a 25-year debenture, and when does it lock?
- What is the bank's rate, and for how many years is it fixed before it resets?
- What are the total fees in dollars, and which are financed?
- What is the prepayment schedule, year by year?
- How many days from application to closing on your last three deals?

Then write the offer to match. The financing contingency in the purchase and sale agreement should reflect the real 504 timeline, and your attorney should draft it.

1. Pull three years of business tax returns and a current debt schedule. Every lender asks for them first.
2. Call one bank and one CDC that both do 504 loans in Massachusetts and ask the five questions above.
3. Write down the square footage you actually need, and confirm it is more than half of any building you are considering.
