Every candidate for state office in Massachusetts, and most mayoral and council candidates in the larger cities, files campaign finance reports with the Office of Campaign and Political Finance, and the reports are online, searchable, and free. Most town-level candidates file with the town clerk, where the reports are public records anyone can request. Almost nobody reads them.

That is a waste, because a campaign finance report is the only document in politics where a candidate tells you, under penalty of law, who is paying for them and what they bought. Here is how to read one in fifteen minutes, what the patterns mean, and, just as important, what they do not prove.

## Minute one: know which report you are holding

Start with who filed and on what schedule. Non-depository candidates, which includes most legislative and town candidates, file a pre-primary report about eight days before the primary, a pre-election report about eight days before the general election, and a year-end report in January, with additional reports in some years. Depository candidates, a category that covers statewide offices and many candidates in larger cities including Brockton, use a designated bank that reports receipts and expenditures to OCPF on a more frequent schedule, so the public data updates roughly monthly. Check the current filing calendar on the [OCPF](/glossary/ocpf/) site, because the categories and deadlines are adjusted from time to time.

Why this matters: a pre-election report filed in late October covers a period that ended weeks earlier. The last-minute money shows up in the year-end report, in January, after the election. If you are reading a report in October to understand a race, you are reading history, not the present. The late contributions filing for large last-minute donations is the exception, and worth checking.

## Minutes two to three: the summary

The first page gives you the arc of the campaign in five numbers: beginning balance, total receipts, total expenditures, ending balance, and outstanding liabilities. Write them down.

Then do three pieces of arithmetic. Receipts minus expenditures tells you whether the campaign is building or spending. Ending balance against the weeks remaining tells you the burn rate it can sustain. Liabilities against ending balance tells you whether the campaign is actually solvent. A committee showing $41,000 on hand and $38,000 in unpaid bills to a consultant has $3,000, not $41,000.

Compare the summary across the last three reports. A campaign that raised $60,000 in its first cycle and $19,000 in the current one is a campaign whose donors have moved on, and the summary page says so before anyone reads a receipt.

## Minutes four to eight: receipts

Schedule A is the list of contributions. Under Massachusetts rules, contributions over $50 are itemized with the donor's name and address, and donors who have given $200 or more in a calendar year must also list occupation and employer. Cash gifts over $50 are prohibited. The limits are $1,000 per individual per candidate per year, $200 for registered lobbyists, $500 for PACs, and nothing from corporations.

Sort the itemized receipts three ways:

- **By date.** Clusters tell you about events. Nineteen $1,000 contributions dated across four days are a fundraiser, and the host is usually among them.
- **By employer.** Eleven donors listing the same company or its affiliates are an organized interest. Lawful, and worth knowing.
- **By address.** The share of money from inside the district versus outside it. A council candidate with 70 percent of dollars from out of town has a different base than the one with 70 percent from the ward.

Then look at the unitemized total, the loans, and the candidate's own money. A campaign where the candidate has lent $45,000 to the committee is self-funded until the loan is repaid, and repayment shows up later as an expenditure to the candidate.

OCPF's database searches by donor name across every committee that files electronically. A donor who gave the maximum to both candidates in a race is hedging, and that is a public fact you can find in thirty seconds.

## Minutes nine to twelve: expenditures

Schedule B lists every expenditure: date, payee, address, purpose, and amount. Sort by payee and total each one.

What you are looking for:

- **Concentration.** One consultant taking 60 percent of all spending is running the campaign. Note the firm and search it across other committees.
- **Payments to relatives or to the candidate.** Lawful in many cases, reimbursements especially, but a candidate's spouse drawing $2,800 a month as "campaign manager" is a fact that belongs in the file with the dates.
- **Vague purposes.** "Consulting" and "reimbursement" with no detail. Massachusetts requires reasonable specificity and sub-vendor disclosure for some payments; vagueness is a question to ask, not a conclusion.
- **What the money bought.** Mail, digital, payroll, polling, signs. A campaign spending $22,000 on mail and nothing on digital has decided who its voters are.

A worked example from a composite city council race: the committee reported $38,400 raised and $31,900 spent. Receipts showed 19 contributions of $1,000, 11 from donors listing a single development company or its affiliates as employer, all dated within four days in September. Expenditures showed $18,500 to one consulting firm, $7,200 for mail, $2,800 in monthly payments to a family member listed as campaign manager, and a $12,000 outstanding liability to the consultant.

What that means: the campaign has one organized funding source, one strategist, and a cash position far thinner than the ending balance suggests. What it does not prove: that the development company's employees were reimbursed, that anything was promised to anyone, or that the family member did not do the work. Those are questions. The report cannot answer them.

## Minutes thirteen to fifteen: in-kind and liabilities

Schedule C lists in-kind contributions: goods or services donated rather than paid for, valued at fair market value and counted toward the donor's limit. A printing company donating $900 of signs, a restaurant hosting an event, a consultant working for free. In-kind is where organized support hides from the receipts column, and a campaign with $6,000 of in-kind from one law firm's partners has a backer who does not appear in Schedule A at that scale.

Schedule D lists liabilities: loans and unpaid bills. The $12,000 owed to the consultant in the example above is here. So is a candidate loan that has been carried for three cycles. A committee that ends every cycle owing money to the same vendor is being financed by that vendor, which is a relationship the vendor's other clients might want to know about.

Fifteen minutes in, you have the arc, the base, the strategist, the hidden support, and the debt. That is more than most voters, most reporters, and most opponents will ever know about that race.

## What the patterns mean, and what they never prove

A campaign finance report is a disclosure document. It proves that a contribution was reported and that an expenditure was reported. It does not prove motive, coordination, or a quid pro quo, and a researcher who says it does will be wrong in public.

The honest version of each common finding: clustered employer giving means organized support, not corruption. Out-of-district money means the candidate's network is elsewhere, not that the candidate is bought. A relative on payroll means a relative on payroll. A heavy liability means a vendor is extending credit. Each is a fact for the file, with the date and the page, and each produces a question for the candidate rather than a headline. If the record suggests an actual violation, such as a straw donor scheme or an unregistered committee, that is a complaint to OCPF, and a conversation with counsel about how to make it. It is not an accusation at a debate.

## What to do this week

1. Find the right filing office for the race: OCPF for state and most large-city candidates, the town clerk for the rest.
2. Download the last three reports and write down the five summary numbers from each.
3. Sort receipts by date, employer, and address, and note the clusters.
4. Total expenditures by payee and read the in-kind and liability schedules.
5. Write each pattern as a question with a page reference, not a conclusion.
6. If you are the candidate, run your own reports through the political desk's [self-check](/political/self-check/) before your opponent does.
