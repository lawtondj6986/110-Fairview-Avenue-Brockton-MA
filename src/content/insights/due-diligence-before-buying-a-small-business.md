A buyer looking at a small business gets a package from the seller or the broker: three years of tax returns, a profit and loss statement, an equipment list, a customer summary. Every page of it was prepared by the person who wants the sale to close.

The public record was not. Liens, lawsuits, license files, entity filings, and the deed to the building were created by lenders, courts, regulators, and clerks, on their own schedules, and the seller cannot revise them. That makes the public record the cheapest and most independent diligence available, and it should come first, before the letter of intent hardens and before you pay an accountant to test the numbers.

It takes about a day. Here is the order.

## Start with what you are actually buying

Small business sales on the South Shore are usually asset sales: you buy the equipment, the name, the customer list, and the goodwill, and the seller keeps the entity and its history. Sometimes they are stock or membership interest sales, where you buy the entity itself and everything it has ever done. The difference decides how much of the seller's past becomes yours. Talk to your attorney and your CPA about structure before you sign anything, because the public record findings below land very differently in each.

Then confirm the seller owns what is for sale. Look up the entity with the Secretary of the Commonwealth: exact name, formation date, status, managers or officers. Check that the trade name on the trucks is held by that entity, through a business certificate at the town clerk or a trademark registration, and not by the owner personally or by a second company. A business called one thing, operated by an LLC called another, with the trucks titled to a third, is common and fixable. It needs to be fixed in the purchase agreement, not discovered after closing.

A lapse in the entity's annual reports is not fatal. It is a preview of how the books were kept.

## Liens: who else has a claim on the assets

A [UCC filing](/glossary/ucc-filing/) is a public notice that a lender claims a security interest in a business's assets. They are filed with the Secretary of the Commonwealth, searchable free by debtor name, and they lapse after five years unless continued.

Search the entity's exact name, then each variation, then the owner's name for a sole proprietorship. Read three things on each filing:

- **The collateral.** "All assets" is a blanket lien. A specific truck or machine is equipment finance.
- **The secured party.** A bank, an equipment lender, a supplier. A filing made by a service company "as representative" hides the lender's name and is common with merchant cash advances.
- **The date.** Recent filings in clusters are the signal.

Then run a [lien search](/glossary/lien-search/) for state and federal tax liens and for any recorded judgment lien. Every lien has to be paid or released at closing, with a payoff letter, or the asset you bought still has someone else's name on it.

## Courts and regulators: the last five years

Search the entity and its principals, in their business capacity, across the state trial court's public case system for Plymouth, Norfolk, and Bristol counties, and the federal docket including bankruptcy. A litigation search is looking for patterns, not a single case:

- **Suppliers suing for payment.** Cash is tight.
- **Customers suing over work.** Quality or warranty problems the reviews may not show.
- **Wage claims.** Labor trouble, and possible liability that follows the operation.
- **The business suing customers.** Collection problems in the receivables you are being asked to value.

Then the regulators. Which licenses does this business need to operate, and whose name is on each one? In the trades the license often belongs to the owner personally, not the company. Check the state licensing boards, the home improvement contractor registration, and the local board of health, building department, and fire department for inspection and complaint history. A restaurant's inspection file and a liquor license's violation history are public records, available on request from the town.

## A worked example: an HVAC company in Hanover

A composite. Asking price $850,000 for a residential heating and cooling company with nine employees, six vans, and stated owner earnings of $290,000 a year. That is a multiple of about 2.9, reasonable for the trade.

One day in the public record finds:

1. **Entity.** An LLC formed in 2011, active, with annual reports filed late in two of the last four years.
2. **UCC.** Three active filings. A bank blanket lien from 2021. An equipment lender on two vans. And a 2025 blanket filing by a service company as representative, with no lender named.
3. **Courts.** Two District Court suits by parts suppliers in the past eighteen months, totaling $31,400. Both settled.
4. **Licenses.** The refrigeration and sheet metal licenses are held by the owner personally. No other employee holds them.
5. **Permits.** Steady permit volume in Hanover, Norwell, and Pembroke through 2024, then down roughly a third in 2025.

None of this kills the deal. Each finding changes it. The unnamed 2025 lender has to be identified and paid from proceeds. The supplier suits and the 2025 filing together say cash got tight last year, which matches the permit count and should be tested against the 2025 numbers. And the licenses mean the business cannot legally operate the day the owner leaves, which is [key person risk](/glossary/key-person-risk/) in its plainest form.

The buyer's response in this example: payoff letters for all three liens at closing, $75,000 of the price held in escrow for twelve months, and a twelve-month employment agreement keeping the seller as license holder while a technician qualifies. Same $850,000 headline. A very different deal.

## The lease and the location

Many small businesses are worth what their location is worth, and the buyer rarely owns it. Pull the deed and the recorded documents for the building at the county registry, the same search described in our guide to [researching a landlord](/insights/how-to-research-a-landlord-before-you-sign/).

Then read the lease for four things: the remaining term, the renewal options and whether they survive a transfer, the assignment clause, and any personal guaranty the seller signed. Most commercial leases require the landlord's consent to assign, and some let the landlord take the space back or reset the rent when asked. A business with fourteen months of lease left and no option is a business with a fourteen-month horizon, whatever the earnings say. Add up the rent, term, and escalations to see the full occupancy cost you are inheriting.

Check zoning and the certificate of occupancy too. A use that has run for thirty years may be a lawful nonconforming use that carries conditions, or one that was never permitted at all.

## What the record cannot tell you, and where ICS stops

The public record will not tell you whether the revenue is real, whether the customers will stay, or what the business is worth. That is the work of your accountant, your attorney, and your own time in the shop. The record tells you where to point them.

We do not surveil private individuals, and the seller's family, health, and personal life are not in the file. We do not obtain medical, credit, or private financial data. ## What to do this week

1. Look up the seller's entity and write down the exact name, status, and annual report dates.
2. Run the UCC search on every name variation and list each secured party, date, and collateral description.
3. Search the entity in the state and federal court systems for the past five years.
4. List every license the business needs to operate and whose name is on each.
5. Ask the seller for the lease and read the assignment clause before anything else.
