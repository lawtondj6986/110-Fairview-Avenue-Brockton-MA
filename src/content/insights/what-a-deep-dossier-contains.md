People ask what they get for the money. Fair question. "Research" can mean a search engine and an afternoon, or it can mean a document that a lawyer, a lender, or a board will rely on. The Deep Dossier is the second thing, and the way to understand it is to walk through the file the way a reader would, from the first page to the methods note at the back.

This article does that. It also states, in plain terms, what the file never contains, because the limits are what make it usable. A finding gathered cleanly can be put in front of anyone. A finding gathered improperly cannot be used at all.

## The scope letter comes before the file

Nothing starts until a one-page scope letter is agreed in writing. It names the subject, the decision riding on the research (a purchase, a partnership, a loan, a lease, a campaign), the specific questions the file must answer, the jurisdictions in play, the timeline, and the price. It also says what the lawful record cannot answer, so nobody expects a bank balance to appear on page nine.

It includes a conflict check. ICS does not research a subject for two opposing parties, and in political work it is one client per race. Pricing is stated as a number, not a range you discover later; most [Deep Dossiers](/intelligence/dossier/) land between roughly $2,500 and $10,000, with multi-state, multi-entity files running higher. If the decision does not justify the number, the scope letter says so and points you to the free checklist instead. The [60-second scoper](/intelligence/scope/) does a rough version of this on the site.

## Section one: the BLUF

The file opens with the bottom line up front. One page. The three findings that decide the question, each stated in a sentence, each carrying a confidence level, each pointing to the exhibit that supports it.

A [BLUF](/glossary/bluf/) from a composite file on a seller of a flex building might read: the entity on the purchase agreement is active and controlled by the person you have been negotiating with (high confidence, exhibits 1 to 4); the property carries an unreleased $340,000 second mortgage not disclosed in the offering package (high confidence, exhibit 7); the seller's prior LLC was the defendant in a 2021 Superior Court case alleging undisclosed roof defects on a comparable sale, settled before trial (moderate confidence on relevance, exhibits 11 to 13). Three sentences, and the buyer's attorney knows what to ask for.

## Section two: chronology and entity map

The chronology is a dated table of everything the record shows about the subject, from first corporate filing to last court entry, with a source on every line. Read top to bottom, it shows the shape of a career: the entities formed and dissolved, the properties bought and sold, the suits filed and settled, the licenses obtained and lapsed. Gaps are marked as gaps. A twelve-year chronology with a two-year hole is itself a finding.

The entity map is the diagram of who owns what. Starting from the name on the contract, it traces LLCs, holding companies, trusts, officers, managers, registered agents, and shared addresses until it reaches the people and the assets. On a typical South Shore commercial subject this runs three to eight entities. The point is [beneficial ownership](/glossary/beneficial-ownership/): the entity three layers down that actually holds the asset, or the partner nobody mentioned who holds 40 percent of it.

## Section three: litigation, liens, and licensing

Three tables, one per category, each sortable and each footnoted.

**Litigation** covers every civil and criminal docket in every county and state where the subject or its entities have operated, with the subject's role (plaintiff or defendant), the claim type, the disposition, and the amount. A dismissed case is listed as dismissed. A default judgment is listed as a default judgment, because they mean different things.

**Liens and encumbrances** covers tax liens, UCC secured filings, judgments recorded at the registry, mechanic's liens, mortgages, and bankruptcies. For a seller or borrower this is the public footprint of solvency. A $1.1 million property with $1.4 million of recorded debt is a fact the deal has to account for.

**Licensing and regulatory** covers professional licenses, their status and discipline, and actions by regulators from the Attorney General to the licensing boards to the SEC. A revoked license in another state, under a prior business name, is exactly the kind of item that never surfaces in a search and sits in a board's public records for anyone who pulls them.

## Section four: reputation, sources, and confidence

Reputation is the open web across time: news coverage, public statements, prior positions, and the pages that were deleted and preserved in public web archives. A 2019 bio that claimed a degree the 2024 bio no longer mentions is clipped, timestamped, and included. Social media is read only where it is public, and only for what the subject said in a business or public capacity.

Then the part that separates a dossier from a pile: every source carries a reliability grade on the standard A to F and 1 to 6 scale used across professional intelligence work, and every conclusion carries a confidence level of high, moderate, or low. [Source grading](/glossary/source-grading/) means a court docket and an anonymous forum post do not weigh the same, and the reader can see the difference. The file keeps the record in one column and ICS's assessment in another, and for any finding that could be read two ways it states both hypotheses and which one the evidence supports.

A worked example of the discipline: a subject's name appears as a defendant in four Housing Court cases. The damaging reading is a landlord who evicts freely. The innocent reading is a property manager named as a formality. The file pulls the four complaints, finds the subject named personally as owner in two and as agent in two, and rates the finding "moderate confidence: two owner-capacity evictions in three years." That is the number that goes to the client. Not four.

## What the Deep Dossier never does

The limits are published on purpose, and they apply to every file at every price.

- **No pretexting or impersonation.** Nobody calls a bank, a doctor's office, or a former employer pretending to be someone else. Nothing is obtained by deception. That is the pretexting line and it is not negotiable.
- **No surveillance of private individuals.** No following, no photographing, no device access, no location tracking, no monitoring of private communications.
- **No medical, credit, or private financial data.** Credit reports, bank records, medical history, and anything else available only with consent or legal process are out, full stop. The financial section is built from the public record of liens, judgments, and filings, nothing else.
- **No private individuals' personal lives.** Subjects are organizations, public figures, officials, and the principals behind a transaction, in their business or public capacity. A counterparty's divorce is not in the file unless it is in a recorded deed that affects title.
- **No consumer reports.** The Deep Dossier is never produced for employment, credit, or tenant-screening decisions. Those are governed by the Fair Credit Reporting Act and belong with licensed consumer reporting agencies. If a request is really a hiring decision, we decline it.

The file also does not offer legal conclusions. It tells you what the record shows and how sure we are. What to do about it is a conversation with your attorney, and the dossier is written so your attorney can check every line.

## What arrives, and how

A PDF with the BLUF, the chronology, the entity map, the three tables, the reputation section, the source exhibits, and a methods note describing exactly what was searched, where, and on what dates, so that anyone can repeat the work. Delivery is confidential, walked through personally, and the working file is not retained beyond the engagement. The subject does not learn that anyone asked.

A fully fictional [sample dossier](/intelligence/dossier/sample/) is on the site, with a BLUF, an ownership map, graded sources, and a worked competing-hypotheses finding, so you can see the standard before you ask for anything.

## What to do this week

1. Write down the decision and the dollar amount riding on it. If the amount is small and reversible, use the free checklist and stop.
2. List every name on the other side: the entity on the paper, the people you have met, and the management company or broker in between.
3. Pull the deed, the corporate filing, and the trial court docket yourself. Thirty minutes, free, and often enough.
4. Note what you still do not know after that and whether it would change your decision.
5. If it would, run the [60-second scoper](/intelligence/scope/) and we will tell you what the record can deliver and what it costs, in writing, before you commit a dollar.
