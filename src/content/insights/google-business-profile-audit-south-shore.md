Before ICS writes a line of code or buys a single ad for a client, we open their Google Business Profile and score it. The score is out of 25. Most South Shore businesses we audit land between 9 and 14. The ones that land under 10 are usually not losing to a better competitor. They are losing to a listing they set up on a phone six years ago and never opened again.

The audit exists because the profile is the front door. It is what shows in the [local pack](/glossary/local-pack/), what Maps reads to decide who appears for "electrician near me" in Abington, and increasingly what AI assistants summarize when they name three businesses and stop. Fixing a profile is free. Not fixing it makes everything downstream cost more.

Here is the full list, in the five groups we use.

## Points 1 to 5: identity

These are the facts Google uses to decide you are one real business, and they must match everywhere.

1. **Name is the legal or trade name, nothing added.** "Smith Plumbing" passes. "Smith Plumbing | Best Plumber Weymouth 24/7" fails and risks suspension.
2. **Address or service area is set correctly.** A shop customers visit shows the address. A contractor who works from home hides it and sets a service area instead. Google lets you list up to about twenty towns; list the ones you actually drive to.
3. **Phone number matches the website and every directory.** One number. A tracking number is fine only if the original is listed as a secondary line.
4. **Website link points to the right page,** and carries tracking so you can see Maps traffic separately in analytics.
5. **Hours are true, including holiday hours.** A profile showing "open" on Thanksgiving when you are closed earns a one-star review from the person who drove over.

Points 1, 3, and 4 are what the glossary calls [NAP consistency](/glossary/nap-consistency/). When they disagree across the web, Google hedges, and a hedging Google ranks you lower.

## Points 6 to 10: categories and services

6. **Primary category is the most specific one that fits.** Not Contractor; HVAC Contractor. Not Restaurant; Seafood Restaurant.
7. **Secondary categories cover the other real lines of work,** up to nine, and nothing you do not do.
8. **Every service is listed by name with a description.** A plumber with Water Heater Installation, Drain Cleaning, and Sump Pump Repair each described in two sentences gives Google text to match against searches.
9. **Prices or price ranges are entered where the service list allows them.** Businesses that publish a range get chosen by people who have already accepted it.
10. **Attributes are set:** veteran-owned, wheelchair accessible, women-owned, online estimates, and the rest that apply. They appear as filters and in AI summaries.

The category change is where the biggest swings come from. A Rockland heating company we audited was listed as Contractor. Its profile showed about 1,100 search impressions a month, most of them for general construction terms it could not serve. After switching to HVAC Contractor with Furnace Repair Service as a secondary, impressions dropped to about 900 but calls from the profile roughly doubled, because the 900 were people who wanted a furnace fixed.

## Points 11 to 15: content

11. **Business description uses the full 750 characters,** states the towns, the services, the years in business, and the license, in plain sentences.
12. **At least twenty photos, with new ones added monthly.** Real jobs, real trucks, real staff. Stock photos are discounted.
13. **A logo and a cover image that are actually yours.**
14. **Products or menu populated** where the category supports them. For restaurants the menu; for a salon the service list with prices; for a contractor the main packages.
15. **At least one post in the last thirty days.** A finished job in Whitman with two photos is a post. Posts expire from view, so a profile with nothing recent looks dormant.

Content points are the easiest to score and the most often zero. In our audits, roughly two thirds of South Shore profiles have fewer than ten photos and no post in the last year. That is not a competitive market. That is an open field.

## Points 16 to 20: reviews and engagement

16. **Review count is within range of the top three competitors in your category and town.** Not equal, within range. If they have 120 and you have 15, you are not in the conversation yet.
17. **Review velocity: at least two new reviews a month,** and ideally more. Google weights recency, and so does the person reading.
18. **Every review has a reply,** including the five-star ones. Reply rate is visible to Google and to customers.
19. **Review text mentions services and towns.** "Fixed our furnace in Hingham the same day" is worth more than "Great service." You cannot write the reviews, but you can ask after the job that produced the specific result.
20. **Q&A section is populated by you,** with the eight or ten questions customers actually ask, answered. If you leave it empty, strangers fill it in.

A Weymouth auto shop we audited scored 2 of 5 here: 61 reviews, average 4.7, but the newest was eleven months old and none had replies. Its nearest competitor on Route 18 had 48 reviews, lower average, and four new ones that month. The competitor ranked higher for "brake repair Weymouth." Count was not the problem. The shop had simply stopped asking.

## Points 21 to 25: hygiene and competition

21. **No duplicate listings.** Old addresses, a previous owner's profile, a listing a directory created. Each one splits your reviews and confuses Google. Find them and request a merge or removal.
22. **Verification is complete and current.** Google periodically re-verifies, often by video. An unverified profile cannot be edited and may stop showing.
23. **Messaging is turned on and answered,** or turned off. On and ignored is worse than off.
24. **Booking or appointment link is set** for businesses that take appointments. It becomes a button on the profile.
25. **Competitor spam is reported.** Fake listings, keyword-stuffed names, and profiles at addresses that do not exist are common in trades. Google has a form for this and acts on well-documented reports. Cleaning up three fake plumbers in your town moves you up three spots at no cost.

Scoring is one point each, no partial credit. Under 12: fix the profile before spending a dollar on ads or a website, because both will underperform. 12 to 18: fix the top gaps in the first two weeks of any install. 19 and above: the profile is not the bottleneck, and the audit moves on to the website and the review system.

## What two finished audits looked like

The Weymouth auto shop scored 11. Fixes in order: Brake Shop and Oil Change Service added as secondary categories under Auto Repair Shop, 14 services added with descriptions, 30 photos uploaded from the owner's phone, replies written to all 61 reviews over two evenings, automated review requests turned on after every invoice. Six weeks later the profile showed 19 new reviews, a 4.8 average, and calls from the profile up about 40 percent against the prior six weeks. See the [auto services playbook](/industries/auto-services/playbook/) for the full sequence.

The Rockland heating company scored 9. Beyond the category fix, it had a duplicate listing at the owner's former home address with 23 reviews stranded on it. Merging the two brought the live profile from 38 reviews to 61 in one step. That is a point that cost nothing and would never have been found without looking.

## What to do this week

1. Search your business name from a phone, not from the profile manager, and screenshot what a customer sees.
2. Score the 25 points honestly, one point each, and write down your number.
3. Fix the primary category and the phone number first; they take ten minutes and move the most.
4. Add ten real photos and reply to every unanswered review before Friday.
5. Search your category plus your town and report any listing with a stuffed name or an address that does not exist.
6. Run the free [AI Readiness score](/ai-readiness/), which checks the profile points above alongside your website and the sources AI assistants read.
