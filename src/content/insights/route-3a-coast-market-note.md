Route 3A is the South Shore the postcards show. It leaves Weymouth as Bridge Street, passes Hingham Harbor and the Shipyard, runs through Cohasset village, past Scituate Harbor and North Scituate, along Marshfield's Ocean Street, through Duxbury and Kingston, and ends at the Plymouth waterfront. Hull hangs off the top of it on Route 228, a peninsula with one road and a beach.

The commercial stock is older, smaller, and mixed: storefronts with apartments above, marinas and boatyards, restaurants that make their year between Memorial Day and Labor Day and hold on through February. Almost none of it is vacant for long, and almost none of it is cheap.

This note is for two readers at once. The business that lives on the summer and needs to survive the winter. And the owner of the building, who collects rent twelve months a year and needs to understand what the tenant downstairs is going through. The numbers are typical bands from our reads, not named deals.

## Eight towns, one road, several markets

The coast is not one market, and the town line matters more here than almost anywhere on the South Shore.

[Hingham](/south-shore/hingham/) is the affluent anchor: the Shipyard's mixed-use waterfront, the historic storefronts of Hingham Square, and professional and medical office along 3A. High value, tightly held, with diligence on title, use restrictions, and parking that matters more than price per foot. [Cohasset](/south-shore/cohasset/) is a short list: the village on South Main Street, the harbor, and the 3A plazas it shares with its neighbors. [Scituate](/south-shore/scituate/) is a working harbor on Front Street, a second band on 3A, and the Greenbush rail terminus anchoring commuter demand at the south end.

[Marshfield](/south-shore/marshfield/) is where Route 139 meets the coast: a year-round service economy on 139 and 3A with the seasonal layer at Brant Rock and Green Harbor stacked on top. [Duxbury](/south-shore/duxbury/) keeps its footprint deliberately light at Hall's Corner and Snug Harbor. [Kingston](/south-shore/kingston/) is the retail district at the Route 3 exit, repricing as big-box retail reshapes, with 3A carrying the service and auto stock. [Plymouth](/south-shore/plymouth/) is the biggest stage: the downtown waterfront on Court Street and Main Street, tourism traffic, and a trade area measured in counties. [Hull](/south-shore/hull/) is Nantasket Avenue facing the beach, mixed-use blocks in the village, and buildings that trade almost always off-market.

The [Route 3A corridor guide](/corridors/route-3a/) and the [Route 228 guide](/corridors/route-228/) cover who does business on each stretch.

## The seasonal tenant's math

Start with the restaurant, the business the coast is built on and the one the winter hurts most.

A worked example. A 2,400 square foot restaurant on a harbor-front street rents at $36 per square foot: $86,400 a year, $7,200 a month, before pass-throughs. The operator does $1.4 million in annual sales. Rent is 6.2 percent of sales for the year, which is healthy.

Week by week it looks different. In our reads, a harbor restaurant does roughly 55 to 65 percent of its year in the fourteen or fifteen weeks from late May to early September. In a July week this operator takes in about $55,000. In a February week, closer to $12,000. Rent is $1,660 a week either way. That is 3 percent of sales in July and 14 percent in February.

The lease decides whether February is survivable. Three terms help, and a tenant with a real monthly sales history can usually get one:

- A rent schedule that follows the season: more per month in summer, less in winter, same total.
- A percentage rent component in place of part of the base, so the landlord shares the summer and the tenant is not alone in the winter.
- A written right to reduce hours or close in the slowest months without default, including under any operating covenant.

The same math applies to the ice cream window, the charter boat, and the marine detailer. If the season is the revenue, the lease should know it.

## The year-round owner's math

Now the building. The typical coastal village asset is a two- or three-story mixed-use building: two storefronts below, three apartments above, on a tight lot with parking that was never enough.

A worked example. A 6,500 square foot building in a village center holds two 1,200 square foot storefronts and three two-bedroom apartments. In our reads the storefronts rent in a band of roughly $24 to $30 per foot and the apartments at $2,300 to $2,800 a month on this coast. Call it $26 per foot and $2,500 a month: $62,400 from the commercial and $90,000 from the residential, $152,400 gross. An older coastal building runs expenses of roughly 35 to 40 percent of gross once heat, insurance, and deferred items are counted. Net operating income lands near $95,000 to $100,000.

Coastal village buildings in our reads trade at cap rates of roughly 6 to 7 percent when they trade at all, because the buyer is paying for the address. That puts this building in a band of about $1.4 million to $1.65 million.

The owner's exposure is the storefront downstairs. If the restaurant tenant closes in March, the owner has a space with a hood, a grease trap, and a long winter before anyone opens a restaurant again. That is why coastal landlords who understand the season write seasonal leases with strong tenants rather than flat leases with weak ones. A tenant who survives the winter is worth more than two dollars a foot.

## Flood, septic, parking: what decides coastal deals

Price per foot is the last thing that kills a coastal deal. These three come first.

**Flood.** Much of the harbor and beach stock sits in a FEMA special flood hazard area. A federally backed mortgage on a building in one generally requires flood insurance, and the premium runs from a few thousand dollars a year to much more for an older building below base flood elevation. Substantial improvement of a building in a flood zone can also trigger flood-resistant construction rules under the state building code, turning a cosmetic renovation into an elevation project. Pull the flood map before you price the work.

**Septic.** Many coastal parcels outside the village centers are on septic. Massachusetts Title 5 generally requires a septic inspection within two years before a transfer of title, with some exceptions, and local boards of health in several of these towns layer stricter rules on top. A restaurant or medical use on septic is a design flow problem before it is a lease problem.

**Parking.** Village and harbor districts were built before the car and have never caught up. A change of use from retail to restaurant typically raises the parking requirement, and the lot cannot grow. Some towns offer shared or municipal parking credits; some do not. Read the bylaw and the town's practice, not just the number.

Add historic district review in several village centers and wetlands buffers near every harbor. Talk to your attorney before you commit on any of this.

## Marine services and the spring rush

The marine economy is the coast's quiet second market. Boatyards, marinas, winter storage, repair, charters, and detailing live on a calendar as sharp as the restaurant's, with the rush running from the first warm week in April through launch.

The buildings are specific: waterfront or near-water parcels with outdoor storage, a travel lift or a ramp, and zoning that still permits the use. They trade rarely because the alternatives do not exist; a boatyard that closes usually becomes housing. An operator who wants to own one should be in conversation with the current owners years before any of them decide to sell.

The business side is simpler. Marine customers call in a short window, every call is a quote, and phone tag in April is revenue lost for the season. The operators who put quoting and booking online before the rush book the summer in the spring. The marine services playbook on this site is written for exactly that calendar.

## What to do this week

1. Decide which town you are really in: village, harbor, or the 3A service strip, because the rent band and the buyer pool differ in each.
2. If you are a seasonal tenant, build a month-by-month sales history and bring it to the next lease conversation with a seasonal rent schedule in hand.
3. If you own, pull the flood map and the septic record for your parcel before a buyer does.
4. Check the parking requirement for any change of use you or a tenant is considering, in the bylaw and in the town's practice.
5. Marine and seasonal operators: get quoting and booking live before April, not during it.
6. Run the building's numbers through the [lease analyzer](/lease-analyzer/) to see what the current lease is really costing each side.
