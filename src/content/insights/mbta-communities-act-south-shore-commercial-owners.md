Most commercial owners on the South Shore have heard of the MBTA Communities Act as a housing fight at town meeting. Fewer have asked the question that matters to them: is my parcel inside the new district, and if so, what is the land under my building worth now?

Section 3A of Chapter 40A, added in 2021, requires MBTA communities to zone at least one district of reasonable size where multifamily housing is allowed as of right. Most of the South Shore qualifies. The districts have been drawn, voted, contested, redrawn, and in some towns are still moving. Where they landed, a strip retail parcel or a surface parking lot near a station is no longer just an income property. It is a development site with a second value.

This article is about finding out which side of the line you are on and what to do about it. It is not legal advice. Zoning varies town by town and the compliance picture keeps changing, so talk to your attorney before relying on any of this for a transaction.

## What the law actually does

Section 3A requires each MBTA community to have a multifamily district where housing is permitted as of right, meaning no special permit and no discretionary vote. Site plan review is still allowed, but the town cannot say no to a conforming project. The state's rules set a minimum gross density of 15 units per acre, bar age restrictions, and require the district to be suitable for families with children. Where a town has a transit station, the rules generally require some portion of the district to fall within a half mile of it.

Towns are sorted into categories: rapid transit, commuter rail, adjacent, and adjacent small town. The category sets how many units the district must be able to hold and how much of it must be near a station. Deadlines were staggered from the end of 2023 through the end of 2025.

The enforcement picture changed in early 2025 when the Supreme Judicial Court ruled that the law is constitutional and mandatory, while also finding the state's original guidelines had not been properly promulgated. The state reissued them as regulations. The practical result is that towns that refused to comply face loss of certain state grant programs and potential enforcement by the Attorney General. Some South Shore towns complied early, some complied late, and a few are still in dispute. Check the current status of your town before assuming anything.

## Where the South Shore stands

The Middleborough/Lakeville commuter rail line runs through [Brockton](/south-shore/brockton/) with three stations, then Bridgewater. The Kingston line runs through Abington, Whitman, Hanson, Halifax, and Kingston. Plymouth has a station on that line whose service has been intermittent, which affects how the half-mile rule is applied there. Quincy and Braintree are Red Line communities with the heaviest requirements. Weymouth has Greenbush line stations. Rockland, Hanover, Norwell, and Marshfield are adjacent communities with lighter obligations and no station-proximity requirement.

Compliance has looked different in each place. Brockton already allowed dense residential downtown and had relatively little to change. Some towns drew the district over land that is already built out, which satisfies the letter of the law without inviting much new construction. Others, under pressure or by choice, drew districts over genuinely redevelopable commercial land near stations. That second kind of district is where the owner question gets interesting.

Do not take anyone's word for which kind your town has. Get the adopted map and read the district text. The questions that matter: what density is allowed above the 15-unit floor, what height, what parking minimum, and whether mixed-use with ground-floor commercial is permitted alongside the residential. The state rules prevent a town from requiring ground-floor commercial in the district, but towns may allow it.

## Two values for one parcel

An income property has one value: what its net operating income supports at a market [cap rate](/cap-rate/). A development site has another: what a builder will pay per buildable unit, less the cost of clearing what is there. Before 3A, most South Shore commercial parcels had only the first value because housing needed a special permit nobody could count on. Inside a 3A district, both values exist, and the owner gets the higher one.

A worked example. A 0.9-acre parcel near a Kingston line station holds a 6,000-square-foot single-story retail building with three tenants. Net operating income is $90,000. At a 7.5 percent cap rate, the income value is $1.2 million.

Now the land. At the 15-unit floor, 0.9 acres supports about 13 units. If the town's district text allows 30 units per acre, the same parcel supports about 27 units. South Shore builders have been paying roughly $40,000 to $70,000 per buildable unit for sites with utilities and no environmental problems, in our experience, with the number driven by what rents or condo prices the location can support. At 27 units and $50,000 per unit, the land is worth $1.35 million before demolition, which might run $60,000 to $100,000 for a building that size.

The land value edges out the income value, and the gap widens if the district allows more density or if rents justify a higher per-unit number. The owner has not done anything. The zoning moved.

## Where this applies most

Three property types are most affected:

- **Surface parking lots and underused yards within a half mile of a station.** These have little income value and the most to gain. A 1.5-acre paved lot earning $20,000 a year in monthly parking is worth maybe $250,000 as income. At 20 units per acre and $50,000 per unit, it is a $1.5 million site.
- **Single-story retail and service buildings on larger lots.** Land-to-building ratios of 4 to 1 or more mean the building was never the point. A buyer who can go to four stories by right sees the lot, not the laundromat.
- **Older mixed-use buildings in downtowns like Brockton, Rockland, and Whitman.** Here 3A often confirms what the market already wanted. It removes permitting risk, which lowers a developer's required return and raises what they will pay.

Owners of well-leased, newer buildings on tight lots are the least affected. The income value still wins, and the 3A optionality is worth little until the leases run out.

## The hedges

Do not price optionality you cannot verify. A 3A district drawn over your parcel is a fact on a map. A builder paying for it depends on things the map does not show. Check each one:

- **Utilities.** Sewer capacity is the quiet limit in several South Shore towns. Multifamily on septic is not realistic at 15 units per acre. See the [septic versus sewer](/glossary/septic-vs-sewer/) note.
- **Environmental history.** A former auto shop or dry cleaner carries [Chapter 21E](/glossary/chapter-21e/) exposure that a residential builder prices harshly.
- **Lease encumbrances.** A tenant with eight years left and a renewal option sets the earliest redevelopment date. Buyers discount for every year they wait.
- **Town stability.** A district adopted under protest may be amended. Towns can move the district as long as they stay compliant, and some have signaled they will.
- **Market depth.** Fifteen units per acre is a floor on paper. If no one has built a 20-unit project in that town in a decade, the per-unit land value is theory.

Also remember that selling a long-held commercial building to a developer has tax consequences that differ from a sale to another investor, and that a ground lease or joint venture might suit you better than a sale. Talk to your attorney and CPA before structuring anything.

## What to do this week

1. Request the adopted 3A district map and text from your town's planning department and confirm whether your parcel ID is inside it.
2. Read the district's density, height, parking, and mixed-use provisions, and note any pending amendments on the next town meeting warrant.
3. Pull your rent roll and lease expirations to find the earliest date the site could be delivered vacant.
4. Run your income value with the [cap rate tool](/cap-rate/) and compare it to a per-unit land estimate at the district's allowed density.
5. Ask your attorney about the tax picture on a developer sale versus a ground lease or hold.
6. Order a [Buyer Brief](/buyer-brief/) on your own parcel to see zoning, assessor data, deed history, and environmental flags on one page.
