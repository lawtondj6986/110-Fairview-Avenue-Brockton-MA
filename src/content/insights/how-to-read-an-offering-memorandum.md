An offering memorandum arrives as a glossy PDF with a drone photo on the cover and a cap rate on page three. It is the listing broker's case for the asking price. It is not a disclosure document, it is not audited, and the broker has no duty to make the deal look worse than it is.

That is not a complaint. It is the reason a buyer needs a method. Read an [offering memorandum](/glossary/offering-memorandum/) the same way every time, pull the same seven numbers, recompute the one that matters, and then ask about the three things the document almost never contains. Twenty minutes gets you to a pass, a tour, or a short list of questions for the broker.

Here is the method we use on South Shore deals.

## The seven numbers

Find these before reading a word of the narrative:

1. **Asking price and the cap rate it implies.** Note which NOI the cap rate is computed on. It is almost always pro forma.
2. **Net operating income, actual and pro forma.** If only one is shown, it is pro forma and you need to ask for the trailing twelve months.
3. **In-place rents versus the rents assumed.** The rent roll shows what tenants pay now. The pro forma often assumes something higher. The gap is the broker's thesis.
4. **Weighted average lease term remaining.** How long the income is contracted. A 7 percent cap rate with 18 months of average term is a different asset than the same cap rate with seven years.
5. **Expense ratio and recovery structure.** What percentage of gross income goes to operating expenses, and who pays them. A gross lease building at 45 percent and a triple-net building at 12 percent are not comparable on cap rate alone.
6. **Price per square foot.** Compare it to replacement cost and to recent sales of similar buildings. A price above replacement cost needs a reason.
7. **Occupancy and the vacancy allowance.** A building shown at 100 percent occupied with no vacancy factor in the pro forma is a building with a pro forma that will not survive the first move-out.

Write all seven in the margin. Most of the twenty minutes is spent on the next step.

## Recompute the cap rate yourself

The quoted cap rate is the broker's number. Yours will be lower. The question is how much lower.

A worked example from the kind of deal that crosses our desk. A 12,000-square-foot multi-tenant retail building in Rockland is offered at $2.4 million at a 7.0 percent cap rate on pro forma NOI of $168,000. The rent roll shows in-place gross rent of $228,000 and the pro forma shows $240,000 after two assumed renewals at higher rates. Expenses are listed at $72,000.

Rebuild it on actual:

- Start with in-place rent, $228,000, not the assumed $240,000.
- Apply a 5 percent vacancy and credit loss allowance, about $11,400, because tenants leave.
- Add a management fee of 4 percent of collected rent, about $8,700, even if you plan to self-manage. Your time is not free and a lender will underwrite it anyway.
- Add a capital reserve of roughly $0.25 per square foot, $3,000, for roof and paving over time.
- Check the tax line. The OM shows $26,000 in taxes based on the current assessment. Rockland's assessor will likely reset to something near your purchase price. At the town's commercial rate, taxes on $2.4 million might run $38,000 to $42,000. Call it a $14,000 increase. If leases are triple-net, tenants absorb some of this, but check each lease for tax caps and base-year clauses.

Actual NOI: $228,000 less $11,400 vacancy, less $72,000 stated expenses, less $8,700 management, less $3,000 reserves, less $14,000 tax increase equals $118,900. At $2.4 million, that is a 4.95 percent cap rate, not 7.0 percent.

The deal is not necessarily dead. But the price that produces a 7.0 percent return on real income is about $1.7 million. That is the number to negotiate from, and the [cap rate tool](/cap-rate/) on this site runs this arithmetic in two minutes.

## Read the rent roll like a lender

The rent roll is the only page in the OM that is close to a fact. Read every line.

For each tenant, note the start date, expiration, current rent, scheduled increases, options to renew, and security deposit. Then compute the weighted average lease term. Multiply each tenant's remaining term in years by its share of total rent, and add the results.

A second worked example. Three tenants: a pharmacy paying $120,000 with six years left, a nail salon paying $48,000 with 14 months left, and a pizza shop paying $60,000 with three years left. Total rent $228,000. The pharmacy is 53 percent of rent at six years, contributing 3.2 years. The salon is 21 percent at 1.2 years, contributing 0.25 years. The pizza shop is 26 percent at three years, contributing 0.8 years. Weighted average term is about 4.2 years.

That is a reasonable number. But notice that 47 percent of the income rolls within three years, and the pharmacy's lease, when you eventually read it, may hold a termination right tied to a corporate relocation. The OM will say the pharmacy is a national credit tenant on a long lease. Both true. Neither tells you about the clause on page 31.

Ask for the actual leases before the letter of intent. A [rent roll](/glossary/rent-roll/) summarizes; the lease governs.

## The three things it leaves out

**Capital needs.** No OM has a line that says the roof is 24 years old and the parking lot will need $90,000 in three years. Look at the photos for ponding, patched asphalt, rooftop units of varying ages, and single-pane storefronts. Ask the broker directly for the age of the roof, HVAC, and paving, and for any capital spent in the last five years. Budget what they cannot answer.

**The leases themselves.** Options to renew at below-market rents, early termination rights, exclusive-use clauses that block you from leasing to a competitor, co-tenancy clauses that let a tenant cut rent if an anchor leaves, and relocation rights that run against you. None of this appears in a rent roll. All of it changes value.

**Taxes after the sale, and the property's history.** Massachusetts assessors reset on sale, and several South Shore cities including Brockton use a split rate that taxes commercial property well above residential. Separately, the OM will rarely mention that the building was a gas station in 1978. Pull the assessor card, the deed history, and check for any [Chapter 21E](/glossary/chapter-21e/) filings before you fall in love. If the deal is complex, talk to your attorney and CPA about structure and tax before signing anything.

## Pass, tour, or question list

The twenty minutes should end one of three ways.

Pass when the recomputed cap rate is more than 150 basis points below the quoted one and the broker's pro forma depends on rent growth that the submarket does not support. Pass when the weighted average lease term is under two years and you are not an operator who wants the space.

Tour when the recomputed number is within 50 to 100 basis points of the ask, the rent roll is credible, and the photos do not show deferred maintenance you cannot price.

Send a question list when you are in between. Five questions, in writing, to the broker: trailing twelve months of actual income and expenses, copies of all leases and amendments, age of roof, HVAC, and paving, any environmental reports, and the current assessed value with the tax bill. The answers, or the silence, tell you more than the glossy pages did.

## What to do this week

1. Pull the next OM in your inbox and write the seven numbers in the margin before reading the narrative.
2. Recompute NOI on in-place rent with vacancy, management, reserves, and a post-sale tax estimate.
3. Build the weighted average lease term from the rent roll and flag every lease that rolls within 36 months.
4. Send the broker the five-question list and note how long the trailing twelve takes to arrive.
5. Pull the assessor card and deed history for the parcel yourself.
6. Run your recomputed numbers through the [deal tools](/deal-tools/) to see the price that gives you the return you actually need.
