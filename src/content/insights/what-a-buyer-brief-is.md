Ask an owner-user what they are looking for and the first answer is usually "something bigger." Ask again and it becomes "a shop with a yard, under two million, somewhere near Route 24, this year if the numbers work." That second answer is a buyer brief. It is the difference between scrolling listings and running a hunt.

## Why the hunt needs a page

On the South Shore, sub-$3M commercial buildings mostly trade quietly. The owner of the flex building in West Bridgewater is a contractor who has owned it for twenty years and will sell to the first serious person who asks on the right day. He is not on a portal. He is not returning cold calls. He is answering a neighbor, a tenant, or an advisor who can say in one sentence what their client needs and why his building fits.

That sentence has to be true, specific, and ready before the conversation starts. A brief makes it so. It also saves everyone's time: an advisor with a clear brief can tell you in one call whether the building exists at your budget, instead of touring six that were never going to work.

## What goes in it

**The seat.** Owner-user or investor. An owner-user is buying a means to an end: more room, a yard, a loading dock, a sign on the road. An investor is buying income, and the questions are cap rate, tenant credit, and lease term. The first move is different for each, which is why the [Buyer Brief tool](/buyer-brief/) points owner-users at the [Lease vs. Buy calculator](/lease-vs-buy/) and investors at the [Cap Rate and NOI calculator](/cap-rate/).

**The building.** Property type (retail, industrial and flex, office, mixed-use, automotive, medical, land) and size range. "Flex" on the South Shore means clear-span space with a drive-in door and, if you are lucky, a fenced yard. Be honest about the size you need in three years, not today.

**The towns.** Name them. Inventory, zoning, and price per foot change at town lines, and so does the owner network. A brief that says "Brockton, West Bridgewater, Abington" lets an advisor work three owner lists instead of twenty-nine. The tool lets you tap towns on the map and pulls each town's corridors and property read onto the brief automatically. If you think in roads rather than towns, start with the [corridor guides](/corridors/).

**The must-haves.** Yard or outdoor storage. Loading dock or drive-in door. Route frontage and signage. Parking count. Clear height. Zoning for your use, confirmed, not assumed. Each one removes buildings from the list, which is the point. A short list of real must-haves beats a long list of wishes.

**The money.** A budget band and how you will finance it. SBA 504 and 7(a) loans make owner-user purchases possible with ten percent down, but they have their own timelines and documentation, and a seller who hears "SBA" early rarely balks later. Cash sets the terms. "Not sure yet" is an honest answer that becomes the first thing to fix.

**The timing.** Now, three to six months, six to twelve, or watching. Watching is fine. A live brief with an advisor means you hear about the building when it fits, not when it is listed.

## What happens after you send it

The advisor reads it and calls within a business day with a straight answer: does this building exist in those towns at that budget? If yes, the hunt starts with owner outreach block by block, pre-offer intelligence on the property and the seller from public records, financing framed before the offer, and an offer the math supports. If no, you hear that too, with what would have to change.

Nothing in the brief commits either side. It is a tool for being specific, and specificity is what finds buildings that are not for sale yet.
