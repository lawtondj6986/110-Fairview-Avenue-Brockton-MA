A customer drops off a Civic for an oil change. The technician finds the front pads nearly gone and a seeping valve cover gasket. The service writer calls at 11:15. Voicemail. Calls again at 1:30. The customer picks up in a meeting, hears "brakes" and "$489," and says she will think about it.

She is not being cheap. She cannot see the pads. She is being asked for $489 by a voice on the phone, about a car she thought was fine. So the car leaves with 2 millimeters of pad left, and the shop loses the job twice: once today, and once when the brake shop down Route 18 does it next month.

Approval rate on recommended work is one of the biggest levers an independent shop has, and most shops run it at 40 to 50 percent. The [auto services playbook](/industries/auto-services/playbook/) sets the target at 65 percent or better. Here is how photo estimates get it there.

## Why the phone estimate fails

It fails for three reasons that have nothing to do with price.

First, timing. The estimate is ready when the technician finishes, which is rarely when the customer is free to talk. Every round of phone tag keeps a car on a lift that another car needs.

Second, evidence. "Your pads are at 2 millimeters" means nothing to most drivers. A photo of the worn pad next to a new one means everything.

Third, pressure. A live call asks for a decision right now. People under pressure default to no, and "let me think about it" is how a polite person says it.

A text with photos fixes all three. It arrives when it is ready. It shows the problem. And the customer answers when she has a minute, which in our installs is usually within the half hour.

## What a good photo estimate contains

The message itself is short:

"Hi [Name], here is what we found on the [Civic]: [link with photos]. Recommended today: [brakes, $489]. Can wait: [cabin filter]. Reply YES to approve the recommended items."

The link opens a page built for a phone. For each finding:

- Two to four photos, in focus, with the worn part clearly visible.
- A measurement where one exists: pad thickness, tread depth, battery test result.
- One plain sentence on what it means. "These pads have about 10 percent left."
- A firm price, parts and labor together.

The order matters. Safety items first, then things that will get more expensive if ignored, then maintenance. Keep it to what you would tell a family member. An estimate with eleven items reads like an upsell, even when every item is real.

## The approval math

Take a shop writing 220 repair orders a month. On about 100 of them, the technician finds additional work worth recommending. Say the average recommendation is $420.

| | Phone estimates | Photo estimates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recommendations per month | 100 | 100 |
| Approval rate | 45% | 65% |
| Jobs approved | 45 | 65 |
| Average recommended job | $420 | $420 |
| Monthly revenue from recommendations | $18,900 | $27,300 |

That is $8,400 a month from cars already in the bays. No new customers, no advertising, the same technicians. Over a year it is about $100,000.

There is a second gain that is harder to count. The playbook's target for estimate turnaround is under 2 hours for common jobs. When approvals come back by text in 20 minutes, the car stays on the lift and gets finished. Every phone-tag estimate is a car pulled off, parked, and pulled back in.

## One tap, and the record it leaves

Approval by text is faster. It is also better paperwork.

Massachusetts consumer protection rules for auto repair require the customer's authorization before work is done, and again before the bill goes meaningfully beyond what was estimated. Shops have been burned by the phone version of this for decades: the customer says he never approved the second repair, and the shop has a service writer's memory.

A text approval leaves a record with five things in it: the itemized findings, the price, the photos, the customer's reply, and the time. If there is ever a dispute, you are not arguing about who said what.

Two details to get right. The approval should name what is being approved, so "Reply YES to approve the recommended items" sits directly under the listed items and prices. And if the job grows after teardown, send a new estimate and get a new YES. Talk to your attorney once about the authorization wording on your repair orders and messages, then leave it alone.

## The "can wait" list is the trust builder

The most valuable line in the estimate is the one that makes you no money today.

When a shop tells a customer that the cabin filter can wait, or that the rear pads have another 8,000 miles, the customer learns something: this shop does not sell everything it finds. That makes the "recommended today" list believable.

It also builds the next visit. Every deferred item goes into the customer record with a date. Five months after the oil service, the reminder goes out:

"Hi [Name], the [Civic] is about due for service. Tap to pick a time this week: [link]. We will check the brakes and tires while it is in."

Now the reminder is specific to the car. The playbook's target is 70 percent of customers returning within 12 months. Deferred work, written down and followed up, is how a shop gets there.

## Making it routine in the bay

The tools are simple. The habit is the work.

- Pick a fixed photo set for every car: pads, tires, fluids, battery test, anything leaking.
- Wipe the part first. A photo of grime shows nothing.
- Put a new part in the frame beside the worn one whenever you can.
- The technician shoots. The service writer prices, orders the list, and sends.
- Confirm the mobile number at drop-off. "Best number for photo estimates and approvals?" is an intake question, asked every time.

Expect two rough weeks. Photos will be blurry and some will be skipped on busy days. Review ten estimates every Friday with the technicians for the first month. By week four it takes two to four minutes a car.

## What to do this week

1. Pull last month's repair orders and count recommendations made versus recommendations approved. That is your baseline.
2. Count how many estimates took more than two hours to get an answer.
3. Choose your fixed photo set and tape the list inside each bay.
4. Start asking for a mobile number for estimates at every drop-off.
5. Write your "recommended today" and "can wait" rules on one page so every writer sorts findings the same way.
