A salon owner in Hingham has 1,400 client phone numbers in her booking software. A vendor tells her she can text all of them a holiday special this afternoon. She can. With the same tap, she can also create a legal exposure larger than the salon's annual revenue.

Texting works. In our installs it gets response rates email never approaches, and it is the channel behind missed-call recovery, reminders, and review requests. It is also the one channel where a single message carries a statutory price tag. The rules are specific, and most vendors selling texting do not explain them.

This article is a map, not advice. For your particular business and list, talk to your attorney before the first campaign goes out.

## Two kinds of texts, two kinds of consent

The federal Telephone Consumer Protection Act treats a text as a call. The consent it demands depends on what the text is for.

**Informational texts** are about something the customer already asked for: your tech is twenty minutes out, your part arrived, your appointment is Thursday at 2. For these, the customer giving you their number for that purpose is generally enough. This is why a text-back to someone who just called you sits on firm ground when it only answers their call. The glossary entry on [TCPA compliance](/glossary/tcpa-compliance/) has the short definitions.

**Marketing texts** promote something: a seasonal special, a new service, a reminder that it has been a year since the furnace tune-up. For these, sent through the kind of automated platform every small business uses, the rule generally requires prior express written consent. "Written" can be electronic. It must include a clear statement that the customer agrees to receive marketing texts from your business at the number they give, that consent is not a condition of buying anything, and an affirmative act by the customer: a box they check, a keyword they text in.

The line between the two is where small businesses get in trouble. The appointment reminder that ends with "Ask about our spring AC special!" just became a marketing text. Keep them in separate templates, sent only to the people who consented to each.

## Capturing consent that will hold up

Consent that exists only in the owner's memory does not exist. A compliant program records, for every number, what the customer agreed to, when, and how. In practice:

- **A dedicated checkbox on every form that collects a phone number,** unchecked by default, with the disclosure next to it. Pre-checked boxes and consent buried in a terms link are the two most common failures.
- **The disclosure itself,** something like: "Check here to receive marketing texts from Fairview Electric at this number. Consent is not required to purchase. Message frequency varies. Reply STOP to cancel, HELP for help."
- **A keyword path** for in-person signups: a sign at the counter saying text SAVE to the shop's number. The inbound text is the affirmative act. Reply once confirming what they signed up for.
- **A record** of the timestamp, the form or keyword, the disclosure shown, and the number. Your platform should store this automatically. If it does not, change platforms.
- **No importing of old lists.** The 1,400 numbers in the Hingham salon's booking software were given for appointments, not marketing. Those clients need to be asked, at the next visit or by a method that is not itself marketing.

Consent attaches to a number, not a person.

## Quiet hours, and why Massachusetts is tighter

Federal telemarketing rules bar marketing calls and texts before 8 a.m. and after 9 p.m. in the recipient's local time zone. That is the floor, and it is the recipient's clock, not yours.

Massachusetts has its own telemarketing statute and Attorney General regulations, with its own do-not-call list and its own time window. As we read the Massachusetts rules, the window closes at 8 p.m., an hour earlier than federal, and the state rules reach marketing texts as well as voice calls. The state also has registration requirements that can apply to outbound solicitation. Which of those apply to your business depends on facts an article cannot see.

The practical answer is to build the program narrower than any rule requires. In our installs, marketing texts go out between 9 a.m. and 7 p.m. Eastern, never on Sundays, never on a holiday. Informational texts follow the customer's reasonable expectation: a confirmation at 8:30 p.m. for a job booked at 8:25 p.m. is fine; a review request at 10 p.m. is not.

Scheduling is a software setting. Set it once and it holds.

## STOP, HELP, and the suppression list

Every marketing text should tell the recipient how to stop, and the carriers that deliver business texts require that STOP and HELP keywords work. That is the easy part. The hard part is making the stop hold across everything you use.

A typical South Shore business texts from three places: the booking software, the review tool, and the owner's own phone. A customer who replies STOP to the review tool and gets a holiday special from the booking software the next week has a violation to point to. One suppression list, shared by every system, checked before every send. If your tools cannot share one, you have too many tools.

Recent federal rules also require honoring a revocation made by any reasonable means, not only the exact keyword. "Please stop texting me" counts. "Unsubscribe" counts. The rules give a short window, currently ten business days, to process it; a system that processes it in ten seconds is the only sane design. A single text confirming the opt-out is generally permitted. A second one is not.

Finally, register. Business texting now runs through carrier registration (10DLC), which records your business, your use case, and your sample messages. Unregistered traffic gets filtered. Registration is not a legal shield, but it signals a program built by someone who knew the rules existed.

## What getting it wrong costs

The federal statute sets damages at $500 per violating text, and up to $1,500 per text when a court finds the violation willful or knowing. There is no cap. Claims are brought as class actions, so the plaintiff's lawyer is not suing over one text. They are suing over every text sent to every number that lacked proper consent.

Run the Hingham salon's afternoon special through that math. 1,400 numbers, one text each, none with written marketing consent. At the minimum figure that is $700,000 of exposure. If the campaign went out at 8:45 p.m. and also crossed the state's time window, the willful figure is on the table: $2.1 million. The salon's annual revenue is about $900,000.

Compare the cost of doing it right. The checkbox is free. The keyword signup is free. The suppression list and the send window are settings in any competent platform. The disciplined program reaches maybe 60 percent of the list in six months instead of 100 percent in an afternoon. That is the whole price of safety: patience.

## A compliant program for the salon

Here is what we would build for the salon instead. The [salon and wellness playbook](/industries/salons-wellness/playbook/) has the longer version.

Month one: add the checkbox to the online booking form and the front-desk intake. Put the SAVE keyword sign at the counter. Every client who books or visits is asked once, the same way. In our installs roughly 60 percent say yes at the point of service, so by month four the salon has about 850 consented numbers with a timestamped record for each.

Then one marketing text a month, between 10 a.m. and 6 p.m. on a weekday, with the salon's name in the first five words and STOP in the last three. A single text to 850 consented clients with a 4 percent booking response is 34 appointments, at a $95 average ticket, about $3,200 a month from a channel that costs almost nothing to run.

[Two-way texting](/glossary/two-way-texting/) for replies, [missed-call text-back](/glossary/missed-call-text-back/) for the phone, and the review request after each visit all run on the same number and the same suppression list. One system, one list, one set of rules, every time.

## What to do this week

1. List every tool that sends a text for you, including your own phone, and ask whether they share one opt-out list.
2. Pull up every form that collects a phone number and check for an unchecked consent box with plain disclosure beside it.
3. Set the send window on every marketing template to 9 a.m. to 7 p.m. Eastern, weekdays, and turn off any campaign scheduled outside it.
4. Text STOP to your own business from a personal phone and confirm nothing else arrives afterward.
5. Ask your attorney to look at your consent language and your Massachusetts obligations once, before the first campaign, not after.
6. Add the consent capture and the suppression list to your launch plan with the [First 90 Days](/first-90-days/) plan on this site.
