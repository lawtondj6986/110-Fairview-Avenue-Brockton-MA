The ROI slide is the least trusted page in any marketing pitch, and it deserves to be. It usually starts from an assumed lift, multiplies it by an assumed value, and arrives at a number that would make the vendor's fee look like a rounding error. Here is the same arithmetic with the assumptions in the open and the fee in the open, which is the only way it means anything.

## Three numbers you already know

**Average booked job value.** What a typical job or customer is worth when it closes. A heating replacement, a driveway, a new patient's first year.

**Gross margin.** What is left after materials, labor, and subs. Many trades run thirty-five to fifty percent; practices and services often higher. Multiply the two and you have **profit per extra job**: at $2,500 a job and forty percent margin, $1,000.

**Jobs you book today.** Not strictly needed for the math, but it keeps the next number honest.

## The assumption that decides everything

**Extra booked jobs per month** from the system. Not leads: booked jobs. This is your assumption, and it should be yours, not the vendor's. Start low. Two or three a month is conservative for a business that currently answers in hours and lets quotes go quiet. If that number only works at ten, the system is being sold on hope.

Two things make a low assumption reasonable. First, the [Revenue Leak calculator](/ai-services/#leak) estimates what faster response alone recovers from the leads you already get, using your own lead count and close rate. Second, the [sample monthly report](/monthly-report/sample/) shows, with invented numbers and labeled as such, what the pipeline looked like for a fictional HVAC company by month four: eighty-seven leads, thirty-four booked, up from twelve booked before the system. Your numbers will be your numbers. The point is that the assumption should come from somewhere.

## The arithmetic

**Break-even jobs per month** = monthly retainer ÷ profit per extra job. Lead Engine's published retainer is $1,500 to $2,500 a month; at $1,000 profit per job that is 1.5 to 2.5 extra jobs a month before the retainer is covered. JobSite's $450 to $700 retainer needs about half a job. Market Lock's $3,500 to $6,000 needs 3.5 to 6, which is why it is built for established businesses with real volume.

**Payback months for the build** = build cost ÷ (extra monthly gross profit − retainer). At three extra jobs a month and $1,000 each, extra gross profit is $3,000. Lead Engine at the low end: $12,000 ÷ ($3,000 − $1,500) = 8 months. At the high end: $18,000 ÷ ($3,000 − $2,500) = 36 months. That spread is the honest picture, and it is why the written quote after a call matters: the number lands somewhere inside the range, and you should see both ends before the call.

**Year-one net** = 12 × extra monthly gross profit − build − 12 × retainer. Same inputs, Lead Engine: $36,000 − $12,000 − $18,000 = $6,000 at the low end; negative $12,000 at the high end. Market Lock at three extra jobs: negative at both ends. Move the assumption to eight jobs and Market Lock pays back in under five months at the low end.

## What the math tells you to do

If the system only pays back at the low end of the range, say so on the call. A vendor who would rather scope down than sell is the one to work with. If it does not pay back at any reasonable assumption, do not buy it; fix the response time yourself with the [90-Day Playbook](/playbook/) and come back when the volume is there.

And separate the two kinds of promise. Revenue cannot be guaranteed by anyone honest. The behavior of the system can: live by a date, answered in a time, measured against your own baseline. Those are [in writing](/guarantees/). The payback is the arithmetic above, and it is yours to run.
