A business owner finds the right building on Route 139. The rent works, the parking works, the landlord is ready. Then the building commissioner looks at the use table and says the use is allowed by special permit only.

That phrase adds a public hearing and about three to five months to the plan. It is not a no. Most well-prepared special permit applications for ordinary commercial uses are granted, usually with conditions. The ones that fail tend to fail for reasons that were visible before filing.

ICS's founder is a licensed Massachusetts real estate salesperson, not an attorney. This note describes the general process under state law. Every town's bylaw and board practice differ, so talk to a local land use attorney before you file.

## What a special permit is

Every zoning bylaw has a table of uses. For each district, a use is marked one of three ways: allowed by right, prohibited, or allowed by [special permit](/glossary/special-permit/).

The third category covers uses a town is willing to have in a district if a board can review the specifics. Common examples on the South Shore include drive-throughs, auto repair and sales, restaurants in some districts, contractor yards, daycares in some settings, self-storage, kennels, and larger buildings above a size threshold.

The governing statute is [Chapter 40A](/glossary/chapter-40a/), Section 9, of the Massachusetts General Laws. It lets a town designate a special permit granting authority: the [Zoning Board of Appeals](/glossary/zoning-board-of-appeals/), the Planning Board, or in some communities the select board or city council. A single town can split the job by type of use.

A special permit is discretionary. The board may grant, deny, or grant with conditions such as limited hours, screening, or a cap on vehicles stored outside.

## Before you file: the work that decides the outcome

The hearing is usually the shortest part. What happens in the four weeks before filing matters more.

- **Read the bylaw section for your use.** It lists the criteria the board must find, often traffic, parking, noise, lighting, drainage, and compatibility with the neighborhood. Your application should answer each one in order.
- **Meet the building commissioner and the planner.** Ask which board hears it, what else is required, and what the board has asked of similar applicants. Many towns offer an informal pre-application meeting.
- **Find out what else applies.** Site plan review, a Conservation Commission filing if wetlands are near, Board of Health sign-off, or a curb cut permit from the state on a numbered route. These can run alongside the special permit or stack after it.
- **Talk to the abutters.** They will receive notice by mail regardless. A neighbor who hears about the plan from you first reacts differently from one who learns of it from a certified letter.
- **Get a real plan.** A stamped site plan showing parking, circulation, lighting, and drainage answers most questions before they are asked.

## The statutory clock, with dates

State law sets outer limits. Towns move faster or slower inside them.

1. **Filing.** The application goes to the town clerk and the board.
2. **Notice.** The hearing is advertised in a newspaper in two successive weeks, with the first notice at least 14 days before the hearing, and mailed to abutters and other parties in interest.
3. **Hearing opens.** Generally within 65 days of filing.
4. **Decision.** Generally within 90 days after the hearing closes. Both deadlines can be extended by written agreement.
5. **Filing with the clerk.** The written decision is filed and notice is mailed.
6. **Appeal period.** Twenty days from the filing with the clerk.
7. **Recording.** After the clerk certifies that no appeal was filed, the decision is recorded at the Registry of Deeds. It generally does not take effect until then.

A worked example:

| Step | Date |
|---|---|
| Application filed | Monday, January 4, 2027 |
| Latest the hearing may open | March 10, 2027 |
| Hearing opens | Tuesday, February 9 |
| Continued hearing, board votes | Tuesday, March 9 |
| Decision filed with clerk | Thursday, March 18 |
| Appeal period ends | April 7 |
| Recorded at the Registry | About April 12 |

That is fourteen weeks with one continuance and no appeal. It is a good outcome, not a slow one.

## At the hearing

Hearings follow a pattern. The chair opens, the applicant presents, the board asks questions, the public comments, and the board deliberates.

Three things help.

**Present to the criteria.** Walk through the bylaw's findings one at a time and show how the plan meets each. Boards have to write a decision. Give them the material to write it in your favor.

**Offer conditions before they are imposed.** If the concern will be hours, propose hours. If it will be headlights into a neighbor's yard, show the fence.

**Count the board.** A special permit generally needs a supermajority. On a five-member board that is four votes. If only four members are sitting, you need all four. Asking to continue to a night with a full board is common and usually granted.

Expect at least one continuance if the board wants a traffic memo, a revised plan, or comment from another department. If the application is heading toward denial, an applicant can ask to withdraw without prejudice. That matters, because a denied application generally cannot be refiled for two years without a specific finding by the board.

## What it costs and how to protect the deal

Budget ranges vary widely, so treat these as typical and rough. Filing fees and legal advertising usually run a few hundred dollars to low four figures. The real costs are professional: a civil engineer's site plan, sometimes a traffic study, and a land use attorney. All-in, a simple change of use might cost a few thousand dollars and a new drive-through or contractor yard well into five figures.

The bigger cost is time, and who carries the building during it.

- **Tenants:** make the lease contingent on receiving the permit, with a deadline and a right to walk away. Push rent commencement to after the appeal period. The [lease clause library](/lease-clauses/) covers the language to look for.
- **Buyers:** put a permitting contingency in the purchase and sale agreement, with enough days to cover the full sequence and one continuance. Sellers often ask for a nonrefundable deposit in exchange for the time.
- **Everyone:** a special permit lapses if it is not used within a period the bylaw sets, which under state law cannot exceed three years. Many towns set less.

A special permit generally runs with the land for the approved use, not with the person, unless the board conditions it otherwise. Read the conditions before you rely on a permit granted to a prior owner.

## What to do this week

1. Look up the property's zoning district and find your use in the bylaw's table of uses.
2. Call the building commissioner to confirm the classification and which board is the granting authority.
3. Ask the planning office for the board's meeting schedule and filing deadlines for the next three months.
4. Read two recent decisions from that board on similar uses. They are public records and show what conditions to expect.
5. Get a quote from a civil engineer and a local land use attorney before you sign a lease or an offer.
6. Add the permit to your site criteria in a [Buyer Brief](/buyer-brief/), and score each candidate property's zoning risk on the [tour scorecard](/tour-scorecard/).
