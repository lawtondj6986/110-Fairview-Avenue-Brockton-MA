Hurricane season runs from June 1 to November 30, with the peak in August, September, and early October. It ends in eleven days. That makes this the right week to write down what worked and what did not, while everyone on your staff still remembers.

Most South Florida businesses have a storm plan for the building: shutters, backups, the generator. Very few have a plan for the customers. The result is the same every time. The owner decides to close on a Tuesday night, the Google profile says open on Wednesday, forty people show up or call, and the week after the storm is spent on the phone untangling a calendar by hand.

Communication is the cheap part of storm preparation. It is five messages, written once in May, sent from a phone in a few minutes. Here is the set.

## The timeline customers are on

Customers follow the forecast on roughly the same schedule you do, and their questions change by the day.

- **Five days out.** The cone includes South Florida. Customers wonder whether Thursday's appointment will happen. Nobody needs a message yet, but your team should know who decides and when.
- **Watch issued, about 48 hours out.** People are buying water and fuel. They want to know one thing from you: are you open. This is when the closure message goes.
- **Warning and landfall.** Nobody should be working. One status line on the profile and website is enough.
- **The day after.** Power and cell service are uneven. Customers want to know if you are open, and for trades, whether you can come.
- **The week after.** Rescheduling, catching up, and for many trades the busiest week of the year.

A business that sends the right message at each point looks organized. A business that goes silent on Tuesday and reappears the next Monday looks closed, and some customers find someone else in between.

## The five messages to write in May

Write these once, with blanks for dates, and keep them where you can send them from a phone.

1. **Watching.** We are open normal hours and watching the storm. If that changes, you will get a text from this number.
2. **Closing.** We are closed Wednesday and Thursday for the storm. Your appointment is on hold. We will text you new times as soon as we reopen. Stay safe.
3. **Status.** Everyone is safe. We are checking the shop and will post reopening hours by 5 p.m. tomorrow.
4. **Reopening.** We reopen Saturday at 9. Reply 1 for Saturday 10:30, 2 for Monday 2:00, 3 for Tuesday 9:00.
5. **Caught up.** We are back to normal hours. Thank you for your patience this week.

Each one is under 300 characters. Each one answers the question the customer has that day and promises the next message. That promise matters more than the wording, because it stops the phone calls.

## Four places, same hour

A closure is only communicated if it shows everywhere a customer looks. There are four places, and they need to agree.

- **The Google Business Profile.** Use special hours for the closed days. This is what Maps shows and what an AI assistant reads when someone asks if you are open.
- **The website.** One banner at the top with the dates and the reopening promise.
- **The phone.** A new voicemail greeting, or an updated script for the answering agent, with the same dates.
- **The text.** To customers with appointments or open jobs in the affected days, not to the whole list.

The whole update should take one person fifteen minutes. In the restaurant, salon, and medical playbooks for Florida the September note says the same thing in different words: same hour. See the [restaurant playbook](/industries/restaurants-food/playbook/?market=fl) for the version that covers reservations and private events.

One caution for the phone. In a storm week, calls double and staff is short. A missed-call text-back that says we are closed until Saturday, reply here and we will hold your place, keeps the customer without anyone picking up.

## Worked example: a dental practice closed three days

A family dental practice in [Coral Springs](/broward/coral-springs/) sees about 45 patients a day. A storm closes it Wednesday through Friday. That is 135 appointments.

The manual way: two front-desk staff start calling Monday. At about six minutes per patient including voicemails and callbacks, 135 patients is roughly 13 to 14 hours of phone time, spread over three days while the normal schedule is also running. Some patients are never reached. If 20 of them drift and the average visit is worth $250, that is $5,000 in the first round and more over time.

The prepared way: Tuesday at 4 p.m., message two goes to all 135. Saturday morning, message four goes out with three time options each, sorted so patients in pain or mid-treatment get the first slots. In a typical run, roughly 70 to 80 percent rebook by reply within 24 hours. The front desk calls the remaining 30 or so. Total staff time: about three hours.

The same math applies to a salon, a veterinary clinic, an auto shop, or a home-service route. The number of appointments changes. The ratio does not.

## Customers who are not here

A large share of customers in coastal Broward and Palm Beach County are seasonal, and in September they are up north watching the storm on television. For a pool service, a landscape company, an AC company, or anyone with a key or a gate code, those customers are the most anxious ones you have.

A two-line text the day after does a great deal: we came by this morning, the screen enclosure is intact, the pool is running, one palm frond down and removed. With a photo if you can. A pool company in [Lighthouse Point](/broward/lighthouse-point/) with 220 accounts, 90 of them seasonal, can send 90 of those from the truck in a day. Those customers renew without shopping.

Keep these messages to what your trade actually inspected. A pool company reports on the pool. It does not report on the roof.

## What not to send

Three lines to hold during storm season:

- **No promotions tied to the storm.** A discount text while people are boarding windows reads badly and stays in screenshots.
- **No pricing surprises.** Florida law restricts unconscionable price increases on essential goods and services during a declared state of emergency. Trades should publish their emergency rates before the season and keep to them.
- **No texting outside the rules.** Florida's telemarketing statute is stricter than the federal one, including a calling window that ends at 8 p.m. in the recipient's local time. Operational messages to existing customers are treated differently from sales messages, but the line is a legal one. Talk to your attorney once and write the answer into your plan.

Post-storm demand for roofers, tree crews, and restoration is real and legitimate. Answering fast and quoting plainly is the right way to meet it.
