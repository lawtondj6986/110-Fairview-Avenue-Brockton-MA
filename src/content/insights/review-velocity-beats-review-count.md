Two landscapers in Plymouth. The first has 412 Google reviews and a 4.6 average. He has been in business since 2004 and the reviews show it: hundreds of them, mostly good, mostly from 2017 to 2022. He got nine last year. The second has 86 reviews and a 4.8 average. He opened in 2021. He got 22 in October alone.

Search "landscaper Plymouth MA" from a phone and the second one is in the three-pack. The first is not. The first landscaper thinks this is unfair. It is not unfair. It is arithmetic, and once you see the arithmetic, you can fix it in about ninety days.

## What Maps is actually weighing

Google says plainly that local results rest on three things: relevance, distance, and prominence. Relevance is whether your profile matches the search. Distance is how far you are from the searcher or the town they typed. Prominence is how well known and well regarded the business is, and reviews are the largest piece of prominence a small business controls.

Inside prominence, Google does not treat all reviews as equal. It reads the count, the rating, and the dates, and it reads the text. A review from last Tuesday that mentions "sprinkler install in Plymouth" tells Google the business is active, in Plymouth, doing that work, right now. A review from 2019 that says "great guys" tells it something used to be true.

The default sort on a Google review list is "most relevant," and most relevant leans heavily toward recent and detailed. So the first screen a customer sees is the newest, most specific reviews. For the first landscaper, that screen shows reviews from eighteen months ago. For the second, it shows last week. The [local pack](/glossary/local-pack/) entry covers the three-pack mechanics in more detail.

## The arithmetic of a frozen average

Here is why the first landscaper cannot move his number, up or down.

His average is the sum of 412 ratings divided by 412. One new five-star review changes the average by about (5.0 minus 4.6) divided by 413, which is just under 0.001. A full year of nine reviews moves it less than 0.01. His 4.6 is, for practical purposes, a fixed fact about the past.

The second landscaper's 86 reviews mean one new five-star moves his average by about 0.002, which is still small. But his count is growing by a quarter every year, and the growth itself is the signal. Google can see that in month after month of fresh dates. His profile is a live thing. The first landscaper's is a monument.

Now flip it. A one-star review lands on each. The first landscaper's average drops by (4.6 minus 1.0) divided by 413, about 0.009. Barely visible. The second's drops by (4.8 minus 1.0) divided by 87, about 0.044, so 4.8 becomes roughly 4.76. That looks like the big total is the safer position, and for the displayed number, it is. But the second landscaper will bury that one-star below twenty new reviews within a month. The first landscaper's one-star will sit at the top of "most relevant" for a year, because nothing newer arrives to push it down. The count protects the average. Only velocity protects the first screen.

## Two Plymouth landscapers, side by side

Lay the two profiles out the way Google and a customer see them.

| Total reviews | 412 | 86 |
| Average rating | 4.6 | 4.8 |

| Newest review on first screen | 14 months ago | 3 days ago |
| Reviews mentioning a town | about 40 | about 50 |
| Owner replies | 0 | all |

Landscaper A still wins on one row, the total, and it is the row that matters least for a search happening today. Landscaper B wins on every row that tells Google and a reader what the business is like now. He is also replying to every review, which Google counts as engagement and which a reader counts as a business that pays attention.

If A simply turned on a steady ask after every completed job, his 412 would start growing by fifteen to twenty a month, and his first screen would fill with current, specific reviews inside six weeks. He would keep his monument and add the live signal. Within a quarter he would be back in the pack, because very few competitors in a South Shore town have both. ## What velocity tells the person reading

Google is one reader. The homeowner on her phone at 7 p.m. is the other, and she reads the same way.

She does not scroll to review 300. She looks at the star, then the count, then the first three or four reviews, and she checks the dates. If the newest is from early last year, she wonders whether the business is still operating, whether the owner retired, whether the crew turned over. She does not wonder those things consciously. She just calls the other one.

She also reads for her own situation. A review from last month that says "replaced our walkway in Manomet, done in two days, cleaned up everything" is a near-exact match for what she wants. It does the selling. Nothing from 2019 can do that, no matter how glowing, because 2019 is not a time she is buying in.

So velocity does two jobs at once. It tells Google the business is active, and it puts a fresh, specific, relevant testimonial in front of every prospect at the moment of decision. Twenty reviews this month is twenty new sales pitches you did not write.

## Getting to twenty a month without faking anything

The lever is not persuasion. It is ask rate, and the math is simple.

A landscaper doing 120 jobs a month who asks by hand, when he remembers, asks maybe 30 percent of customers: 36 asks. Roughly 15 percent of people asked in person follow through: five or six reviews a month. That is where most South Shore trades sit.

The same landscaper with an automated request after every closed invoice asks about 95 percent of customers: 114 asks. A text with a direct link, sent within two hours of the job, converts closer to 18 percent: about 20 reviews a month. Same customers, same work, same satisfaction. Four times the velocity, from asking everyone instead of some.

Three rules keep it clean. Ask everyone the same way; selecting the happy ones is [review gating](/glossary/review-gating/) and it violates Google's policy. Never pay, discount, or trade for a review. Never write one, ask staff or family to write one, or buy them. The penalties for faking now include federal civil fines, and the reputational cost of being caught is worse. ## What to do this week

1. Open your profile and count your reviews from the last 90 days; if it is under ten, velocity is your problem, not count.
2. Look up your top three competitors in your category and town and count theirs for the same window.
3. Reply to every unanswered review, oldest first, in two or three plain sentences each.
4. Pick the moment in your job flow when the customer is happiest (usually invoice paid) and attach a review request to it, for every customer, automatically.
5. Write the request in your own voice with a direct link, under 160 characters, and send a test to yourself.
6. Map the first 90 days of a review system against your calendar with the [First 90 Days](/first-90-days/) plan on this site.
