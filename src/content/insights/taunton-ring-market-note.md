Draw a circle around Taunton with a twelve-mile radius and you capture five towns that the rest of the South Shore tends to think of separately: Taunton the city, Raynham the retail town, Norton the college and yard town, Mansfield the industrial crossroads, and Lakeville the lakes and cranberry town at the Middleborough line. The buyers who need square footage do not think of them separately. They think of them as one ring, tied together by Routes 24, 44, 140, and 138 and by I-495.

That is how this note reads them. The ring holds more industrial and flex inventory than anywhere else in southeastern Massachusetts, two long retail roads, a downtown rebuilding, and the yard country a contractor cannot find closer to Boston at any price. Prices run below Brockton for comparable product, and inventory runs deeper.

The numbers are typical bands from our reads, not named transactions.

## The ring and its roads

Each highway does a different job, and a buyer should know which one their building lives on.

[Taunton](/south-shore/taunton/) is the center: the Myles Standish Industrial Park at Route 495, big-box and strip retail along Route 44 and Route 140, auto dealers and repair on Broadway and Route 138, downtown blocks around the Green, and medical office around the hospital. [Raynham](/south-shore/raynham/) is a corridor town: the Route 44 plazas, Broadway's restaurants and auto, the Raynham Woods commerce park near Route 24, and the former dog track site awaiting its next life. [Norton](/south-shore/norton/) is scattered along Routes 123 and 140: strip retail, contractor shops and yards, flex near the I-495 ramp, and a college in the middle. [Mansfield](/south-shore/mansfield/) is where the region keeps its inventory: Cabot Business Park and the parks along I-495, the Route 140 plaza corridor, and a downtown rebuilt around the train. [Lakeville](/south-shore/lakeville/) is light on commercial stock: service and retail on Routes 18 and 105, office and medical near the hospital campus redevelopment, flex near the I-495 ramp, and a commuter rail station at the Middleborough line.

The corridor guides for [Route 140](/corridors/route-140/), [Route 44](/corridors/route-44/), [Route 138](/corridors/route-138/), and [Route 24](/corridors/route-24/) cover who does business on each. The short version: 140 is the industrial-park road, 44 is the regional retail road, 138 is the auto and trades road, and 24 is how the ring reaches Brockton and Boston.

## The industrial core: parks, ramps, and the owner-user's math

The ring's defining product is the clear-span building near a ramp. Distribution, manufacturing, food production, fleet, and the contractors who outgrew their Brockton yard all want the same thing: 10,000 to 50,000 square feet, docks, a yard, and a highway within five minutes.

A worked example. A 20,000 square foot warehouse built in the 1980s, two docks and one drive-in door, modest clear height, on two acres near a Route 495 interchange. In our reads this product rents in a band of roughly $9 to $14 per foot triple net and sells at roughly $110 to $160 per foot, with clear height, power, and dock count setting the spread. Call it $11 per foot to rent: $220,000 a year, plus taxes and insurance passed through.

To buy, call it $135 per foot, or $2.7 million. Under a typical SBA 504 structure the owner-user puts down 10 percent, about $270,000, and finances $2.43 million across a bank first mortgage and a CDC debenture. At a blended rate of roughly 7 percent over 25 years, the payment is about $17,200 a month, or $206,000 a year. Taxes and insurance are the same either way.

Owning costs about what renting costs, before depreciation and before the owner controls the yard, the expansion, and the exit. That is why the product does not wait for a listing. Lender terms and eligibility vary by business, so talk to your CPA and lender before you set the number.

## The retail roads: Route 44 and Broadway

Route 44 through Raynham and into Taunton is the ring's shopping trip. Big-box anchors, plazas, pads, and quick service, built for the car and competing with the plaza across the street. Broadway, which is Route 138, is the auto road: dealers, repair, body, tires, and the restaurants that feed the traffic.

What trades on 44 is the pad and the inline unit with full access. A worked example. A one-acre pad on Route 44 with a signalized entrance and a left-turn lane, suited to a drive-through user, supports a ground lease in our reads of roughly $100,000 to $160,000 a year triple net, depending on the exact location and the co-tenancy of the plaza behind it. The same acre two hundred yards down the road with right-in, right-out access and no signal can lose a quarter or more of that, because the customer heading the other way will not make the U-turn. The frontage is the asset; the building is a fixture on it.

What sits on 44 is second-generation big-box space when an anchor leaves: too large for local operators, too dated for national ones, waiting until a landlord subdivides it or a medical, fitness, or indoor recreation user takes the box at a rent well below the original. That repricing is where a well-advised tenant finds the best deal on the road.

On Broadway, the product is the auto building with a history. Environmental diligence decides the deal, and a Phase I is read before any offer.

## Norton and Lakeville: the yards and the edges

The ring's outer towns are where the trades buy.

Norton's commercial buildings are strung along Routes 123 and 140: strip retail, contractor shops and yards, a few plazas near the center, and flex near the I-495 ramp. It is practical and reasonably priced for a town between Mansfield's parks and Taunton's city. A typical Norton yard in our reads, two to three acres with a 3,000 to 4,000 square foot shop and a fenced lot, sits in a band of roughly $700,000 to $1 million, with highway access the main thing moving the number inside that band. A contractor with a Norton yard and a phone that gets answered covers five towns from one address.

Lakeville is lighter. The town is mostly lakes, bogs, and single-family lots, which keeps commercial supply tight and makes a well-located building on Route 18 valuable to whoever gets to it first. Contractor yards on the rural roads and flex near the I-495 ramp are the owner-user products. The hospital campus redevelopment and the commuter rail station at the Middleborough line are the two things to watch, and neither should be underwritten on a schedule.

Zoning in both towns is lot-by-lot and the wetlands question is real near every pond and bog. Ask the conservation commission early.

## What is repricing inside the ring

Three things are moving the ring's numbers, by our read:

- **Taunton's downtown.** The blocks around the Green are being rebuilt and re-tenanted, and the gap between a downtown mixed-use building's current rents and what the next tenants will pay is where patient investors are working. Pricing below Brockton for comparable product makes the entry realistic.
- **Raynham's large sites.** The former dog track site and the land near the Route 24 and Route 44 interchange are the ring's biggest redevelopment stories. They will set the next decade's retail and flex rents nearby. Watch them; do not underwrite on them.
- **Mansfield's park rents.** Newer and higher-clear product along I-495 has pulled the top of the industrial band up, which makes the older buildings in Taunton and Raynham look cheaper by comparison and keeps owner-users hunting there.

The hedges: each of the five towns has its own zoning, its own sewer and septic map, its own signage bylaw, and its own view of a change of use. Taunton is a city with a city's permitting; the four towns around it run through town meeting. Treat every parcel as its own jurisdiction.

## What to do this week

1. Write down the three numbers that define your building: square feet, dock and door count, and minutes to a ramp. Everything else is negotiable.
2. Tour the ring as one market, in one week: Myles Standish, Raynham Woods, the Mansfield parks, and the Norton and Lakeville ramps.
3. Pull assessor records on the ten buildings that fit and note which owners have held twenty years or more; that is the off-market list.
4. If you are on Route 44 or Broadway, confirm the access permit and the signage bylaw before you price the frontage.
5. Have a lender frame the SBA 504 structure now, so the first owner who says yes gets a real offer the same week.
