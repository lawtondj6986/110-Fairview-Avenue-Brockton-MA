A woman who spends her winters in a community off Yamato Road wakes up on a Monday in January with a crown that came loose over the weekend. Her dentist is in New Jersey. She searches, picks three practices in Boca Raton with good reviews, and calls the first one at 12:20. It rings to voicemail, because the front desk is at lunch. She calls the second. Someone answers, and she has a dentist for the next ten winters.

The first practice never knows she called. Its schedule is full anyway, so nothing feels wrong.

That is the new-patient problem in season. Demand is so strong from November through March that a practice can miss a third of its new-patient calls and still feel busy. The misses are invisible, and they are the most valuable calls of the year.

## Who is calling in season

[Boca Raton](/palm-beach/boca-raton/) sets a high bar: an affluent residential base, a dense daytime population on the Glades Road and Yamato Road office corridors, and patients who choose on polish and response. In season three groups call at once.

- **Returning seasonal residents** who need a local provider, or who have one and want in this week. They often need records moved from a northern office and ask about that first.
- **Year-round residents after the insurance reset.** In January deductibles restart and new plans begin. People who changed plans need a practice that takes the new one.
- **Year-end benefit users in November and December,** who have a few weeks to use what they have paid for.

The medical and dental calendar for Florida puts it simply: new-patient calls peak in January, seasonal patients want treatment finished before they leave in March, and the schedule opens up in April. ## Count the misses first

Before changing anything, look at the call log. Nearly every modern phone system keeps one. Pull one full week in season and count three numbers: inbound calls, calls answered by a person, and calls from numbers not already in your patient system.

A worked example for a four-provider dental practice, with typical figures:

- 520 inbound calls in a January week.
- 385 answered live. That leaves 135 missed, about 26 percent.
- Of the 135, about 50 are from numbers not in the patient system. Some are vendors and spam. Assume 30 are real prospective patients.
- Of those 30, perhaps 8 leave a voicemail and get a callback. The other 22 simply call the next practice.

Now the value. A new dental patient is commonly worth $1,000 to $3,000 in the first year and several times that over the relationship, more for an implant or cosmetic case. At a cautious $1,500, 22 lost patients a week is $33,000 in first-year production. Over a twelve-week peak it is close to $400,000.

Your numbers will differ. The point is that nobody on staff will see them unless someone counts. The [Payback Calculator](/payback/) does the arithmetic once you have the three figures.

## When the calls are missed

The misses are not random. They fall in four predictable windows.

- **12 to 2.** Patients call on their lunch break, which is also the front desk's.
- **The first 45 minutes of the day.** The desk is checking in the morning's patients while the phone rings.
- **After 5 and on weekends.** A large share of working adults on the office corridors can only call then.
- **Any time the desk is with a patient in person.** A good front desk gives the person at the counter full attention. The phone loses.

More staff is the obvious answer and only a partial one. A second person helps the morning rush and does nothing at 7 p.m. The practical fix is layered: stagger lunches so the phone is always covered, then put a system behind the staff for the moments when both lines are busy and the hours when the office is closed.

## What the caller needs in the first minute

A prospective patient is trying to answer four questions. Whoever answers them first usually gets the appointment.

1. **Are you taking new patients?** Yes or no, and how soon.
2. **Do you take my plan?** This is the top question in South Florida, where Medicare, Medicare Advantage plans, and employer PPOs all mix in the same waiting room. A caller wants a clear answer for the plan they name, or an honest we will verify and text you by 3 p.m.
3. **Where are you and what are the hours?** Near Glades and the Turnpike, east on Federal Highway, by the office parks on Congress Avenue. Seasonal residents navigate by landmarks.
4. **When can I come in?** Two specific times beat we will call you back.

For returning seasonal patients, add a fifth: can you get my records? A front desk with a ready answer, here is the release form, we request them the same day, removes the last reason to keep looking.

Put those answers in writing on the website and the Google Business Profile as well. Many patients, and the AI assistants they ask, check before they call.

## Where automation helps and where it stops

A practice is not a pizza shop, and the system should be narrower than what a contractor would use.

What it should do:

- Text back a missed call within a minute with a neutral message and an offer to schedule.
- Answer administrative questions after hours: location, hours, new-patient availability, the list of plans accepted.
- Book or request an appointment and collect name, date of birth, and insurer through a secure intake form, not through open text messages.
- Hand the front desk a clean list each morning of who called, what they asked, and what was booked.

What it should not do:

- Give medical advice of any kind.
- Discuss symptoms or treatment by text.
- Hold an emergency. Anyone describing one is told to call 911, and urgent clinical questions go to the on-call provider.

Patient privacy rules govern how health information is handled, and vendors that touch it need the right agreements in place. Set this up with your compliance advisor or healthcare attorney, once, before launch. The same narrow design works for practices on Linton Boulevard in [Delray Beach](/palm-beach/delray-beach/) and in the family suburbs across the county line, such as [Coral Springs](/broward/coral-springs/).

## Season has an end date

The other half of the job is using the peak to prepare for April.

Seasonal patients want treatment finished before they leave. A treatment plan presented in February with a March completion date gets accepted more often than an open-ended one. Before they go, book the November visit. A recall appointment already on the calendar is the one that happens.

Then in April and May, when the schedule opens, run recall on year-round patients and ask for reviews from the winter's happy ones. A practice that collects 40 specific reviews in the spring answers the question every January caller is really asking, which is whether the people who went there were glad they did.
