A university's public record is not kept in one place. It is spread across the institution's own website, federal and state agencies, and the courts. This lesson is a map of five families of document and what each can tell you. Detailed search instructions are in the guides linked from each section.

## What you will learn

- The five families of document that make up most of a university's public record
- Who produces each one, and where the official copy is kept
- What each family can and cannot tell you
- How to record a search so that a failed search is also useful

## Documents the university must publish or file

Annual security reports. A college or university that takes part in federal student aid programs must publish an [annual security report](/glossary/annual-security-report/) each year under the [Clery Act](/law/clery-act/). It contains campus crime statistics for the three most recent calendar years and a set of required policy statements. The university posts it, usually on a campus police or public safety page, and the U.S. Department of Education publishes the reported statistics in an online database. The report counts crimes reported to certain officials within defined geographic areas. It is not a count of everything that happened to students. The [reading guide](/guides/read-a-campus-security-report/) explains the categories.

Tax filings. Most private nonprofit universities file a [Form 990](/law/form-990/) with the Internal Revenue Service each year, and the form is open to public inspection. It reports revenue, expenses, the highest-paid people, governance practices and, on its schedules, matters such as transactions with insiders. Public universities, as arms of state government, generally do not file one, but the separate foundations that raise money for them usually do. The public copy generally does not name donors. Copies are available from the IRS's online search tool for tax-exempt organizations and from the organization itself on request. See the guides on [finding nonprofit filings](/guides/method-finding-nonprofit-filings/) and on [reading a Form 990](/guides/read-a-form-990/).

Other required disclosures exist, including federal reports of certain foreign gifts and contracts, explained in the [Section 117 explainer](/law/higher-education-act-section-117/). The reporting rules have been revised over time, so check the current text.

## Documents made by courts and agencies

Court dockets. When a university sues or is sued, the court keeps a [docket](/glossary/docket/), a dated list of everything filed in the case, with the filings themselves. Federal dockets are available through the federal courts' electronic records system, which charges fees for most documents. State court access varies widely, from full online access to paper files at a clerk's counter. A docket shows who claimed what, what the court decided, and how the case ended. Many filings record allegations only, a point Lesson 6 takes up. Start with the guides on [finding court records](/guides/method-finding-court-records/) and [reading a docket](/guides/read-a-court-docket/).

Agency findings. Federal agencies that oversee universities produce their own records. The Department of Education's civil-rights office issues letters and resolution agreements at the close of investigations. The Department also reviews compliance with campus safety law. Research regulators publish some determinations. Many of these appear in agency online reading rooms, and others can be requested under the [Freedom of Information Act](/law/freedom-of-information-act/). State attorneys general, who oversee charities, sometimes publish reports or settlements. The guide on [federal agency records](/guides/method-federal-agency-records-and-foia-reading-rooms/) explains where to look.

## Documents the university chooses to publish

Institutional reports form the largest and most uneven family. It includes audited financial statements, annual fundraising reports, fact-finding reports released after a controversy, climate survey results, policy manuals, faculty handbooks, governing board minutes where they are released, and official statements. The student newspaper and the university archives are not official publications in the same sense, but they preserve much of the record. See the guide on [using the student newspaper](/guides/student-newspaper-as-a-record/).

These documents are A1 as records of what the institution said. They are written by the institution, within a scope it chose, and they can be revised or removed. Two habits follow. Save a dated copy of anything you rely on, and check a web archive for earlier versions. The guide on [web archives and link rot](/guides/method-web-archives-and-link-rot/) explains how.

## Record the search, not only the result

A search that finds nothing is still information, provided you wrote down what you did. For every search, note the place searched, the terms used, the date range covered and the date of the search. Without that note, "I found nothing" cannot be checked or repeated, and it cannot support the nothing-on-record label from Lesson 2.

Note the limits of each place as well. An online court index may begin in a certain year. An agency reading room holds only what the agency chose to post. A university website shows the current version of a policy, not the one in force five years ago.

## Exercise

Build a document inventory for your chosen university.

1. Find the most recent annual security report. Record its title, the calendar years its statistics cover, and where it is posted.
2. Find the most recent Form 990 for the university, or for its affiliated foundation if it is a public university. Record the fiscal year covered and the filing organization's exact legal name.
3. Search one court system for cases in which the university is a party. Record the court, the search terms, the date range and the number of results. Open one docket and record the case number and the date and title of the first and latest entries.
4. Search one federal agency's online reading room or published case list for the university's name. Record what you searched and what you found, including nothing.
5. Find one report the university published about itself in the last five years. Record its author, its stated scope and its date.
6. Save a copy of each document and record the retrieval date.

## Check your work

The exercise was done well if your inventory has an entry for all five families, including the ones where the entry is a recorded search with no result.

Common mistakes:

- Searching under the everyday name only. Universities often litigate and file taxes under a formal corporate name, and may have several related legal entities.
- Confusing the year a report was published with the years it covers. A security report published this year covers earlier calendar years. A Form 990 covers a fiscal year that may not match the calendar.
- Taking a news article's description of a filing in place of the filing.
- Recording "no cases found" without the court and date range. A search of one court says nothing about others.
- Relying on an unofficial copy when the official one is available.
