A professor's lab is funded from several streams that run through different offices under different
rules. A federal grant, a company contract, a donor's gift and a start-up package can all pay for
the same people and equipment, yet each is reviewed, accounted for and reported differently. When a
reader asks whether a donor's money influenced a lab, the first task is to work out which channel
the money used. This primer describes the common channels at U.S. research universities. Terms and
thresholds vary by institution and by funder.

## The basic distinction

Universities sort incoming money into categories that determine which office handles it. The central
distinction is between a gift and sponsored funding, explained in the glossary entry on [gifts,
grants and sponsored research](/glossary/gift-grant-sponsored-research/).

A [gift](/glossary/gift/) is a voluntary transfer with no expectation of specific deliverables,
intellectual property rights or detailed reporting beyond stewardship. It is handled by the
development office. Sponsored funding, which includes grants and contracts, comes with a defined
scope of work, a budget, reporting requirements and terms. It is handled by the [sponsored programs
office](/glossary/sponsored-programs-office/). The label matters. A payment the sponsor calls a gift
but that comes with deliverables may be reclassified as sponsored research, and the reverse also
occurs. Institutions write policies to decide borderline cases, and those policies differ.

## Federal and other grants

Federal grants, such as those from the National Institutes of Health or the National Science
Foundation, are the largest source of research funding at many universities. The professor, as
[principal investigator](/glossary/principal-investigator/), writes a proposal. Before submission
the sponsored programs office reviews the budget and certifies compliance on the institution's
behalf, because the award is legally made to the university, not to the professor. After peer
review, an award arrives with terms the university must follow.

Federal awards are subject to government-wide cost principles and administrative rules for grants
(commonly called the Uniform Guidance), plus each agency's own policies. They require financial
reports, progress reports, effort certification for people paid from the award, and compliance with
rules on human subjects, animals, conflicts of interest and research integrity. The [PHS conflict of
interest rules](/law/phs-financial-conflict-of-interest-rules/) are one example. Foundation and
nonprofit grants work similarly but follow each funder's own terms.

Grants also carry [indirect costs](/glossary/indirect-costs/), paid to the university for shared
infrastructure on top of the project's direct costs. Federal indirect cost rates are negotiated with
the government. Private funders often cap them or pay none.

## Industry-sponsored research

A company that wants work done on a particular question usually funds it through a sponsored
research agreement, a contract negotiated by the sponsored programs office or a related
industry-contracts office. These agreements address scope, budget, publication rights,
confidentiality and ownership or licensing of resulting inventions. University policies generally
protect the right to publish, often allowing a sponsor a short review period to identify patentable
material or confidential information. Federal law governs inventions from federally funded work, as
described on the [Bayh-Dole Act page](/law/bayh-dole-act/), and university patent policy usually
assigns ownership of inventions to the institution.

Companies also provide materials, data or equipment under a [material transfer
agreement](/glossary/material-transfer-agreement/) or similar contract. These can carry restrictions
even when no money changes hands.

## Gifts to a lab or a professor

Donors can give in support of a specific lab, research area or professor. Legally the gift is to the
university, which holds the money in a fund designated for that purpose. Gifts can be current-use,
spent over a few years, or endowed, with the annual payout supporting the work indefinitely. An
[endowed chair](/glossary/endowed-chair/) is a common endowed form whose payout supports the
holder's salary and sometimes research.

Gift funds are prized because they are flexible. They often have no fixed project, no progress
reports to an agency, and fewer restrictions on what can be bought. That flexibility is also why
gift funds receive less routine oversight than grants. Spending is governed by the gift's terms, the
institution's accounting rules and the department's approval chain, but there is usually no external
reviewer. The [primer on gift acceptance](/guides/primer-gift-acceptance-and-vetting/) explains why
gifts below a review threshold may not be examined individually.

## Discretionary and institutional funds

Many faculty members also control money that is neither a grant nor a single donor's gift. Common
sources include:

- Start-up packages offered to new faculty, which pay for equipment, staff and supplies for the
  first years.
- Retention packages offered to keep faculty who have outside offers.
- Income from an endowed chair's payout.
- Internal seed grants and pilot funds awarded by schools or centers.
- Residual balances left over from completed fixed-price contracts, which some institutions allow to
  be moved into a discretionary account.
- Recharge income from services the lab sells to other groups.
- Accounts funded by small gifts, honoraria or other income the institution permits to be directed
  to the lab.

These accounts are often described informally as discretionary funds. The rules on what they may pay
for vary widely by institution and by account type. Their balances may be known only to the
professor, a department administrator and the school's finance staff.

## How the money is tracked

Each funding source is assigned its own account or fund number in the university's financial system.
Salaries, stipends and purchases are charged to specific accounts. A single person can be paid
partly from a grant, partly from a gift fund and partly from departmental money, with the split
recorded in payroll distribution. Department administrators reconcile these accounts. Internal
auditors review samples. Federally funded research is also covered by the institution's annual
single audit.

In a university with [decentralized budgeting](/glossary/decentralized-budgeting/), schools hold
most of the money and make most spending decisions, so the paper trail for a lab's spending usually
sits at the school level rather than centrally.

## Where influence can enter

Each channel has a different exposure. Grants are peer reviewed and audited, so a single funder
rarely shapes the work invisibly, though industry contracts can shape topics and publication timing.
Gifts can shape which questions get pursued, which students and staff are hired, and who is invited
into the lab, with limited review. Discretionary funds can combine money from several sources, which
makes tracing a particular donor's influence difficult. Financial interests of the professor, such
as consulting or equity in a company, are a separate matter covered by [research conflict of
interest](/glossary/research-conflict-of-interest/) rules and [research financial
disclosure](/glossary/research-financial-disclosure/).

## A worked hypothetical

A professor runs a lab funded by two federal grants, a sponsored research agreement with a company,
and a gift fund established by a donor. A new research assistant is hired. Half the assistant's
salary is charged to one federal grant, because they work on that project. The other half is charged
to the gift fund.

The donor visits the lab twice a year and sometimes suggests topics. Because the gift agreement
names only "support for research in the field," the suggestions carry no formal weight, but the
professor tends to pursue them. The company contract allows the sponsor to review manuscripts before
submission. When the contract ends, a small unspent balance is moved to the professor's
discretionary account, which later pays for a conference trip that includes a meeting with the
donor.

Each step is permitted. The full picture appears only by reading the grant records, the contract,
the gift agreement, the payroll distribution and the discretionary account together.

## What the documents look like

- Grant proposals and award notices: held by the sponsored programs office. Federal award data is
  public through federal spending databases and agency award search tools, though proposals
  themselves usually are not.
- Progress and financial reports to the funder: internal and funder-held, sometimes obtainable
  through public records requests to a federal agency (see the [FOIA
  page](/law/freedom-of-information-act/)).
- Sponsored research agreements and material transfer agreements: internal contracts.
- Gift agreements and fund descriptions: internal, held by development and finance.
- Start-up and retention letters: part of the faculty member's personnel file.
- Account ledgers and payroll distribution reports: internal financial records.
- Conflict of interest disclosures: internal, with limited public disclosure required in some
  federal contexts.
- Audited financial statements and single audit reports: public, but aggregated far above the level
  of a single lab.

## Questions worth asking

1. Which accounts paid for this person, project or purchase, and under which category of funding?
2. Was a payment treated as a gift or as sponsored research, and who made that classification?
3. What do the terms of each source say about scope, publication and reporting?
4. Does the professor control discretionary funds, and what money flows into them?
5. Did a donor or sponsor have access to the lab, its people or its results beyond what the
   agreement provides?
6. Does the professor have a financial interest related to any sponsor, and was it disclosed and
   managed?
7. At what level of the institution are these records held?
