Members and committees of the U.S. Congress send letters to universities, agencies and companies
asking for information. These letters are usually released to the press on the day they are sent.
They can be valuable sources, particularly when they attach documents, and they are also political
communications. An oversight letter begins an inquiry. It does not conclude one.

## Who sent it

Read the letterhead and every signature.

- A committee or subcommittee chair. The chair controls the committee's agenda and, under most
  committees' rules, its power to issue subpoenas. A chair's letter has the most force behind it.
- The ranking member. This is the senior member of the minority party on the committee. A ranking
  member can ask questions and attract attention but generally cannot compel answers alone.
- Chair and ranking member together. A bipartisan letter signals broader support and is harder for a
  recipient to treat as partisan.
- Individual members, on their own letterhead. These carry no committee authority. They are requests
  from legislators whose goodwill the recipient may value.

Check which committee it is and whether the subject falls within its jurisdiction. Letters usually
say why it does.

## What authority is behind it

- A letter is a request. Compliance is voluntary in the legal sense, though recipients often comply
  to avoid escalation.
- A subpoena is a formal demand, issued under a committee's rules. It is a separate document. A
  letter may mention the possibility of one.
- A preservation request asks the recipient to keep records. It signals that document demands may
  follow.
- Some letters cite a statute that requires reporting to Congress or to an agency. In that case,
  read the statute too.

Do not describe a letter as a subpoena, or an inquiry as an investigation by Congress as a whole. It
is an inquiry by the signers.

## The usual structure

1. Opening paragraph. Who is writing, and the subject.
2. Background. A narrative of the facts as the signers understand them, with footnotes.
3. Statement of interest or jurisdiction. Why the committee is asking, often tied to federal funding
   or to possible legislation.
4. Requests. A numbered list of documents and questions, with a time period.
5. Deadline and instructions for delivery, sometimes with an attachment defining terms such as
   "document" and "communication."
6. Signatures and a list of people copied.

## Reading the background section

This is the part most often quoted, and the part needing the most care.

- Check every footnote. Sources range from primary documents (records obtained by the committee,
  court filings, official reports) to news articles and earlier letters. A claim is only as strong
  as its source.
- Distinguish what the letter says is established from what it says is alleged or reported. Look for
  "according to," "reportedly" and "it appears."
- Remember the genre. The background is written to justify the request and to be read by the press.
  It selects and emphasizes.
- Note any documents the committee released with the letter. Read those directly. They are often the
  most valuable part.

A statement in the background section is the signers' assertion. It becomes evidence only to the
extent the cited source supports it.

## Reading the requests

- Questions are not findings. "Provide all communications regarding gifts from this donor"
  establishes that the committee wants to know, not that such communications exist.
- The scope of the requests shows what the signers think may be there. That can be useful as a guide
  to their theory.
- Note the time period requested. It often reflects what the committee already knows.

## What happens next

- The recipient may answer in full, in part, or with a short letter and a promise of rolling
  production. Responses are often not made public. Sometimes the recipient or the committee releases
  them.
- The committee may send follow-up letters, hold a hearing, conduct transcribed interviews, issue a
  subpoena, or publish a staff report.
- Many inquiries produce no public conclusion. They end with a change in committee leadership or
  simply stop.

## How to follow one

1. Save the letter and note its date, signers and deadline.
2. Check the committee's website, and the minority's separate site, for releases.
3. Check the recipient's website and news coverage for a response.
4. Look for hearings on the committee calendar and for published transcripts.

## What an oversight letter can tell you

- That named legislators asked a named recipient specific questions on a specific date.
- What those legislators believe or suspect, and what sources they cite.
- Sometimes, primary documents released with it.
- What information may later become public.

## What it cannot tell you

- That the assertions in the background section are true.
- That the recipient did anything wrong.
- What the recipient's answer was or will be.
- That any formal finding, penalty or legislation will follow.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting the letter's narrative as fact without checking its footnotes.
- Describing a single member's letter as action by a committee or by Congress.
- Describing a request as a subpoena.
- Reporting the letter and never checking whether a response was given.
- Ignoring the political setting. Party control, timing and the signers' stated priorities shape
  what is asked. That setting does not make a letter wrong, and it does not make it right.

## How to cite one

Name the signers and their roles, give the date, and attribute: "the letter states," "the senators
wrote," "the committee asked." Where the letter relies on a primary document, cite the document.
This site's /sources/ page lists oversight materials separately from findings for this reason.
