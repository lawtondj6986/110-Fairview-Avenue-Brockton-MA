A claim ledger is a table with one row for every factual claim you publish or intend to publish. Each row records the claim's exact wording, its evidence state, the grade of its source, the source itself and the date you last checked the source. It is dull to keep and it prevents most of the errors that damage careful work: claims that drift from their sources, claims nobody can trace, and corrections that fix one page but miss three others.

## What counts as a claim

A claim is a single statement of fact that could be shown to be true or false. "The center received a gift in a given year" is one claim. "The center received a gift from a named donor, which funded two positions" is three: the gift, the donor, and the use of funds. Split compound sentences until each row could be checked against one passage in one document.

Opinions, definitions and descriptions of your own method are not claims in this sense. Statements of absence are. "The report does not mention the second gift" is a checkable claim about a document and belongs in the ledger.

## The columns

A minimal ledger has these columns.

- ID. A short stable identifier, such as C-014. Never reuse an ID.
- Wording. The claim exactly as published, or as you intend to publish it.
- State. Stated intent, documented activity, verified implementation, or nothing on record.
- Grade. A1, A2, B1 or B2, describing the source. Definitions are at `/methodology/`.
- Source. Enough detail for a stranger to find the passage: title, author or issuing body, date, page or paragraph, and a link where a stable one exists.
- Date checked. The date you personally opened the source and compared it with the wording.

Useful additions are a column for where the claim appears in your work, a column for any response or denial on record, and a notes column for conflicts between sources. A fill-in row is in the Templates series under `/guides/`.

## How to fill a row

1. Write the wording first, as a plain sentence.
2. Open the source itself. Find the passage. Copy the page or paragraph reference.
3. Compare the wording to the passage, verb by verb. If the passage says "proposed" and your wording says "arranged", change your wording.
4. Assign the state from what the passage shows, not from what you believe happened.
5. Assign the grade from the kind of source, and write a few words explaining why.
6. Enter today's date as the date checked.

If you did not open the source, and are working from a summary of it, leave the date blank and mark the row "pending check". A blank date is honest. A date entered from memory is not.

## Rules that keep the ledger useful

One source per row is the ideal. If a claim needs two sources to stand, it is often two claims. Where several sources support the same claim, list the strongest first and note the others.

The lower grade governs. If any essential part of the claim rests on a B2 source, the row is B2.

Wording and ledger must match. When you edit a sentence in the published text, edit the row in the same sitting. When the row changes, search the text for every use of that claim.

Nothing is deleted. When a claim is withdrawn, mark the row withdrawn, with the date and reason. The history is part of the record and supports any correction notice you publish.

Rows about named living people get extra care. Set a minimum grade for them before you start. This site requires A1 or A2. Record any denial or response in the row so it travels with the claim.

## Reviewing the ledger

Set a schedule for rechecking. Sources change: reports are revised, articles are corrected, pages move. On each review:

- open the source for every row older than your chosen interval, and update the date checked;
- look for rows marked pending and either check them or reduce the confidence of the published wording;
- sort by grade and read every B2 row, asking whether the claim needs to be published at all;
- sort by state and read every "verified implementation" row, asking what official finding or independent result supports that label.

## What the ledger shows you

Patterns in the ledger are informative. If most rows about one subject are stated intent, your text should not read as though events occurred. If one source carries a large share of rows, your work depends heavily on that source, and its limits are your limits. If many rows share one date checked, they were probably checked in a batch, which is fine, but spot-check a few.

## Limits

A ledger records what you checked and when. It does not make a weak source strong, and it does not prove a claim. It also covers only the claims you thought to enter. Implications created by placing two true claims side by side are not rows, and they need a separate read of the finished text. The ledger's value is narrower and practical: any reader, or you a year from now, can pick a sentence and find out in a minute what it rests on.
