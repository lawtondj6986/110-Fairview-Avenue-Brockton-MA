In most doctoral programs, one faculty member shapes a student's research, funding, degree progress
and future references. Most advisers use that position well. The structure still creates dependence,
and a good program builds safeguards that do not rely on any one person's character.

## Why the adviser's position is unusual

An adviser often controls, at the same time:

- The student's stipend and research costs, where these come from the adviser's grants.
- The research topic and access to data, equipment and collaborators.
- The judgment of when the work is sufficient to graduate.
- Authorship and credit on publications.
- Letters of recommendation, which matter for years afterward.
- For international students, continued enrollment, on which visa status depends.

Fields are small. A student may reasonably fear that a complaint will follow them. This makes
students slow to report problems of any kind, from neglect to harassment, and it is why formal
complaint numbers alone say little about a program.

## Structural features that reduce the risk

Look for these in the program handbook, on the department website and in the offer letter.

### Funding not held by one person

- A funding guarantee from the program or school for a stated number of years, regardless of
  adviser.
- Bridge funding when an adviser's grant ends or the relationship breaks down.
- Fellowships and training grants that attach to the student.

### More than one faculty member involved

- A thesis committee formed early, meeting at least annually, with time for the student to speak
  with the committee without the adviser present.
- A co-adviser or designated second mentor.
- Lab rotations in the first year, where the field uses them, so that students choose with some
  knowledge.
- A director of graduate studies who is not the student's adviser and who meets students regularly.

### A workable exit

- A written process for changing advisers that does not require the current adviser's consent.
- Assurance that a student who changes labs keeps funding during the transition.
- Clear rules on what happens to data and partially completed work.

### Written expectations

- Annual written progress reviews seen by someone beyond the adviser.
- A stated policy on authorship.
- Stated norms on working hours, leave and vacation.
- Requirements for graduation that are set by the program, not solely by the adviser.

### Places to go

- An ombuds office: a confidential, informal and neutral resource that does not report to the
  department.
- A clear description of which staff are confidential and which must pass on reports.
- A policy on relationships between faculty and students they supervise. Many institutions prohibit
  them.
- An explicit rule against retaliation, covering informal acts such as withholding letters or
  authorship.
- A complaint process that applies to faculty, with a named decision-maker outside the department.

## Questions to ask

Ask the program.

1. What funding is guaranteed, by whom, and for how long?
2. How does a student change advisers, and how often does it happen?
3. Who conducts annual reviews, and who sees them?
4. Where does a student go with a concern about their own adviser?
5. Does the program survey its students or conduct exit interviews, and are results shared?

Ask current students, ideally without faculty present.

1. Do you know anyone who changed labs? What happened to them?
2. Does your committee function as a real check?
3. If you had a serious problem with your adviser, where would you go? Would you go?
4. How are authorship decisions made in your lab?

Ask about a specific lab.

1. How many students have left without the degree they sought, and why?
2. How long do students take to finish?
3. Where are former members now, and will the adviser put you in touch with them?

## Reading the answers

- "It never comes up" is not reassuring in a program of any size. A program that can describe a
  recent adviser change, in general terms, has a process that works.
- A handbook that is detailed and current reflects attention. One that is years out of date does
  not.
- Hesitation from students is information. So is the adviser's reaction to being asked for former
  members' contacts.
- Good outcomes for people who left a lab matter more than the outcomes of those who thrived in it.

## What these features can and cannot tell you

They tell you whether the program has distributed power so that one failed relationship does not end
a student's career. They tell you whether there is a route that does not run through the adviser.

They cannot tell you how a particular adviser behaves. They cannot guarantee that safeguards are
used. A process can exist on paper and be distrusted in practice, which is why the conversations
with students matter.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing on the adviser's reputation for research alone.
- Assuming the university-wide policy tells you how the department operates. Practice is local. See
  the guide on decentralized universities.
- Speaking only with the students an adviser selects.
- Treating the absence of formal complaints as evidence that there are no problems.
- Leaving questions about funding guarantees until after accepting.

The guide on how research labs are funded covers the money side in more detail, and /glossary/
defines terms such as ombuds and principal investigator.
