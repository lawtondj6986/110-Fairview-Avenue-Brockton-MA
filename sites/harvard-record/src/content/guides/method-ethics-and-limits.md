Open-source research means research built on material that is publicly and lawfully available: published reports, court dockets, official filings, news reporting and public web pages. Its strength is that anyone can check it. That strength depends on staying inside limits. This guide sets out the limits this site observes and the reasoning behind them. It describes ethical practice, not law. Laws on privacy, computer access, recording and harassment vary by place, and nothing here is legal advice.

## Public sources only

Use material that a reader could also reach. If a source cannot be traced by a stranger, it cannot be checked, and a claim resting on it asks for trust the reader has no reason to give.

This excludes leaked documents of unknown origin, private correspondence passed along informally, and material behind a login that you are not entitled to use. It also means not bypassing technical barriers. If a page requires authorization you do not have, you do not have access to it.

## No pretexting

Pretexting means obtaining information by pretending to be someone you are not, or by hiding your purpose in a way that deceives. Examples include posing as a student, an employee, a customer or a relative to get records or statements, and creating false online profiles to enter private groups.

Do not do it. When you contact an office or a person, say who you are and why you are asking. Deception harms the people deceived, taints whatever is obtained and can be unlawful. If information can only be had by lying, it is outside the scope of this kind of research.

## No private data

Some information may be findable and still should not be collected or published.

- Home addresses, personal phone numbers and personal email addresses.
- Medical, genetic, financial and educational records of individuals.
- Government identifiers and account numbers.
- Information about a person's family members, especially children.
- Images of private individuals taken from personal accounts.

The test is not whether you can find it but whether the public purpose of the research requires it. For research about how institutions behave, the answer for items on this list is almost always no. If personal identifiers appear in a public document through someone's failure to redact, do not repeat them. Remember too that separate harmless details can identify or locate a person once combined. Do not assemble profiles of individuals.

## No harassment

Research becomes harassment when it turns into pressure on a person. Limits to observe:

- Contact people only through public, professional channels, and only for a clear purpose such as a right of reply.
- One letter and at most one reminder. Accept silence or refusal as an answer.
- Do not contact family members, neighbors or employers to reach or pressure a subject.
- Do not publish contact details or encourage readers to contact anyone.
- Do not monitor a person's ongoing private life or movements.
- Write so that your work does not invite others to target someone. Bystanders named in documents are especially exposed.

## Do not contact victims

Do not seek out or contact victims or alleged victims of abuse or exploitation, and do not try to identify those who have been kept anonymous. Unsolicited contact can cause real harm, and independent researchers generally lack the training and support structures needed to do it safely. Rely on what victims have chosen to say publicly, through court filings, their own published statements or established reporting, and cite it with care.

Do not name a victim who has not chosen to be named. Do not publish details that would identify one. If a person approaches you about their own experience, be respectful and point them to appropriate support and reporting channels. This site's page at `/campus-safety/` gives general orientation.

## Respect sealed and restricted material

Courts seal documents and redact names for reasons, commonly to protect victims, minors, privacy or the integrity of proceedings.

- Do not seek sealed documents through unofficial routes.
- Do not attempt to recover text hidden beneath redactions, and do not publish what others claim to have recovered.
- Do not cite unofficial copies of material that the official release withheld.
- If you come across material that appears to have been released by mistake, do not spread it.

Report what the public record shows, and state that sealed or redacted material exists where that is relevant.

## Honesty about what you know

Ethics also governs what you write.

- Keep each claim at the level its evidence supports. Definitions of the evidence states and source grades this site uses are at `/methodology/`.
- Separate findings from allegations.
- Do not imply connections that no source establishes.
- State absences as absences.
- Include denials and responses.
- Correct errors openly.

These are covered in the other guides in this series under `/guides/`.

## Know where this method stops

Open-source research can establish what public documents say and where they are silent. It cannot compel anyone to answer, examine private files, or weigh credibility the way a court or official inquiry can. When a question can only be settled by powers you do not have, say that the public record does not resolve it.

If your research turns up what appears to be evidence of an ongoing risk to someone's safety or of a crime, the appropriate course is to report it to the relevant authorities, not to investigate it yourself.

## A working test

Before any step, ask four questions. Is the material lawfully public? Am I being honest about who I am and what I am doing? Could this harm a private person, and does the public purpose justify that? Would I be comfortable describing this step, in full, in a public note on method? If any answer is no, do not take the step.
