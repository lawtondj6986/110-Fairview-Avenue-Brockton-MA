Emails become public through court cases, public records requests, congressional releases and
institutional reviews. They are among the most persuasive kinds of document, because they are
written at the time and in the writer's own voice. They are also fragments. A released email is a
selected piece of a larger conversation, and it has to be read with that in mind.

## Start with how it was released

1. Who produced it. The party that searched its own files and handed them over, or a third party.
2. Under what process. A court's discovery process, a subpoena, a public records law, a voluntary
   release, or a leak. Each has different rules about completeness.
3. Who selected it. A production may contain many thousands of pages. What reaches the public is
   often a subset chosen by a litigant, a committee or a journalist, each with a purpose.
4. In what form. A native file with metadata, a printed page image, or a quotation inside another
   document. Each step away from the original loses information.

Look for identifying marks. Pages produced in litigation usually carry a Bates number, a sequential
stamp with a prefix identifying the producing party. Bates numbers let you cite a page exactly and
see where it sat in the production: adjacent numbers are often related documents.

## Read the header

- From. The account that sent it. Assistants send from principals' accounts, and people use more
  than one address. A sender line shows which account, not always which person.
- To. The intended recipients.
- Cc. People copied. Being copied shows the message was sent to that address. It does not show the
  person opened or read it.
- Date and time. Time zones vary and may be converted during processing. Be careful when building a
  minute-by-minute sequence, and note the zone if it is shown.
- Subject. "Re:" and "Fwd:" indicate replies and forwards. Subject lines persist long after a thread
  has changed topic.
- Attachments. Often listed by file name. Check whether the attachment was produced. It may appear
  as the next Bates-numbered document or be missing.

## Read the thread in the right order

Most email software puts the newest message at the top and quotes earlier messages below. Read from
the bottom up to follow the conversation as it happened.

- Check whether the thread is complete. People delete quoted text, reply to only some recipients,
  and start side conversations.
- Watch the recipient list change from message to message. Someone on the first message may not have
  received the last.
- Quoted text can be edited by the person replying. An earlier message as quoted in a reply is not
  as reliable as the original.

## Read the message as a piece of speech

- Tone. Email is informal. Irony, exaggeration, shorthand and private jokes are common and easily
  misread by outsiders years later.
- Speaker's knowledge. A writer may be stating a fact, passing on something heard, guessing or
  proposing. "I hear she will join us" is not evidence that she did.
- Purpose. Writers flatter, sell, reassure and posture. A message written to impress its recipient
  is evidence of what the writer wanted the recipient to believe.
- Plans and outcomes. A message arranging a meeting shows that one was planned. Whether it took
  place needs other evidence, such as a later message, a calendar entry or a travel record.
- Third parties. A person mentioned in an email between two other people has not said or done
  anything by virtue of that mention. The message is evidence of what the writers said about them.

## What a reply, or the lack of one, shows

- A reply shows receipt and, to the extent of its content, engagement.
- No reply in the production does not show there was none. The reply may have been by phone, in
  person, from another account, or simply not produced.

## Authenticity

Emails produced by a party from its own systems in litigation, or released by an institution or
agency from its own records, are generally reliable as to the text. Material of unknown origin needs
more care.

- Is there a known chain from the source to you?
- Does the document carry production marks consistent with its stated source?
- Do names, dates and details match other records?
- Has any party to the message confirmed or disputed it?

## Redactions and withholding

Parts may be blacked out, and whole messages may be withheld for privilege or privacy. A privilege
log, where available, lists withheld documents by date, author and recipients. See [the guide to
reading a redacted document](/guides/read-a-redacted-document/).

## What a released email can tell you

- That a message with this text was sent from one account to others at about this time.
- What the writer chose to say to that recipient.
- That the writer and recipient were in contact, and something of the terms they were on.
- Leads: names, dates, attachments and events to check elsewhere.

## What it cannot tell you

- That its statements are true.
- That recipients read it, agreed with it or acted on it.
- What happened next.
- What the rest of the correspondence said.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting one line without the thread.
- Reading the thread top down and reversing the sequence.
- Treating a person who was copied, or merely mentioned, as a participant.
- Treating a plan, invitation or offer as an event that occurred.
- Ignoring who selected the document and why.

## How to cite one

Give the date, the sender and recipients as shown, the source of the release, and the Bates or
exhibit number. Quote exactly, indicate omissions, and describe the message in terms it supports:
"wrote," "told," "proposed," "said he planned." This site's [methodology page](/methodology/)
describes how released correspondence is labeled.
