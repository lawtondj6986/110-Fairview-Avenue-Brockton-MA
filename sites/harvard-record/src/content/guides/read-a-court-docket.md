A docket is a court's running index of a case. It lists, in date order, every document filed and
every action the court took. The docket is not the documents themselves. It is the table of
contents, and it is the quickest way to learn what stage a case has reached and which filings are
worth reading.

## Where dockets are found

- Federal courts in the United States use an electronic filing system. The public reaches it through
  a service called PACER, which requires an account and charges per-page fees, subject to caps and
  waivers that are set by the judiciary and change from time to time.
- Free archives run by nonprofit groups hold copies of many federal dockets and documents that other
  users have already retrieved. Coverage is partial.
- State courts each have their own systems. Some offer full online access, some only an index, and
  some require a visit or a request to the clerk.

You need the court and either the case number or a party's name.

## Reading the header

The top of a docket sheet gives the essentials.

- Court and case number. A federal case number usually encodes the office where the case was filed,
  the year, the case type and a sequence number, often followed by judges' initials. "cv" indicates
  a civil case and "cr" a criminal one.
- Case title, such as one party's name "v." another's. Additional parties may be covered by "et al."
- Assigned judge, and any magistrate judge handling pretrial matters.
- Date filed and, if the case has ended, date terminated.
- Nature of suit and cause. Short codes or phrases giving the legal category of the case.
- Parties and their lawyers. "Pro se" means a party has no lawyer.

## Reading the entries

Each entry normally has three parts: the date filed, an entry number, and a short text description.
The number identifies the document, written in citations as "ECF No." or "Dkt." followed by the
number. Attachments and exhibits are sub-numbered under the main entry.

Some points about the text.

- The description of a party's filing is usually written by the lawyer who filed it. It labels the
  document. It does not report what the court decided.
- Entries by the court are orders, opinions, notices and minute entries. A minute entry is a short
  note recording what happened at a hearing or a brief ruling, sometimes with no separate document.
- A "text-only order" is a ruling contained entirely in the docket text.

## Who filed it, and what kind of thing it is

The most important skill is distinguishing the parties' requests and arguments from the court's
decisions.

- Complaint, answer, counterclaim: the parties' pleadings. See [the guide to reading a civil
  complaint](/guides/read-a-civil-complaint/).
- Motion: a request that the court do something. A motion is followed by an opposition (or response)
  and often a reply. None of these is a ruling.
- Declaration or affidavit: a sworn statement submitted in support of a filing, often with exhibits
  attached.
- Order: the court's decision on a motion or direction to the parties.
- Memorandum opinion: the court's written reasons.
- Judgment: the final decision ending the case in the trial court.
- Stipulation: something the parties agreed between themselves, such as a deadline extension or a
  dismissal.
- Notice of appeal: a party is taking the case to a higher court, which will have its own docket and
  number.

To find out what happened to a motion, look for a later order that refers to its entry number.

## Sealed and restricted entries

- A sealed document has been filed but is not available to the public. The docket may show an entry
  marked "sealed" or may skip a number.
- A motion to seal, and the order deciding it, are usually public and explain in general terms what
  is being withheld and why.

A gap in the numbering or a sealed entry tells you that something exists. It does not tell you what.
Sealing is common for medical information, minors' identities and commercially sensitive material,
and is not in itself a sign of anything improper.

## Working out the status of a case

1. Read the header for a termination date or status flag.
2. Read the last ten or so entries.
3. Look for a judgment, a dismissal order or a stipulation of dismissal. Note whether dismissal was
   "with prejudice" (the claims cannot be refiled) or "without prejudice" (they can).
4. Look for a notice of settlement. The terms are usually not on the docket.
5. Check for a notice of appeal and, if there is one, find the appellate docket.
6. Check whether the case was transferred or consolidated into another, in which case later activity
   is recorded elsewhere.
7. Note the date you checked. A docket is current only as of that day.

## What a docket can tell you

- That a case exists, who the parties and lawyers are, and which judge is assigned.
- The sequence and dates of everything filed.
- What the court has ordered.
- Which documents exist, so you can obtain the ones that matter.

## What it cannot tell you

- Whether any claim is true. The docket lists filings without assessing them.
- The content of documents. A one-line description can mislead.
- Anything under seal.
- What happened outside court, including the terms of most settlements.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a filing as a ruling. "Motion to dismiss" on a docket means one was requested.
- Relying on the docket text without opening the order.
- Assuming a case is active because no closing entry was noticed, or closed because nothing has been
  filed lately.
- Reading a terminated party as having won. Parties are terminated for many reasons, including
  settlement and voluntary dismissal.
