Sources often disagree about numbers. One report gives a total of gifts, an article gives a larger
one, and a filing gives a third. The temptation is to pick the figure from the most trusted source,
or the one repeated most often, and move on. A better approach is to treat the disagreement as
information. Usually the figures differ because they count different things, and finding out what
each one counts is a result worth reporting.

## Why figures differ

Most conflicts come from a small number of causes.

- Different scope. One figure covers gifts to a single school, another to the whole university. One
  includes affiliated foundations and another does not.
- Different period. Calendar years, fiscal years (an organization's own accounting year, which often
  does not start in January) and "since" a given date all give different totals.
- Different definitions. Pledged versus received. Gifts from a person versus gifts arranged or
  solicited by that person from others. Cash versus in-kind support.
- Different dates of knowledge. An early figure may have been revised when more records were found.
- Rounding and approximation. "Nearly", "about" and "more than" are attached to round numbers that
  later lose their qualifiers.
- Currency and inflation. Amounts may be stated in nominal terms or adjusted.
- Transcription error. Digits are transposed, a thousand becomes a million, or a figure is attached
  to the wrong year.
- Compounding. An article adds two figures from separate sources that overlap, and counts some money
  twice.

## Trace each figure to its origin

Do not compare figures as they appear in summaries. For each one:

1. Find where it first appeared. Follow citations back until you reach a primary document or an
   on-the-record statement.
2. Read the sentence around it in that origin. Note the exact wording, including qualifiers and any
   footnote.
3. Write down what it counts: whose money, to whom, over what period, pledged or received.
4. Note the date the figure was stated and whether the source has since revised it.
5. Grade the source. Definitions of the grades are on the [methodology page](/methodology/).

Often this exercise resolves the conflict. The two figures turn out to describe different things,
and both are correct. Then say so: give each figure with its definition.

Sometimes it shows that several figures share one origin and differ only through copying. Then you
have one figure and some errors, and you report the original.

## When the conflict is real

If two sources genuinely disagree about the same quantity, you have choices, and all of them involve
telling the reader.

- Prefer the primary record, but say that you did. An official report based on the institution's own
  ledgers generally outranks a figure in a secondary account. Give the official figure, attribute
  it, and note that other figures have been reported.
- Give a range. "Reported figures range from X to Y" is accurate when you cannot determine which is
  right. Attribute each end.
- Give both, with sources. "The report states X. The complaint alleges Y." This is the right form
  when one figure is a finding and the other an allegation.
- Prefer the more recent of two figures from the same source, if the source revised it, and note the
  revision.

Do not average conflicting figures. An average is a new number that no source supports. Do not
choose the largest figure because it is more striking, or the smallest because it is safer, without
a reason grounded in the sources.

## Doing your own arithmetic

Adding figures across sources is risky, because the categories rarely line up and overlaps are hard
to see. If you compute a total yourself:

- say that the total is yours, and show the components and their sources;
- confirm the components do not overlap;
- carry the qualifiers through. A sum of "about" figures is itself approximate;
- do not present the result with more precision than the least precise component.

The same care applies to percentages and comparisons. State the base. "Doubled" means little without
the starting figure.

## Wording

Attach each number to its source and its definition in the sentence itself.

- "According to the report, the university received X in gifts between [year] and [year]."
- "The article, citing the same report, gives a figure of Y, which includes gifts the report
  describes as arranged from other donors."
- "We could not reconcile the two figures."

Keep qualifiers. If the source says "approximately", so do you. Do not convert "at least X" into
"X". Avoid false precision: a figure reported as "about 9 million" should not reappear as
"9,000,000".

Remember the evidence state as well. A pledged amount is stated intent. A received amount is
documented activity. A total confirmed by an official review is closer to verified implementation.
Different figures for "the gift" sometimes differ exactly along this line.

## Record the discrepancy

In your claim ledger, give each figure its own row with its source, definition and date checked, and
use the notes column to cross-reference the conflicting rows. On the published page, note the
difference where the figure appears, not only in a footnote. This site notes differing figures in
the text where they were found.

If a publisher appears to have made an error, such as a transposed digit, you can ask them to
correct it. A [correction request
template](/guides/template-correction-request/) is in the Templates series.

## What a figure cannot tell you

A total says how much. It does not say what the money did, who decided to accept it, or what anyone
received in return. Precise numbers lend an air of certainty to the sentences around them. Keep the
figure's authority confined to the thing it actually measures.
