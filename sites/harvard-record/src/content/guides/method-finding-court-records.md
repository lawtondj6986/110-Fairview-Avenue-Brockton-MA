Court records are among the strongest public sources, because filings are made under rules, dated by the court and kept by an official custodian. They are also scattered across many systems. This guide gives a general orientation for the United States. Procedures, access rules and costs differ between courts and change over time, so check the current information on each court's own website before relying on any detail here.

## Work out which court

The United States has a federal court system and a separate court system in each state.

Federal trial courts are called district courts. Appeals go to regional courts of appeals, and from there a small number of cases reach the Supreme Court. There are also specialized federal courts, including bankruptcy courts.

State systems use different names for similar levels. A state's general trial court might be called a superior court, a circuit court, a district court or something else, and many states have separate probate, family or housing courts. Trial courts are commonly organized by county.

To find a case, you usually need to know the court. Clues come from news reports ("filed in federal court in..."), from the citation of a decision, or from the parties' locations. One dispute may produce several cases in different courts, such as a civil suit, a criminal case and an appeal, each with its own number.

## Understand the docket

A docket is the court's chronological index of everything filed in a case. Each entry has a date, a short description and usually a number. The docket is your map. It tells you what documents exist, in what order they were filed, and what the court did.

Each case has a case number, also called a docket number, assigned when the case opens. Formats vary by court, but the number typically encodes the year, the case type and a sequence number. Record it exactly. With the court and the case number, anyone can find the same case.

## Federal records

Federal dockets and filings are available electronically through PACER (Public Access to Court Electronic Records), the federal judiciary's public access service, at `https://pacer.uscourts.gov/`. It requires an account. It charges fees for most searches and documents under a fee schedule set by the judiciary, with certain caps and waivers. Check the current schedule on the PACER site before you begin, since the details change.

Some federal material is available without charge.

- Written opinions of federal courts are generally provided free, including through PACER and on many courts' own websites.
- The Supreme Court publishes its own docket and opinions on its website.
- Free archives collect documents that other users have already obtained. The best known is CourtListener, run by a nonprofit, at `https://www.courtlistener.com/`. Coverage is partial: an archive has only what someone has contributed, and its docket may be out of date. Confirm anything important against the court's own record.
- Court clerks' offices generally provide public terminals for viewing electronic records in person.

## State records

State access varies widely. Some states offer a statewide online case search. Others leave access to each county or court. Some provide dockets online but documents only at the courthouse or by request to the clerk. Some charge for copies or for online access. Older cases may exist only on paper or microfilm, or in a state archive.

A workable approach:

1. Find the official website of the state's judiciary and look for "case search", "case information" or "public access".
2. If the search is by county, identify the county where the case was filed.
3. Search by party name, trying variant spellings and, for organizations, the full legal name.
4. If documents are not online, contact the clerk of the court to ask how to request copies. The clerk's office can explain procedure but cannot give legal advice.

## What is not public

Not everything in a court file is open.

- Sealed documents are closed by court order. The docket may show that a sealed entry exists without revealing its contents.
- Redacted documents are public with portions removed.
- Whole categories are restricted in many courts, commonly including juvenile matters and certain family and protective proceedings.
- Court rules require filers to leave out or shorten certain personal identifiers.

Respect these limits. Do not seek sealed material through unofficial channels, and do not publish personal identifiers that slipped through redaction. A sealed entry tells you only that something was filed under seal.

## Reading what you find

Know what kind of document you are holding, because each supports different statements.

- A complaint or indictment contains allegations. It shows what a party claimed, on what date.
- An answer or opposition contains the other side's response. Read it alongside the complaint.
- A motion asks the court to do something. It is argument, not a ruling.
- An exhibit is a document attached by a party. Its presence in the file shows that a party submitted it, not that the court accepted it as true.
- A declaration or affidavit is a sworn statement by an individual.
- An order or opinion is the court speaking. A judgment or verdict is a decision.
- A settlement or dismissal ends a case, usually without any finding on the merits.

Always check the latest docket entries. Complaints are amended, rulings are appealed, and judgments are sometimes reversed. A filing quoted without its later history can mislead.

## Citing a court record

Give the court, the case name, the case number, the title of the document, its docket entry number, its filing date and the page. Say where you obtained it and when. That is enough for a stranger to retrieve the same page from the official record.
