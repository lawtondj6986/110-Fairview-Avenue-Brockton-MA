A campus visit is a chance to test whether an institution can explain its own systems in plain
words. Tour guides and admissions staff may not know the details, and that is fine. What matters is
whether someone can point you to the person or page that does.

## Before the visit

1. Find the university's sexual misconduct and discrimination policy and skim the sections on
   reporting options.
2. Find the names of the offices involved: the office that handles complaints (often the Title IX
   office), campus police or security, the counseling center, and any confidential advocacy service.
3. Write down two or three things the policy left unclear. Those are your best questions.

Asking the same question of two different people, such as an admissions officer and a current
student, is often more revealing than asking many questions of one.

## Questions about reporting options

- If a student is harmed by another student, what are all the places they can go, and what happens
  at each one?
- Which staff are confidential, meaning they can listen without passing a report to the institution?
  Which staff are required to pass on what they hear?
- Can a student get help, such as a housing change or a class schedule change, without filing a
  formal complaint?
- Is there a way to report anonymously, and what can the institution do with an anonymous report?
- Does the process differ if the person accused is a faculty member, a staff member, or a visitor?

A clear answer distinguishes between telling someone confidentially, making a report, and filing a
formal complaint. These are three different acts with different consequences. An institution that
explains the difference well has usually thought about it.

## Questions about what happens after a report

- Who investigates, and who decides the outcome? Are they the same person?
- About how long does a formal case usually take?
- What interim measures are available while a case is open?
- Can both parties bring an adviser? Does the university provide one if a student has none?
- How does a student appeal, and to whom?
- What protection exists against retaliation, and who handles a retaliation complaint?

Federal rules governing these procedures have changed several times in recent years, and state law
adds further requirements in some places. Ask what the current procedure is, and ask when it was
last revised.

## Questions about support

- Is there an advocate whose only job is to support the student, separate from the investigating
  office?
- How long is the usual wait for a counseling appointment? Is there a limit on sessions?
- Are support services available at night, on weekends, and during breaks?
- Do students studying abroad, living off campus, or working in a lab or clinical placement have the
  same access?
- For graduate students: who can a student go to if the problem involves their own adviser? See the
  guide on adviser power in /guides/.

## Questions about transparency

- Has the university run a campus climate survey? Where are the full results?
- Does the complaint office publish an annual report with case numbers and outcomes?
- What changed after the last survey?

A good answer includes a location: a web address, a report title, an office name. A vague assurance
that the university takes these matters seriously is not an answer, though it may simply mean you
have asked the wrong person.

## Questions for current students

Students can tell you what the documents cannot.

- Do students know where to go? Could you name a confidential resource without looking it up?
- Was this covered in orientation, and was the training useful?
- Do students generally trust the process? Why or why not?
- Does the student newspaper cover how cases are handled?

Treat these answers as individual impressions. One student's experience, good or bad, is not a
measure of the whole institution. Several similar answers from unconnected students carry more
weight.

## How to interpret what you hear

- Specific beats general. Named offices, stated time frames and published documents are better signs
  than warm language.
- "I do not know, but here is who does" is a good answer.
- Defensiveness about the question itself is worth noting. So is an answer that treats the topic as
  a matter of personal caution only, with nothing said about institutional response.
- Compare what you were told with the written policy afterward. Where they differ, the written
  policy governs, and the difference tells you how well the policy is understood on the ground.

## What a visit cannot tell you

- It cannot tell you how often harm occurs. Use survey data for that.
- It cannot tell you how any particular case was handled. Case records are confidential, and staff
  are right not to discuss them.
- It reflects the people you happened to meet on one day.

## Common mistakes

- Asking only "Is this campus safe?" The answer is always yes.
- Asking questions that staff cannot lawfully answer, such as details of a named case, and reading
  the refusal as evasion.
- Skipping the questions because they feel awkward. Admissions staff hear them regularly.

After the visit, write down what you learned while it is fresh, and note which answers you could
verify in a published document.
