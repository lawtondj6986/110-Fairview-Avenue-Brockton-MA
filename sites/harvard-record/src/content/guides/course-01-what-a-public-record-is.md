This course teaches one skill: reading what a university has put on the public record, and saying no more than that record supports. It needs no special access and no legal training. Each lesson ends with an exercise you can do with the public documents of any university you choose.

## What you will learn

- What makes a document a public record, and what the word "public" does not promise
- Why a private university's public record is narrower than a public university's
- The four kinds of source used on this site, and how to tell them apart
- Why a grade describes the source and not the truth of what it says
- How to start a source log that the rest of the course builds on

## What counts as a public record

In this course, a [public record](/glossary/public-record/) is a document that an official body made or received in the course of its work and that any member of the public can lawfully obtain. A court filing is one. So is a tax return that the law opens to inspection, a statistical report that a university must publish, a federal agency's letter of findings, or a report that a university commissions and then releases. The related term [official record](/glossary/official-record/) covers documents that speak with the authority of the body that made them.

Three limits apply from the start.

First, public does not mean true. A lawsuit is a public record of what one side claims. A press statement is a public record of what an institution chose to say. Each is good evidence that the words were written, by that author, on that date. Whether the words are accurate is a separate question.

Second, public does not mean complete. Records are redacted, sealed, withheld under an exemption, or never created. What you can read is the part that was released.

Third, much of what happens at a university is not public at all. State public records laws apply to government bodies. A public university is usually covered, with exemptions that vary by state. A private university generally is not. Student education records are protected by a federal privacy law, explained in the [FERPA explainer](/law/ferpa/), and personnel files are normally confidential. The public record of a private university is therefore mostly made of three things: what it must file with government bodies, what ends up in court, and what it decides to publish about itself.

## The four kinds of source

This site sorts every source into one of four kinds. The definitions are on the [methodology page](/methodology/), and a [separate guide](/guides/method-grading-a-source/) works through hard cases.

- A1, primary official record. An official document speaking for itself: a court filing, an agency finding, a statute, a required disclosure, an institutional report.
- A2, reporting that tracks the record. Work by an established news organization that quotes or closely follows primary documents, or that records a statement made on the record.
- B1, secondary analysis. Books, scholarship and sourced commentary. Useful for context and for finding primary documents, weaker as proof of a specific fact.
- B2, thin or unverified. A single uncorroborated account, a second-hand recollection, an anonymous page, or anything you could not check against a primary document.

The quickest way to grade a source is to ask who produced it, how close it was to the event, whether you can see what it rests on, and whether you can open that underlying material yourself. A source you cannot trace is B2 however confident it sounds. A wiki page or an unofficial copy of a document is a lead that tells you where to look. It is not something to cite, for reasons set out in the guide on [mirrors and wikis](/guides/method-mirrors-and-wikis-are-leads/).

## A grade describes the source, not the claim

The most common beginner error is to treat a high grade as a stamp of truth. A grade tells you what kind of thing you are holding. It does not tell you that every sentence in it is established.

A civil complaint is an A1 record. It is first-rate evidence that a plaintiff made certain allegations on a certain date. It establishes nothing about whether they are true. A university's report about its own conduct is also A1, as a record of what the university found and said. It remains the university's own account, written within a scope the university set.

One document can also carry material of several grades. A carefully sourced news article is A2 where it quotes a filing. If the same article relays one person's memory of a conversation, that passage is B2. Grade the path that each claim travels, not the container it arrives in.

A lower grade is not an insult either. A B1 history of university fundraising may be the best explanation available of how gift offices work. It is still not the place to source a specific payment.

## Exercise

Pick one university. It can be the one you attended, one near you, or one you are considering. Use it for the whole course.

1. Choose one broad topic, such as campus safety or finances.
2. Find five items about that topic at your chosen university. Try to include at least one document the university was required to publish, one news article, and one page that is neither.
3. For each item, record in a table: title, the body or person that produced it, the date on the document, the address where you found it, and the date you retrieved it.
4. Assign each item a grade from A1 to B2.
5. For each item, write one sentence that begins "This can show" and one that begins "This cannot show".

Keep the table. It is the start of your source log, and a [source log template](/guides/template-source-log-entry/) shows a fuller layout.

## Check your work

The exercise was done well if a stranger could find each item again from your table alone, and if your two sentences for each item are different from one item to the next.

Common mistakes:

- Grading by reputation. A famous newspaper is not A1, and an obscure county court filing is.
- Grading by agreement. If the items you believe all came out A1 and the items you doubt came out B2, grade them again.
- Recording the address but not the retrieval date. Pages change and disappear.
- Writing "This can show that the event happened" for a document that only shows someone said it happened.
- Treating a news article about a filing as the filing. Where the article names a document, try to open the document and log it as its own row.
