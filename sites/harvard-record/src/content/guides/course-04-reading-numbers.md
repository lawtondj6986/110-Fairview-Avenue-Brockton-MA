A number in an official document looks more certain than a sentence. It is not. Every figure is the product of a definition, a period, a boundary and a counting rule. This lesson teaches four questions that recover those choices, and a method for the common case where two sources give different figures for what seems to be the same thing.

## What you will learn

- The four questions to ask of any figure before you repeat it
- Why calendar, fiscal and academic years produce different totals
- The difference between reports, incidents and prevalence
- What to do when two official figures differ, and what not to do

## Four questions for any figure

What is being counted? Find the unit. A fundraising total may count cash received, new pledges, or both. A crime table counts reports that met a legal definition, not people, not convictions and not complaints of every kind. A survey figure counts responses. If you cannot state the unit in a few words, you do not yet understand the number.

Over what period? Universities use at least three years. Crime statistics under campus safety law are reported by calendar year. Financial statements and tax filings use a [fiscal year](/glossary/fiscal-year/), which at many institutions runs from summer to summer. Enrollment and many internal reports use the academic year. Two figures labeled with the same year can cover different months.

Within what boundary? Ask who and where. Crime statistics cover defined locations, described in the entry on [Clery geography](/glossary/clery-geography/), so an incident involving a student a few streets from campus may fall outside the table. A financial figure may cover the university alone, or the university together with affiliated hospitals, foundations or other entities. A survey covers those who answered it.

Under whose rules? A figure follows the rulebook of the document it appears in. Audited financial statements follow accounting standards. A Form 990 follows tax form instructions. A campaign report follows fundraising [counting standards](/glossary/gift-counting-standards/) or the institution's own policy. The same underlying facts can properly yield different numbers under each.

## Reports, incidents and prevalence

Safety figures cause the most confusion, because three different things are measured and all are called "the numbers".

A reported-crime statistic counts reports made to certain officials, in certain places, that fit certain definitions. A case count from a conduct or civil-rights office counts matters that the office opened or resolved under its own procedures. A survey estimate of prevalence measures how many respondents say they experienced something, whether or not they told anyone.

These do not converge, and they are not supposed to. The glossary entry on [prevalence versus reported incidents](/glossary/prevalence-vs-reported-incidents/) and the primer on [campus crime statistics](/guides/primer-campus-crime-statistics/) explain why. Two consequences matter here. A low count of reports is not evidence of a low rate of harm. A rising count may reflect more incidents, or more willingness to report, or a change in definitions, and the table alone cannot say which.

Survey figures add a further question. Check the [response rate](/glossary/response-rate/) and how the survey describes the people who answered. A percentage of respondents is not automatically a percentage of students. The guide to [reading a climate survey](/guides/read-a-campus-climate-survey/) covers this.

## When two figures differ

Sooner or later you will find two official figures for the same thing that do not match. A fundraising report gives one total for gifts in a year, and the tax filing gives another. A news article cites a third.

Do not average them. Do not pick the one that suits the argument. Do not assume that either is wrong. Work through the four questions for each figure. Most differences turn out to be differences of definition: one figure includes pledges and the other only cash, one covers a fiscal year and the other a calendar year, one includes an affiliated entity and the other does not.

Then write what you found. If you can explain the difference, say so and cite the definitions. If you cannot, report both figures with their sources and say plainly that the record does not explain the gap. The guide on [figures that differ](/guides/method-figures-that-differ/) sets out the method in full, and the guide on [donation statistics](/guides/read-a-donation-statistic/) applies it to gifts.

Comparisons between universities need the same care. Institutions differ in size, in how much housing is on campus, and in how they define their boundaries. The guide on [comparing misconduct data fairly](/guides/compare-sexual-misconduct-data-fairly/) lists what must be held equal first.

## Exercise

Use the documents you inventoried in Lesson 3.

1. Choose one figure that appears, in some form, in two documents. Good candidates are total contributions in a year (annual fundraising report and tax filing) or total expenses (financial statements and tax filing).
2. Make a card for each figure with seven lines: the number exactly as printed, the label the document gives it, the unit, the period with start and end months, the boundary (which entities, places or people), the rulebook, and the page where it appears.
3. Read the notes, footnotes or definitions near each figure and add anything that changes a line on the card.
4. Compare the two cards line by line and mark each line as the same, different or not stated.
5. Write a two-sentence statement that reports both figures and says what you can and cannot explain about the difference.
6. Repeat with one safety figure: a single category in the annual security report, for the same year, compared with any other published figure on a similar subject.

## Check your work

The exercise was done well if someone reading your two-sentence statement could not tell which figure you would have preferred, and if every "different" mark on your cards points to words in the documents.

Common mistakes:

- Leaving the period as a bare year. Write the months.
- Guessing the reason for a gap. "Probably pledges" is a hypothesis. Label it as one unless a document says so.
- Calculating a rate from a count without a denominator that matches the count's boundary.
- Describing a change from one year to the next as a trend. Small counts move a great deal from year to year for ordinary reasons.
- Rounding or converting a figure and then quoting the converted version as if it were printed.
- Comparing a figure for one legal entity with a figure for a group of entities.
