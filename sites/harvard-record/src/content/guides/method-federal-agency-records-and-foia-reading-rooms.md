Federal agencies hold records that bear on universities: grant awards, civil-rights enforcement files, audit reports, correspondence and more. Much of it is already published. The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), at 5 U.S.C. § 552, lets any person request records from federal executive branch agencies, but a request takes time, and the first step is always to look for what is public. This guide covers where to look and how reading rooms fit in.

## What FOIA covers

FOIA applies to federal executive branch agencies. It does not apply to Congress, the federal courts, state or local governments, or private organizations, including private universities. Each state has its own public records law for state and local bodies.

The statute requires agencies to release records on request unless one of nine exemptions applies. The exemptions cover matters such as classified information, personal privacy, certain law enforcement records, confidential commercial information and privileged internal deliberations. Agencies may withhold a record in full or release it with redactions, citing the exemption used. The statute text is at `https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/552`.

## What a reading room is

FOIA also requires agencies to make certain categories of records available to the public without waiting for a request. These include final opinions and orders in decided cases, statements of policy, staff manuals that affect the public, and records that have been requested repeatedly or are likely to be.

Agencies post these in an online collection usually called a FOIA reading room, electronic reading room or FOIA library. Many also post a FOIA log, which is a list of requests the agency has received, sometimes with the outcome.

Reading rooms are uneven. Some are well organized and searchable. Others are a list of files with unhelpful names. Their contents are a selection, and an empty search result means only that the record is not posted there.

## A search order that saves time

1. Identify the right agency and the right office within it. Large departments have many components, each with its own FOIA office and its own reading room. Records about a civil-rights complaint against a university, for example, would sit with the civil-rights office of the agency that received it, not with the department's central office.
2. Search the agency's main website for the subject. Program offices publish reports, datasets, guidance and enforcement documents outside the reading room.
3. Search the reading room, by keyword and by browsing categories.
4. Check the FOIA log for earlier requests on the same subject. If records were released before, the agency can often provide them again quickly, and you can cite the earlier request number.
5. Check government-wide sources, listed below.
6. Only then consider filing a request, drafted narrowly around what you could not find.

The government's central FOIA site, `https://www.foia.gov/`, lists agency FOIA offices and contact details and lets you submit requests to many agencies.

## Government-wide sources worth knowing

Several official sites cut across agencies.

- The Federal Register publishes rules, proposed rules and notices. Public comments on rulemakings are collected on the federal regulations portal.
- The federal spending portal publishes data on grants and contracts by recipient.
- Research funding agencies run searchable databases of the grants they have awarded.
- Inspectors general, the independent audit and investigation offices within agencies, publish reports on their own sites.
- The Government Accountability Office publishes its reports to Congress.
- Congressional committees publish hearing records, and correspondence between committees and agencies is sometimes released by either side.
- The Department of Education publishes data that colleges and universities report to it on certain foreign gifts and contracts.
- The National Archives holds older records that agencies have transferred.

For each, use the official site and record the address and the date you retrieved the item.

## Reading what you find

Released agency records have features that affect how you use them.

- Redactions are marked with the exemption relied on. The marking tells you why something was withheld, in general terms, and nothing about what it said. Do not guess.
- A release letter usually states how many pages were located, how many were released and how many were withheld. Keep it with the records. It defines the scope of what you hold.
- A response that the agency found no responsive records is a statement about the agency's search of the places it looked, using the terms of the request. It is not proof that nothing exists.
- Some agencies may refuse to confirm or deny whether records exist, typically where confirming would itself reveal protected information.
- An open investigation is not a finding. Records showing that a complaint was received or a review was opened establish only that. Look for the resolution document, and report the outcome it states.
- Agency documents carry dates, case numbers and signatories. Use them in citations.

A released agency record is a primary official record of what the agency wrote or received. Third-party letters inside an agency file, such as a complaint from a member of the public, are records of what that party asserted.

## If you do file a request

Ask for specific records, with dates, offices and document types where you can. Broad requests take longest. State the format you want and the fee category you believe applies. FOIA sets time limits for agencies to respond, but backlogs are common and many requests take much longer. If a request is denied in whole or in part, the response letter explains how to appeal within the agency and by when. Agencies also have FOIA public liaisons who can help with a stalled request.
