Most errors in public-record research are not invented facts. They are true facts reported one step
further along than the record supports. A plan becomes an event. A payment becomes a program. The
three-state discipline is a habit of sorting each claim by how far the evidence goes, and of writing
the claim at that level and no higher. This site uses it throughout, and the definitions are set out
on the [methodology page](/methodology/).

## The three states, and a fourth

Stated intent means a record shows that something was proposed, pitched, promised or said. The
record does not show that it happened. Examples include a proposal document, an email offering to
fund something, a draft agreement, or a remark in an interview about future plans.

Documented activity means a record shows that something was done. Examples include a payment in a
ledger, a signed agreement, a visitor log entry, a shipping record, or an appointment letter. The
record shows the act. It does not by itself show what the act produced.

Verified implementation means an official finding or an independent result confirms the activity and
what came of it. Examples include an institutional review that traced the money and reported how it
was spent, a court finding, an audit, or a published result that an independent party has confirmed.

A fourth label matters as much as the other three: nothing on record. It means you looked and found
no public record. It does not mean the thing did not happen. A [separate guide in this
series](/guides/method-stating-an-absence/) covers how to state an absence.

## How to assign a state

Work one claim at a time. For each, ask three questions in order.

1. What is the most advanced thing a document shows directly? Read the document itself, not a
   summary of it. A summary often upgrades the state without saying so.
2. Who made the record, and when? An account written by the person proposing a project is evidence
   of intent. A bank record or a counterparty's receipt is evidence of activity.
3. Has anyone independent confirmed the outcome? If not, the claim stops at documented activity,
   however detailed the paperwork.

Then split compound claims. "A donor funded a research program that produced three papers" contains
at least three claims: that money was offered, that money arrived, and that the papers resulted from
it. Each part gets its own state. It is common for the first to be verified, the second to be
documented activity, and the third to be stated intent or nothing on record.

## Words that match each state

The state should be visible in the sentence, not only in a label beside it.

- Stated intent: proposed, offered, discussed, planned, said he would, described a plan to.
- Documented activity: paid, signed, visited, shipped, was appointed, according to the ledger.
- Verified implementation: the review found, the court found, the audit confirmed.
- Nothing on record: we found no public record of, the report does not address.

Avoid verbs that blur the line. "Was involved in", "was linked to" and "had ties to" describe no
state at all. Replace them with the specific act the record shows.

## Rules for moving a claim up

A claim moves up a state only when a source supports the move. Three rules keep this honest.

- Volume does not upgrade a claim. Twenty emails discussing a plan are still evidence of a plan.
- Repetition does not upgrade a claim. Ten articles repeating one proposal document are one source,
  at the state of that document.
- Later events do not upgrade earlier ones by implication. If a gift was proposed in one year and a
  gift was recorded in another, check that the amounts, parties and purpose match before treating
  the second as the first carried out.

A claim can also move down. If a later official review says a reported payment was never received,
the claim returns to stated intent, and the earlier version should be corrected in the open.

## State is not the same as source grade

The state describes the claim. The grade describes the source. They are assessed separately. A
primary official record (grade A1) may show only stated intent, as when a court exhibit is an
unanswered proposal. A thin source (grade B2) may assert a verified outcome that you cannot confirm.
Record both values for every claim, and do not let a high grade stand in for a high state.

## What the discipline cannot do

It does not tell you whether something was right or wrong. It does not tell you what a person knew
or meant. It only keeps your wording level with your evidence. It also depends on the public record,
which is incomplete. Much real activity leaves no public document, and some documents describe plans
that were quietly dropped. The method does not resolve that gap. It makes the gap visible so the
reader can see where the record ends.

## A quick self-check

Before publishing a paragraph, underline every verb that describes an act by a person or
institution. For each one, name the document that shows the act and the state it supports. If you
cannot name the document, rewrite the sentence or remove it.
