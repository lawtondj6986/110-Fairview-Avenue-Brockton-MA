Universities depend on philanthropy, and donors receive things in return for giving. Most of what
they receive is open and ordinary. Some things are generally off limits by law or by policy. Between
the two lies a gray area that written rules cover poorly. This guide describes each in general
terms.

## What donors ordinarily receive

- Recognition. A name on a building, a professorship, a scholarship or a program, usually under a
  written gift agreement and a naming policy.
- Direction of purpose. A donor may restrict a gift to a field, a school or a use, such as financial
  aid or research on a particular disease. The university must honor a restriction it has accepted.
- Reporting. Stewardship reports on how the money was used.
- Events and contact. Invitations to dinners, lectures and campus visits, and meetings with leaders
  and researchers.
- Advisory roles. Seats on visiting committees or advisory councils, which usually advise and do not
  govern.
- A tax deduction, where the law allows, reduced by the value of anything substantial received in
  return.

None of this is improper in itself. It is how fundraising works at nearly every institution.

## What is generally off limits

The details depend on each institution's policies, but the following principles are widely stated.

- Admission. Institutions state that gifts do not purchase admission. A payment made in exchange for
  a specific benefit is also not a charitable gift for tax purposes.
- Hiring and promotion. A donor may fund a professorship but should not choose who holds it.
  Appointments follow academic procedures.
- Research conclusions. A donor may fund a field of inquiry but may not dictate findings or control
  publication. Sponsored research agreements, which are a different instrument from gifts, have
  their own rules on publication and intellectual property.
- Selection of individual beneficiaries. A donor who picks the specific person to receive a
  scholarship or fellowship generally undermines the gift's charitable status.
- Curriculum and academic decisions. These belong to the faculty and academic leadership.
- Personal financial benefit. Tax law restricts charities from conferring private benefit on
  insiders and donors.

How firmly these lines hold depends on the institution's written agreements and on who enforces
them.

## The gray area: access

Much of what matters is not written in a gift agreement.

- Time. Large donors get meetings with presidents, deans and prominent faculty that others do not.
- Association. Being seen on campus, photographed with scientists or listed as a supporter lends
  standing. For some donors, the university's reputation is the main thing acquired.
- Affiliation. Courtesy titles, visiting status, an office, a building pass or a university email
  address can follow from a relationship with a lab or center, sometimes with little central review.
- Introductions. Access to one researcher leads to others, at the same institution and beyond.
- Informal voice. A donor's views on what a center should work on may carry weight without any
  formal right to decide.
- Goodwill. An institution that hopes for a future gift may be slower to ask hard questions.

These benefits are rarely recorded, which makes them hard to audit afterward. They also tend to be
granted locally, by a faculty member or center director, where central policies reach least.

## Naming

Naming is the most visible exchange and usually the best documented.

- Names are typically approved by senior leadership or the governing board under a naming policy.
- A gift agreement may specify the duration: for the life of the building, for a term of years, or
  in perpetuity.
- Many policies and agreements include a clause allowing the institution to remove a name if
  continued association would harm it. Older agreements may lack one.
- Removing a name can raise questions about the original gift's terms and, in some cases, legal
  obligations to the donor or the donor's estate. These vary by agreement and by state law.

## How to assess a particular institution

1. Read the gift acceptance and naming policies. See the guide on reading a gift acceptance policy.
2. Check whether policies cover affiliations and titles, not only money.
3. Look for a rule that all gifts, including those to individual labs, are recorded centrally.
4. Ask whether donors or their representatives sit on bodies that make academic or admissions
   decisions.
5. Look for a conflict of interest policy that covers fundraisers and faculty who receive personal
   benefits from donors, such as travel, hospitality or investment opportunities.

## What the public record can and cannot show

Public documents can show named gifts, announced totals, board and committee memberships, and
written policies. Tax filings of private foundations list their grants, so a foundation's giving to
a university can often be traced from the donor's side.

Public documents rarely show who met whom, who was introduced to whom, or what was said. Evidence of
informal access usually comes only from released correspondence, calendars, visitor records or an
institution's own review. Absence of such evidence in public does not establish that access was
limited, and presence of contact does not by itself show that anything improper was exchanged.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming every benefit to a donor is corrupt. Recognition and reporting are standard.
- Assuming a formal rule settles the matter. Influence often runs through informal access.
- Treating a meeting or a photograph as proof of a transaction.
- Overlooking gifts made to individual faculty or labs because the central office reported nothing.
