Money leaves more paper than almost anything else a university does, and the paper uses a precise vocabulary. A reader who treats a gift, a pledge, a grant and a rating as the same thing will misread nearly every document about funding. This lesson separates the terms and then asks what a record of money can establish.

## What you will learn

- The difference between a gift, a pledge, a grant and sponsored research
- What restrictions and endowments change about how money can be used
- What a donor rating is, and why it is not a record of giving
- Which document shows which stage of a transaction
- What a record of money does not show

## Four kinds of money that are often confused

A [gift](/glossary/gift/) is a voluntary transfer for which the donor receives nothing of substantial value in return. The donor may limit the purpose, and the institution then has a [restricted gift](/glossary/restricted-gift/). The donor does not direct the work or own the results.

A [pledge](/glossary/pledge/) is a promise to give, often paid over several years. A pledge is not cash. Pledges are sometimes paid late, renegotiated or never completed. Announcements of large gifts are very often announcements of pledges, and words such as "commitment" and "up to" are the signal.

A grant is an award for a defined project, usually made after an application, with a budget, a period and reporting duties. Federal research grants are the largest example. Foundations also make grants.

Sponsored research is work done under an agreement with a sponsor, often a company or an agency, that sets out deliverables and terms on matters such as publication and intellectual property. Unlike a gift, it is an exchange.

The lines matter because different offices handle each, different rules apply, and different records result. The glossary entry on [gifts, grants and sponsored research](/glossary/gift-grant-sponsored-research/) and the primer on [how money reaches a lab](/guides/primer-money-to-a-lab/) give more detail. A further distinction is between money spent now and money placed in an [endowment](/glossary/endowment/), where the principal is invested and only a portion is spent each year.

## Ratings and other records that are not money

Fundraising offices keep records about people who might give. A [capacity rating](/glossary/capacity-rating/) is an estimate of how much a person or organization could give, usually built from public information about wealth. An affinity rating estimates how close the prospect feels to the institution. Both are internal working judgments.

A rating is not a gift, a pledge or a request. It does not show that anyone was asked for money, that anyone gave, or that the estimate was accurate. Being rated shows that a development office did what such offices do routinely for large numbers of people. The primer on [prospect research and ratings](/guides/primer-prospect-research-and-ratings/) describes the practice.

Other records fall in the same class: a name on a list of prospects, a contact report after a meeting, an invitation to an event, a draft proposal. Each is at most stated intent in the terms of Lesson 2, and often it is only evidence that staff were doing their jobs.

## Which document shows what

Follow a hypothetical naming gift from a company for a new engineering laboratory.

A news release announces a commitment of a stated amount. It shows what the university and the company chose to announce. It does not show the payment schedule or the terms.

A [gift agreement](/glossary/gift-agreement/) would show the terms: purpose, schedule, naming, and what happens if circumstances change. These agreements are usually not public at private institutions. Where one is public, it shows what was agreed, not what was paid.

Audited financial statements show totals for contributions and for pledges receivable. They rarely identify individual donors.

The Form 990 shows total contributions for the fiscal year and other aggregate data. The public copy generally does not name donors. If the company gives through its own foundation, that foundation's public filing may list the grant, its recipient and its amount.

A donor honor roll or a plaque shows recognition, often in broad bands, and sometimes cumulative over many years.

No single document shows the whole path. The primer on [how a gift moves](/guides/primer-how-a-gift-moves/) lays out the full sequence, and the guide to [gift acceptance policies](/guides/read-gift-acceptance-policy/) explains who decides whether to accept.

## What a record of money does not show

A payment record shows that money moved. Four things need separate evidence.

Purpose. A transfer to a university does not say what it was for unless a document says so.

Use. Money given for a purpose and money spent on that purpose are two facts. The second needs a spending record, a report or a finding.

Influence. A gift does not show that the donor shaped a decision. Donors may properly receive recognition and reports. Whether one received more is a question for evidence, discussed in the guide on [what a donor can and cannot buy](/guides/what-a-donor-can-and-cannot-buy/).

Knowledge. An institution's receipt of money does not show who inside it knew of the gift or of anything about the donor.

Absence works the same way. No public record of a gift does not mean no gift was made, since most gifts to private institutions leave no public trace.

## Exercise

Use your chosen university.

1. Find one publicly announced gift, pledge or sponsored research agreement from at least three years ago. A named building, professorship or center is a good choice.
2. Copy the exact words the announcement uses for the money: gift, pledge, commitment, grant, investment, partnership, "up to".
3. Classify it as a gift, pledge, grant or sponsored research, and note what in the text supports that.
4. Record the stated amount, the stated purpose and any stated restriction or schedule.
5. Search for later evidence of receipt: the giver's own public filings if it is a foundation, the university's reports, later announcements.
6. Search for later evidence of use: the building opened, the chair was filled, the program reported results.
7. Assign each of the three facts (promised, received, used) a state from Lesson 2 and cite the document.

## Check your work

The exercise was done well if promised, received and used each have their own row, their own source and possibly different states.

Common mistakes:

- Recording the announced amount as the amount received.
- Treating a cumulative recognition level as a single gift.
- Assuming the named party paid personally. Gifts often come from foundations, companies, donor-advised funds or estates.
- Reading a university's total contributions as if it could be divided among known donors.
- Concluding that a program exists because it was funded, or that it was funded because it exists.
- Describing a sponsored research agreement as a gift, or the reverse.
