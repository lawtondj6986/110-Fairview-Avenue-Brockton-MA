This worked example applies the guide on [writing about named
people](/guides/method-writing-about-named-people/) to one part of the [DNA and genetics
record](/dna-genetics/): emails about consumer DNA test kits, quoted in reporting on the Justice
Department release. It adds no facts. Each statement restates a row in the [claim ledger](/ledger/),
with its caveat, or an entry in the site's [index of names](/named/), which adds nothing beyond the
ledger.

The people named in these emails as recipients are not implicated in anything. That is the
conclusion of this example, and it is also the first thing a fair page has to say.

## Step 1: read what the rows record

Three rows cover the emails. All are documented activity, graded A2.

- [Ledger G-06](/ledger/#G-06). In September 2016 Epstein was told 23andMe kits had arrived for
  delivery to Woody Allen and Soon-Yi Previn [@nypost-2026-kits]. Caveat: receiving a kit implies
  nothing about the recipient.
- [Ledger G-07](/ledger/#G-07). In April 2017 Karyna Shuliak asked for two 23andMe kits to be sent
  to Noam and Valeria Chomsky; two kits bought on Amazon were shipped [@nypost-2026-kits]. Caveat:
  receiving a kit implies nothing about the recipient, and no research use is described.
- [Ledger G-08](/ledger/#G-08). In summer 2017 an assistant placed a bulk 23andMe order for Sultan
  Ahmed bin Sulayem's colleagues, according to the correspondence, which the company queried and
  cancelled [@sfgate-2026-kits]. Caveat: accounts differ on the number of kits and on what became of
  them.

The activity in each row is a delivery, a shipment or an order. Where the rows name who handled a
kit, that person was a member of staff or an associate. The people named as recipients did nothing
the rows describe.

## Step 2: decide whether to name them at all

The method asks first whether a name is needed. The site's rule, on the [methodology
page](/methodology/), is that people named only as recipients are named when the cited reporting
names them, and the text says plainly that no wrongdoing is implied. Each name here appears in the
reporting the ledger cites.

One argument for naming rather than withholding is accuracy. An unnamed "public figure" invites
guessing, and a guess can land on the wrong person. The cost of naming is that a name in a record
about Epstein can be read as an accusation. The site meets that cost by saying, every time, what the
name is doing there.

## Step 3: describe the capacity exactly

The index of names gives each person a role, and the role for all three entries is the same: named
only as a recipient. The wording follows from it.

For Woody Allen and Soon-Yi Previn, the index records that an email says kits had arrived and would
be delivered. It records that nothing shows whether the kits were used, that receiving a gift
implies nothing about the recipient, and that no wrongdoing is alleged or suggested.

For Noam and Valeria Chomsky, the index records that two kits were bought on Amazon and shipped, and
the same three limits.

For Sultan Ahmed bin Sulayem, the index records that he is named in correspondence as the person for
whose colleagues the bulk order was placed, and that 23andMe queried the order and cancelled it
under its terms of use. It adds that accounts differ on the size of the order and what became of the
kits, and that nothing describes any use of results.

Useful verbs are "is named as the intended recipient" and "an email says kits would be delivered
to." Verbs such as "received DNA testing from" or "took part in" would each add an act the rows do
not record.

## Step 4: say plainly that they are not implicated

The sentence should be in the same paragraph as the name, in plain words: they appear only as
intended recipients of a retail gift, and nothing in the record implicates them in anything. Placing
that sentence in a footnote, or only on another page, leaves the name without its context.

## Step 5: treat staff by what they did

Staff appear in a different capacity. Karyna Shuliak wrote the April 2017 request. The index records
that the email describes a gift of a retail product and does not describe research, analysis or any
use of results. An assistant, Lesley Groff, placed the summer 2017 order, and the index records that
the correspondence concerns purchasing and shipping. These are descriptions of who handled what.
They carry no suggestion beyond it.

## Step 6: do not build a list

The emails name several people, and it is tempting to set the names out together. The method warns
against this. A list strips each name of its context and implies a shared status that the documents
do not show. The DNA and genetics record gives each email its own row, with its own date and its own
caveat, for that reason. This example does the same.

It is equally important not to set the kits beside the other DNA-related items as though they
formed a whole. [Ledger G-10](/ledger/#G-10) records that no public record shows a shared genetic
database, a research output or an operational program connecting these items, a statement of
absence across the sources reviewed [@nypost-2026-kits]. The kits are retail products sent as gifts.
The guide on [adjacency](/guides/method-avoiding-false-patterns/) covers this in more detail.

## A sentence that works

"In April 2017 an email from Karyna Shuliak asked for two 23andMe consumer kits to be sent to Noam
and Valeria Chomsky, and two kits bought on Amazon were shipped. Nothing shows whether the kits were
used. The Chomskys are named only as intended recipients of a gift, and nothing in the record
implicates them in anything."
