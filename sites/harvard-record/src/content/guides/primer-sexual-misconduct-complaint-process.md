When a student or employee tells someone at a university about sexual harassment, sexual assault,
dating violence or stalking, a set of obligations starts to run. Some come from federal law, mainly
[Title IX](/law/title-ix/) and the [Clery Act](/law/clery-act/) as amended by the [Violence Against
Women Act](/law/vawa-2013-clery-amendments/). Others come from state law and from the university's
own policy. This primer describes the usual sequence in the United States and the records it leaves
behind. It is a general description, not advice about any situation.

## The rules have changed, so check the current policy

The federal regulations that govern Title IX grievance procedures have been rewritten several times.
This primer uses the framework of the regulations issued in 2020 as its reference point, because
that framework set out a detailed, step-by-step process and many university policies still follow
its structure. A later rule was issued in 2024 and was then vacated by a federal court, and the
Department of Education said it would enforce the 2020 rule. The position may have moved again
since. Universities also run parallel procedures for conduct that falls outside Title IX's reach,
and states add their own requirements. Before relying on any step described here, read the
university's current policy and the current [Title IX regulations](/law/title-ix-regulations/).

## Step one: disclosure

Most matters begin with a disclosure: a person tells someone what happened. Who they tell matters.

- A [confidential resource](/glossary/confidential-resource/), such as a counselor or a pastoral
  adviser, generally does not pass the information on with identifying details.
- A [responsible employee](/glossary/responsible-employee/) (the term and its scope vary by policy)
  is required to tell the Title IX office.
- The [Title IX coordinator](/glossary/title-ix-coordinator/) is the official who runs the process.

A disclosure is not a complaint. The difference is explained in [disclosure versus formal
complaint](/glossary/disclosure-vs-formal-complaint/). Telling the Title IX office starts outreach,
not an investigation.

## Step two: outreach and supportive measures

After learning of a report, the coordinator typically contacts the person affected, called the
complainant. Under the 2020 framework the coordinator must discuss [supportive
measures](/glossary/supportive-measures/) and explain how to file a formal complaint. Supportive
measures are non-punitive steps such as a change of class section or housing, extended deadlines, or
a mutual [no-contact order](/glossary/no-contact-order/). They are available whether or not a
complaint is filed. Older policies call similar steps [interim
measures](/glossary/interim-measures/).

Many complainants stop at this point. They receive support and do not ask for an investigation. That
is a common outcome, and it means no finding is ever made.

## Step three: the formal complaint

A [formal complaint](/glossary/formal-complaint/) is a written request that the university
investigate. Under the 2020 framework it is signed by the complainant or by the Title IX
coordinator. The coordinator may sign one even when the complainant does not wish to proceed, for
example where there is a risk to others. The university must also decide whether the conduct falls
within Title IX's jurisdiction. If it does not, the complaint may be dismissed from the Title IX
process and handled under a separate conduct code.

## Step four: notice and informal resolution

Once a formal complaint exists, both parties receive written notice of the allegations. The person
accused is called the respondent. The notice states the conduct alleged, says the respondent is
presumed not responsible until a determination is made, and tells both parties they may have an
adviser of their choice.

The parties may agree to [informal resolution](/glossary/informal-resolution/), such as a
facilitated agreement, instead of an investigation. The 2020 framework requires both parties'
voluntary written consent and does not allow informal resolution where an employee is accused of
harassing a student.

## Step five: investigation

An investigator gathers evidence: interviews with each party and with witnesses, messages,
photographs, and records. Under the 2020 framework both parties get an equal opportunity to present
witnesses and evidence and to inspect and respond to the evidence gathered. The investigator then
writes an investigative report that summarizes the relevant evidence. The parties may respond to the
report in writing before any hearing. The regulations set minimum periods for these reviews; check
the current text for them.

The investigator gathers and summarizes. Under the 2020 framework the investigator does not decide
the outcome.

## Step six: hearing and determination

For universities and colleges, the 2020 framework requires a live hearing. A decision-maker who is
not the Title IX coordinator or the investigator presides. Each party's adviser may ask the other
party and witnesses relevant questions, including challenging ones. The hearing must be recorded or
transcribed.

The decision-maker applies a standard of evidence that the policy names. Universities choose either
[preponderance of the evidence](/glossary/preponderance-of-the-evidence/) or [clear and convincing
evidence](/glossary/clear-and-convincing-evidence/), and must use the same standard for comparable
cases. The result is a written determination that states the allegations, the procedural steps
taken, the findings of fact, the conclusion on each allegation with reasons, any sanction, and any
remedies for the complainant. Both parties receive it at the same time.

A determination of "not responsible" means the standard was not met on the evidence gathered. It is
not a finding that nothing happened. The difference between an allegation and a finding is covered
in [finding versus allegation](/glossary/finding-vs-allegation/).

## Step seven: appeal and closure

Either party may appeal on grounds the policy lists. Under the 2020 framework these include
procedural irregularity that affected the outcome, new evidence that was not reasonably available,
and a conflict of interest or bias on the part of a Title IX official. A different official decides
the appeal. Once the appeal is decided or the time to appeal passes, the determination is final.
Sanctions for students may range from a warning to expulsion. Sanctions for employees follow the
policy and any employment contract or faculty rules.

[Retaliation](/glossary/retaliation/) against anyone for taking part in the process is prohibited
throughout and can itself be the subject of a complaint.

## A worked hypothetical

A second-year student tells a teaching assistant that a classmate repeatedly followed her and sent
unwanted messages. The teaching assistant is a responsible employee under the university's policy
and tells the Title IX office the same day. The coordinator emails the student, offers a meeting and
explains the options. The student accepts a change of lab section and a mutual no-contact order but
does not file a formal complaint.

Two months later the messages resume. The student files a formal complaint. Both students receive
written notice. An investigator interviews them and two witnesses and collects screenshots. Each
student reviews the evidence and comments on the draft report. At the hearing, each adviser
questions the other party. The decision-maker finds the respondent responsible for stalking under
the policy's definition, applying the preponderance standard, and imposes a suspension. The
respondent appeals, citing new evidence. The appeal officer finds that the evidence was available
earlier and upholds the outcome.

Nothing about this matter becomes public by name. One stalking incident may appear in the
university's annual crime statistics if it meets the Clery definitions and occurred within the
geography the law covers.

## What the documents look like

Each step produces records. Almost all of them are private.

- Disclosure. An intake note or report form in the Title IX office's case system. Private.
- Supportive measures. Emails, letters to departments and housing, the no-contact order. Private,
  and the measures themselves are kept confidential where possible.
- Formal complaint. A signed document. Private.
- Notice of allegations. A letter to both parties. Private.
- Evidence and investigative report. Interview notes, collected materials, the report and the
  parties' written responses. Private. Student records are generally [education
  records](/glossary/education-record/) protected by [FERPA](/law/ferpa/).
- Hearing. A recording or transcript. Private.
- Written determination and appeal decision. Sent to the parties. Private, although federal law
  allows the complainant to be told the outcome, and FERPA permits limited disclosure of final
  results in some categories of cases.

A few related records are public. The university's policy and grievance procedures are published.
Under the 2020 framework, the materials used to train Title IX staff must be posted on the
university's website. The [annual security report](/glossary/annual-security-report/) describes
disciplinary procedures for dating violence, domestic violence, sexual assault and stalking, and
reports aggregate crime counts. Some universities publish annual Title IX reports with case counts
and outcomes; these are voluntary or required by state law, and their categories vary. Records may
surface later in a lawsuit or a federal investigation, often redacted.

## What this process is not

It is not a criminal case. A complainant may report to police, pursue a campus process, do both or
do neither. The standards, the decision-makers and the consequences are different, as explained in
[civil versus criminal](/glossary/civil-vs-criminal/). A campus finding is not a conviction, and the
absence of charges says nothing about the campus outcome.

## Questions worth asking

- Which version of the policy applies to conduct on a given date, and where is the current text?
- Who at this university is a confidential resource, and who is required to report to the Title IX
  office?
- Which standard of evidence does the policy use, and does it differ for students and employees?
- Does the university run a separate process for conduct outside Title IX's jurisdiction, and how
  does it differ?
- Does the university publish an annual report of complaints and outcomes, and how does it define
  its categories?
- Where are the Title IX training materials posted?
