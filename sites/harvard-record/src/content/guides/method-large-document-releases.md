Courts, agencies and legislative bodies sometimes release documents by the thousand. Such releases
are valuable and unusually easy to misread. The volume invites counting, and counts sound
authoritative. This guide explains what the common counts really measure and how to describe a large
release without claiming more than it shows.

## Understand the release before you search it

Spend time on the release as a whole first.

- Who released it, under what authority, and why? A court unsealing, a response to a records request
  and a committee publication are selected by different rules.
- What does the releasing body say is included and excluded? Look for a cover letter, an index or a
  production log.
- How are items identified? Many productions stamp each page with a sequential identifier, often
  called a Bates number. Use these identifiers in your notes and citations.
- How was text made searchable? Scanned pages are usually converted by optical character recognition
  (OCR), software that reads images of text. OCR makes errors, especially with names, handwriting
  and poor scans.

A release is a selection. It contains what the releasing body held and chose or was required to
release. It is not a complete record of anyone's dealings.

## A search hit is not a finding

Typing a name into a search box and reporting the number of results is the most common error with
large releases. A hit means a string of characters appears on a page. It does not tell you why.

A name can appear because the person:

- sent or received a message;
- was copied on a message, or was on a mailing list;
- was mentioned by someone else, accurately or not;
- appeared in a news article, a contact list or a calendar that was swept into the release;
- shares a name with someone else entirely.

Search also misses. OCR errors, nicknames, initials, misspellings and redactions all hide relevant
pages. So a hit count is neither a ceiling nor a floor.

The rule is simple. Read every page you intend to rely on, in full, with the pages around it. Report
what the pages say, not how many there are.

## Duplicates inflate everything

Large releases are full of duplicates. The same email may appear in the sender's mailbox, each
recipient's mailbox, as an attachment, and again as an exhibit. Productions from several custodians
(the people or offices whose files were collected) multiply this. Near-duplicates, such as a draft
and a final version, add more.

Before counting anything, deduplicate. Group items by sender, recipients, date, time and subject,
then compare the text. Count distinct documents, and say that you did. "The name appears on 300
pages" and "there are 12 distinct messages, repeated across the release" are very different
statements about the same material.

## Threads repeat their own history

An email thread is a chain of replies. Each reply usually quotes the messages before it. A
ten-message exchange can therefore reproduce the first message ten times. Counting occurrences in a
thread counts the quoting, not the contact.

For each thread:

1. find the latest message, which usually contains the fullest chain;
2. list the distinct messages inside it, with sender, recipients and date for each;
3. note who actually wrote something, as opposed to who was only quoted or copied;
4. check whether any message in the chain is missing, which happens when a reply trims earlier text.

Attribute words to the person who wrote them. Text quoted lower in a thread belongs to its original
author, not to the person who forwarded it.

## Describe presence accurately

When a person appears in a release, say in what capacity. Useful distinctions are author, direct
recipient, copied recipient, person mentioned by others, and name appearing in an attached or
third-party document. Being mentioned in a document, or receiving one, is not evidence of wrongdoing
or of any relationship beyond what the page shows. Where you name someone who appears only as a
recipient or bystander, say plainly that no wrongdoing is implied. [The guide on writing about
named people](/guides/method-writing-about-named-people/) in this series covers this further.

## Keep each item at its evidence state

A release mostly contains communications, and communications mostly show stated intent: plans,
offers, requests, invitations. An invitation is not attendance. A request is not agreement. A draft
is not a signed document. Move a claim to documented activity only when a page records the act, and
to verified implementation only when an official finding or independent result confirms it.
Definitions are on the [methodology page](/methodology/).

## Wording for counts and scope

If you report numbers, define them.

- State the unit: pages, files or distinct documents.
- State the method: the search terms used, whether you deduplicated, and whether you read the items.
- State the date of your search and the version of the release, since releases are sometimes
  reissued.
- Prefer "at least" to an exact figure when OCR or redaction may hide items.
- Do not compare counts between people as a measure of closeness. Counts reflect whose files were
  collected as much as who communicated.

## Cite to the official release

Cite the releasing body's own copy with the page identifier. If you cannot yet link the original,
cite established reporting that describes the document, graded as reporting. Unofficial searchable
mirrors are convenient for finding things, but they are leads, not sources, and anything found there
should be confirmed against the official copy before use.

## Limits to state openly

Tell the reader what portion of the release you read, what you searched for and what you did not
examine. A large release can support narrow, well-cited statements about particular documents. It
rarely supports general statements about how often, how close or how much.
