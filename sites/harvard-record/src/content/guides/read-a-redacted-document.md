A redaction is a deliberate removal of part of a document before release, usually shown as a black
or white box. Redacted documents are frustrating, and the urge to fill the gaps is strong. The
disciplined approach is to learn everything the document offers about its own redactions, read what
remains with care, and leave the hidden text unknown.

## Who redacted it, and under what rule

The same black box can mean very different things depending on who applied it.

- A government agency responding to a public records request. Under the federal Freedom of
  Information Act, an agency must withhold only what falls within specific statutory exemptions and
  release the rest. Agencies commonly mark each redaction with the exemption relied on, such as
  "(b)(6)" for personal privacy. State public records laws have their own exemptions.
- A party in litigation. Court rules require certain personal identifiers, such as full Social
  Security numbers and the names of minors, to be removed from public filings. Parties may also
  redact privileged or confidential material, sometimes under a protective order from the court.
- A congressional committee or an institution releasing documents voluntarily. No statute governs
  these redactions. The releasing body decides.
- A journalist or publisher, to protect a source or a private individual.

Find any cover letter, index or note that came with the release. It often explains the categories of
redaction.

## Common reasons for redaction

- Personal privacy: names of private individuals, contact details, medical information, identifiers.
- Protection of victims, minors and witnesses.
- Legal privilege: communications with lawyers and lawyers' work product.
- Student and personnel records protected by law.
- Ongoing law enforcement matters.
- Trade secrets and confidential commercial information.
- Internal deliberations, where an exemption covers them.
- Material judged irrelevant to the request or the case, sometimes marked "non-responsive."

Most redaction is routine and protects people who have done nothing wrong. A redaction is not a sign
of concealment in itself.

## Read what the redaction tells you

1. Labels. Look for exemption codes, the words "privileged" or "redacted," or a footnote. Look up
   the code in the statute or the agency's guide.
2. Size and position. The length of a box gives a rough idea of how much text is missing: a word, a
   line, a paragraph, a page. Its position in a sentence may show its grammatical role, for example
   a name before a verb.
3. Pattern. If a redaction of the same length appears everywhere a particular role is mentioned, the
   same item is probably being removed each time. Some releases substitute consistent labels such as
   "Individual 1."
4. Whole pages. Pages "withheld in full" should be counted and noted.
5. Structure. Headers, dates, page numbers and paragraph numbers that survive show the shape of the
   document and whether anything is missing between pages.
6. Withholding logs. In litigation, a privilege log lists withheld documents with date, author,
   recipients and the basis claimed. In public records cases that reach court, agencies may file a
   similar index.

## What you may fairly infer

- That information of a stated category was removed, where a label says so.
- Approximately how much was removed.
- That a redacted element recurs, where the pattern supports it.
- That the releasing body considered the material to fall within the stated basis.

## What you may not infer

- The hidden words. Context that seems to point to one name usually fits several.
- That the hidden material is damaging. Privacy redactions cover innocent bystanders as a matter of
  course.
- That an unredacted name was left visible because the person is implicated. Decisions about whose
  names are released follow rules about public officials, consent and prior disclosure that have
  little to do with culpability.

Identifying a person behind a redaction by deduction can be wrong, and where the person is a victim
or a private individual it can cause serious harm. Do not publish such identifications.

## Technical cautions

- Poorly made redactions sometimes leave the text recoverable in the file. Reading or publishing
  recovered text raises ethical and sometimes legal questions, particularly where the redaction
  protects a victim or a private person. The fact that text can be recovered does not make it yours
  to use.

## Compare versions

The same document is sometimes released more than once: by different agencies, in different cases,
or again after an appeal. Later versions may reveal more. Where two versions exist, compare them,
cite the one you quote, and do not assume the less redacted version is complete.

## How to seek more

- Under public records laws, a requester can usually file an administrative appeal challenging
  redactions, and then go to court. Deadlines apply and vary.
- In litigation, the press and public may ask the court to unseal records. Courts weigh the public's
  interest in access against the reasons for secrecy.

## How to describe a redacted document

- Say that it is redacted and, if known, by whom and on what stated basis.
- Quote only what is visible.
- Mark omissions in your own quotation clearly, and distinguish your omissions from the document's
  redactions.
- Do not present a guess as a reading. If you must mention a possibility, label it as unverified.
  Better still, leave it out.

## What a redacted document can and cannot tell you

It can tell you everything its visible text says, plus the fact, extent and stated grounds of what
was removed. It cannot tell you what lies under the boxes, and it cannot tell you why the material
was sensitive beyond the label given.

## Common mistakes

- Filling in a name from context.
- Treating the presence of redactions as evidence of wrongdoing.
- Missing withheld pages because the page numbers were not checked.
