Form 990 is the annual information return that most tax-exempt organizations in the United States
file with the Internal Revenue Service. It is a public document. For a private university, an
affiliated foundation or a charity, it is often the most detailed financial disclosure available. It
is long, and most of its value sits in a few parts and schedules.

## Who files which form

- Form 990. Filed by larger public charities and other exempt organizations, including most private
  colleges and universities.
- Form 990-EZ and Form 990-N. Shorter versions for smaller organizations.
- Form 990-PF. Filed by private foundations, including most family foundations. It lists every grant
  the foundation made, with recipient and amount, which makes it very useful for tracing giving from
  the donor's side.

Not everyone files. Churches and certain religious organizations are exempt from filing. State
universities, as arms of government, generally do not file a Form 990, though the separate
foundations that raise money for them usually do.

## Where to find one

- The IRS offers a public search tool for exempt organizations with copies of returns.
- Several nonprofit data services publish returns at no charge, often with easier search.
- The organization itself must provide copies of its three most recent returns on request.

Search by legal name or by employer identification number (EIN). A university may comprise several
legal entities, each with its own EIN and its own return.

## Check the period first

The top of page one states the tax year, which usually follows the organization's fiscal year, not
the calendar year. Many universities end their fiscal year in the middle of the calendar year.
Returns are filed months after year-end, extensions are common, and public posting takes longer. The
newest return available usually describes a period that ended a year or two ago.

## The core form

- Part I, summary. Mission, revenue, expenses and net assets for the current and prior year.
- Part IV, checklist of required schedules. A series of yes or no answers that show which schedules
  are attached. It is a quick map of the return.
- Part VI, governance. Board size and independence, and whether the organization has written
  policies on conflicts of interest, whistleblowers and document retention. These are disclosures,
  not legal requirements, and a "yes" says a policy exists, not that it works.
- Part VII, compensation. Officers, directors, trustees, key employees and the highest-paid
  employees, along with the highest-paid independent contractors.
- Part VIII, revenue. Contributions and grants, program service revenue (tuition, for a university),
  investment income and other sources.
- Part IX, expenses, by type and by function: program, management and fundraising.
- Part X, balance sheet.

## The schedules that matter most

- Schedule A. Explains why the organization qualifies as a public charity.
- Schedule B. Lists major contributors. In the public copy of a public charity's return, donor names
  and addresses are withheld. Amounts may appear. Private foundations' contributors are disclosed.
- Schedule D. Supplemental financial statements, including endowment balances and activity, and
  donor-advised funds if the organization holds any.
- Schedule F. Activities outside the United States.
- Schedule I. Grants made to organizations and individuals in the United States.
- Schedule J. Detail on compensation for those listed in Part VII, including bonuses, deferred
  compensation and certain benefits.
- Schedule L. Transactions with interested persons, such as loans to officers or business dealings
  with board members and their families.
- Schedule O. Narrative explanations. Read it. Answers to governance questions and unusual items are
  explained here.
- Schedule R. Related organizations: subsidiaries, affiliates and partnerships. This is the map of
  the wider group.

## A reading routine

1. Confirm the entity, the EIN and the tax year.
2. Read Part I for scale.
3. Use Part IV to see which schedules exist.
4. Read Schedule R to learn what other entities belong to the group, then look for their returns.
5. Read Parts VII and Schedule J for compensation, and Schedule L for insider transactions.
6. Read Part VIII and Schedule D for contributions and endowment.
7. Read Schedule O for explanations.
8. Compare three or more years. Changes are more informative than a single year.

## What a Form 990 can tell you

- The size and financial structure of the organization.
- What its leaders and highest-paid staff were paid.
- Its total contributions for the year, and its endowment activity.
- Its related entities and certain insider transactions.
- For a private foundation, exactly whom it gave money to and how much.

## What it cannot tell you

- Who gave to a public charity. Donor names are not in the public copy.
- The terms or purpose of any individual gift.
- Gifts by school, center or lab within a university. The return covers the legal entity as a whole.
- Anything recent.
- Whether a stated policy is followed.

The return is self-reported and signed under penalty of perjury. It is not audited by the IRS on
filing. For audited figures, read the organization's audited financial statements, which many
universities publish separately. The two use somewhat different accounting conventions and will not
match line for line.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the wrong entity's return, or assuming one return covers a whole university system.
- Double counting compensation. Reportable compensation, other compensation and deferred amounts can
  overlap across years, as the Schedule J notes explain.
- Treating total contributions as cash received from individuals. The figure includes grants and may
  include noncash gifts.
- Missing a foundation's Form 990-PF, which may show a gift the recipient's return does not itemize.

Separate federal rules require colleges to report certain foreign gifts and contracts to the
Department of Education. That reporting is distinct from the Form 990. See /law/ for the explainer,
and /glossary/ for terms such as donor-advised fund.
