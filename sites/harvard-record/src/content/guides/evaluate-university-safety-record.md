No single number tells you whether a campus is safe. A fair evaluation uses several public
documents, each of which measures something different, and pays as much attention to how the
institution responds to reports as to how many reports it receives.

## What you are trying to learn

Three separate questions are easy to blur together.

1. How often does harm happen to students? Surveys come closest to answering this.
2. How often is harm reported to the institution? Official crime statistics and office reports
   answer this.
3. What happens after a report? Policies, annual reports from the misconduct office, and enforcement
   records answer this.

Keep the three apart as you read. A document that answers one of them usually says little about the
other two.

## Gather the documents

Most of these can be found on the university's own website. Searching the site for the document name
usually works.

1. The annual security report. Federal law (the Clery Act) requires colleges that take part in
   federal student aid programs to publish one each year. It contains three years of crime
   statistics and a set of policy statements. See [the guide to reading a campus security
   report](/guides/read-a-campus-security-report/).
2. The daily crime log, if the campus has a police or security department. It lists recent reported
   incidents and must be open to the public.
3. The most recent campus climate survey, if one exists. This is an anonymous survey of students
   about experiences such as sexual harassment and assault, and about trust in the institution.
4. The annual report of the office that handles sexual misconduct and discrimination complaints
   (often called the Title IX office). Not every institution publishes one. Where it exists, it may
   show how many complaints were received, how many went to a formal process, and how they ended.
5. The current policies themselves: the sexual misconduct policy, the discrimination policy, and the
   student handbook's disciplinary procedures.
6. Federal enforcement records. The U.S. Department of Education publishes Clery crime data for all
   reporting institutions in a searchable tool, publishes Clery program review findings, and its
   Office for Civil Rights publishes lists of open investigations and many resolution agreements.
7. The student newspaper's archive. See [the guide on using the student newspaper as a
   record](/guides/student-newspaper-as-a-record/).

## Read them in a useful order

1. Start with the climate survey, if there is one. Note the response rate, the definitions used, and
   the time period each question covers.
2. Read the annual security report next. Compare the number of reported offenses with what the
   survey suggests about prevalence. A large gap is normal everywhere, because most incidents are
   never reported to any official. The size of the gap, and whether it is narrowing, is informative.
3. Read the misconduct office's annual report. Look for how many reports led to a formal complaint,
   how long cases took, and what outcomes followed.
4. Read the policies with a specific scenario in mind. Trace what would happen, step by step, if a
   student reported an incident involving another student, and then an incident involving a faculty
   member.
5. Check the federal records for open investigations, past findings, and any agreement the
   institution signed. Note the dates. An old agreement that was completed tells you something
   different from a recent one that is still being monitored.

## Signs of a serious institution

- Documents are easy to find, dated, and published on a regular schedule.
- Survey results are published in full, including unflattering findings and the survey instrument.
- The institution explains what it changed in response to earlier results.
- Confidential resources (people who can talk with a student without triggering a report) are
  clearly separated from reporting offices, and both are described plainly.
- Policies say who decides each step, what the time frames are, and how to appeal.
- Reports cover complaints against faculty and staff, not only complaints between students.

## Signs that call for more questions

- Zero reported sexual offenses for several years at a large residential campus. This more often
  reflects barriers to reporting than an absence of incidents.
- A climate survey that was conducted but never published, or published only as a summary.
- Policies that are hard to locate or that contradict each other across schools within the same
  university.
- Numbers that change sharply from one year to the next with no explanation.

None of these proves a problem. Each is a reason to ask the institution directly.

## What the record cannot tell you

- Official statistics cover reported incidents in defined places. They do not cover most private
  off-campus housing.
- A finding or an agreement describes a past period. It does not describe current practice.
- An absence of public enforcement records does not show that nothing went wrong. Many complaints
  are never filed with an outside agency.
- Comparisons between institutions are difficult. See [the guide to comparing two universities' data
  fairly](/guides/compare-sexual-misconduct-data-fairly/) before drawing conclusions from
  differences.

## Common mistakes

- Ranking campuses by raw counts of reported crimes.
- Treating a published complaint or an open investigation as a finding. An investigation is a
  process, not a conclusion.
- Reading only the statistics and skipping the policy statements, which describe what the
  institution has committed to do.
- Relying on a single year.
