Disciplining a professor is slower and more procedural than disciplining most employees. Tenure,
academic freedom and shared governance give faculty members procedural protections that other staff
do not have. At the same time, federal law requires universities to respond to certain kinds of
complaints in particular ways. The result is a set of overlapping processes that differ by type of
conduct, by the faculty member's appointment and by institution. This primer describes the common
structure at U.S. research universities. The governing rules are found in each institution's faculty
handbook and policies, and some federal rules have changed repeatedly in recent years, so the
current text should always be checked.

## Different conduct, different processes

The first question in any faculty matter is which process applies. Universities usually route
complaints to separate tracks.

- Sexual harassment and sex-based discrimination. These fall under [Title IX](/law/title-ix/) and
  the institution's policies, administered by the [Title IX
  coordinator](/glossary/title-ix-coordinator/). Federal regulations set procedural requirements for
  certain cases; see [Title IX regulations](/law/title-ix-regulations/). Those regulations have been
  rewritten more than once, and litigation has affected which version applies.
- Other discrimination and harassment. Complaints based on race, national origin and other protected
  characteristics are handled under civil rights and employment laws such as [Title
  VI](/law/title-vi/) and [Title VII](/law/title-vii/), usually by an equal opportunity or civil
  rights office.
- Research misconduct. Fabrication, falsification and plagiarism in research are handled by a
  [research integrity officer](/glossary/research-integrity-officer/) under institutional policy
  and, for federally funded work, federal rules such as the [PHS research misconduct
  policy](/law/phs-research-misconduct-policy/). See [research
  misconduct](/glossary/research-misconduct/).
- Professional conduct. Other conduct, such as abusive behavior toward students, conflicts of
  interest, neglect of duties or breaches of university policy, is usually handled under a
  [professional conduct policy](/glossary/professional-conduct-policy/) or the faculty handbook,
  often through the dean.

A single situation can involve several tracks at once. They may run in sequence or in parallel, and
the findings of one can feed into another.

## How a complaint starts

A complaint can come from a student, a colleague, staff, an outside party or the institution itself.
Some employees are required to pass on certain information; see [responsible
employee](/glossary/responsible-employee/). A person can often choose between making a [formal
complaint](/glossary/formal-complaint/), which starts an investigation, and seeking [informal
resolution](/glossary/informal-resolution/) or confidential support. The [ombuds
office](/glossary/ombuds-office/) and other [confidential
resources](/glossary/confidential-resource/) can advise without starting a process. The [disclosure
versus formal complaint](/glossary/disclosure-vs-formal-complaint/) entry explains the difference.

## Interim steps

While a matter is pending, an institution may take interim steps. These can include [supportive
measures](/glossary/supportive-measures/) for the person who complained, such as changes to
supervision or schedules; a [no-contact order](/glossary/no-contact-order/); restrictions on the
faculty member's access to a lab, students or campus; and [administrative
leave](/glossary/administrative-leave/). Leave during an investigation is usually described as
non-disciplinary. It does not mean a finding has been made. The [interim
measures](/glossary/interim-measures/) entry covers the range.

## Investigation and findings

An investigator, sometimes a trained staff member and sometimes an outside investigator, interviews
the parties and witnesses and gathers evidence. Depending on the track, the faculty member may
receive written notice of the allegations, a chance to respond, access to the evidence, an advisor
and sometimes a hearing. Findings are made under a stated standard of proof, typically
[preponderance of the evidence](/glossary/preponderance-of-the-evidence/) or [clear and convincing
evidence](/glossary/clear-and-convincing-evidence/). Which standard applies varies by institution
and by process, and for some processes it is set or constrained by federal rules.

The difference between an [allegation and a finding](/glossary/finding-vs-allegation/) is
fundamental. A complaint, a news report or a pending investigation establishes only that a claim was
made.

## Faculty review and sanctions

Once a violation is found, a sanction decision follows. At many institutions, significant sanctions
against faculty require review by a faculty body, such as a [faculty conduct
committee](/glossary/faculty-conduct-committee/) or a hearing panel drawn from the faculty, before a
dean, provost or president decides. The faculty handbook sets out who recommends and who decides.

The range of [faculty sanctions](/glossary/faculty-sanctions/) usually includes a written warning or
reprimand, required training, loss of a leadership role or [endowed
chair](/glossary/endowed-chair/), restrictions on supervising students, loss of salary increases or
a fine, suspension with or without pay, demotion, and dismissal. Not every institution uses every
sanction. Faculty can usually appeal.

## Tenure and dismissal

[Tenure](/glossary/tenure/) is a commitment to continuous appointment that can be ended only for
adequate cause, financial exigency or program discontinuance, under procedures the institution has
adopted. It is not a guarantee of employment regardless of conduct. Dismissal of a tenured professor
for cause generally requires notice of the charges, a hearing before a faculty committee, the right
to present evidence and respond, and a decision by senior leadership or the board. Many institutions
model these procedures on long-standing recommendations of the American Association of University
Professors, though each adopts its own version.

These procedures take time. Faced with a likely dismissal, faculty members sometimes resign or
retire, often by negotiated agreement. A resignation can end the institution's process before a
final finding. Such agreements may include confidentiality terms, a neutral reference or the
retention of titles such as [emeritus status](/glossary/emeritus-status/), depending on
institutional policy.

Faculty without tenure, including tenure-track, non-tenure-track and visiting faculty, typically
have fewer procedural protections. An appointment can sometimes be allowed to expire rather than
being ended for cause.

## Retaliation and academic freedom

Anyone who complains or takes part in a process is protected from
[retaliation](/glossary/retaliation/) by institutional policy and, in many contexts, by law. Faculty
members are protected by [academic freedom](/glossary/academic-freedom/), which shields teaching,
research and some speech from discipline. Conduct processes are not supposed to punish protected
expression, and disputes about whether conduct or speech is at issue are common.

## Confidentiality

Most outcomes are not announced. Personnel records are generally confidential, and student records
are protected by [FERPA](/law/ferpa/). Parties may receive notice of outcomes in some processes. A
sanction becomes public mainly when the institution chooses to announce it, when it is reported,
when litigation follows, or when public records law reaches it at a public university. The guide on
[reading a faculty sanction announcement](/guides/read-a-faculty-sanction-announcement/) explains
what such announcements tend to include and omit.

## A worked hypothetical

A graduate student reports to a department chair that a professor repeatedly made demeaning comments
and threatened to cut funding. The chair, as a responsible employee, refers the matter to the
appropriate office. Because some comments were sex-based, the Title IX office assesses whether its
process applies, and the remaining conduct goes to the dean under the professional conduct policy.

The dean places the professor on administrative leave from supervising students and reassigns the
student to a new adviser. An outside investigator interviews eight people and finds, by a
preponderance of the evidence, that the professor violated the professional conduct policy. A
faculty committee reviews the record and recommends a two-year bar on supervising students and
removal from a center directorship. The provost adopts the recommendation. The professor appeals and
loses. No public announcement is made. The student is told the outcome in general terms.

## What the documents look like

- Policies and faculty handbook: usually public or available to the campus.
- Complaint or intake record: internal, held by the office that receives it.
- Notice of allegations: a letter to the faculty member. Internal.
- Interim measures letters: notices of leave, restrictions or no-contact orders. Internal.
- Investigation report: the investigator's findings and evidence summary. Internal, sometimes shared
  with parties.
- Faculty committee report and recommendation: internal.
- Decision and sanction letter: internal, placed in the personnel file.
- Appeal decision: internal.
- Separation or resignation agreement: internal, often confidential.
- Public statement: occasional.

## Questions worth asking

1. Which policy and process applied, and why that one?
2. Was the action an interim measure or a sanction following a finding?
3. What standard of proof was used?
4. Did a faculty body review the matter, and what did it recommend?
5. What sanction was imposed, by whom, and was it appealed?
6. Did the faculty member resign or retire before a finding, and on what terms?
7. Which documents would show the outcome, and who holds them?
