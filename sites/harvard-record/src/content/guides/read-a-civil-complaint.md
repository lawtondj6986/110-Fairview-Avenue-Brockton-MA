A civil complaint is the document that starts a lawsuit. In it, the plaintiff (the party suing) sets
out what the defendant (the party sued) is said to have done and what the plaintiff wants the court
to order. It is one side's account, written by that side's lawyers, before any evidence has been
tested. Complaints are often detailed and vivid, and they are frequently reported as news. Reading
one accurately starts with knowing what stage it represents.

## Where a complaint sits in a lawsuit

1. Complaint filed. Allegations only.
2. Response. The defendant files an answer, admitting or denying each allegation, or a motion to
   dismiss arguing that the complaint fails as a matter of law.
3. Discovery. The parties exchange documents and take sworn testimony.
4. Summary judgment. A party may ask the court to decide all or part of the case without trial on
   the ground that the key facts are not in real dispute.
5. Trial, or settlement, or dismissal.
6. Judgment, and possibly appeal.

Most civil cases end without a trial, through dismissal or settlement. A settlement usually resolves
the case with no ruling on whether the allegations were true.

## The parts of a complaint

- Caption. The court, the names of the parties and the case number. A party may appear under a
  pseudonym such as "Jane Doe" where the court allows it.
- Introduction or nature of the action. A summary written to persuade.
- Parties. Who each party is.
- Jurisdiction and venue. Why this court can hear the case.
- Factual allegations. Numbered paragraphs telling the plaintiff's version of events.
- Counts or causes of action. Each legal claim, such as negligence or breach of contract, with the
  facts said to support it.
- Prayer for relief. What the plaintiff asks for: money damages, a court order, fees.
- Jury demand, signature of counsel, and sometimes exhibits.

Read the counts and the prayer for relief as well as the narrative. They tell you what the case is
legally about, which is sometimes narrower than the story suggests.

## Language to recognize

- "Alleges." The plaintiff asserts it. Nothing more is implied.
- "Upon information and belief." The plaintiff does not have direct knowledge and is asserting what
  they believe to be true, often about matters inside the defendant's control.
- "At all relevant times." A drafting phrase fixing a time period.
- "Verified complaint." One in which a party has sworn to the truth of the facts. Most complaints
  are not verified. They are signed by lawyers, who must have a good-faith basis for the claims
  under court rules.
- "Amended complaint." A later version that replaces an earlier one. Always check that you are
  reading the current version, and note what changed.

## Why an allegation is not a finding

- No one has yet tested it. The defendant has not responded, no witness has been cross-examined, and
  no judge or jury has weighed the evidence.
- It is written by an advocate. The duty of the plaintiff's lawyer is to present the client's case
  as strongly as the rules allow.
- Surviving a motion to dismiss does not change this. On such a motion, the court assumes the
  allegations are true for the sake of argument and asks only whether they would amount to a legal
  claim. A ruling that the case may proceed is not a ruling that the facts are as alleged. A court
  opinion at this stage will recite the allegations, and those recitals are easy to mistake for
  findings.
- A settlement does not convert allegations into facts.

A finding exists when a judge or jury, after considering evidence, determines what happened, or when
a party formally admits a fact.

## What a complaint can tell you

- That a lawsuit was filed, by whom, against whom, where and when.
- Exactly what is alleged, in the plaintiff's own framing.
- Which legal claims are made and what remedy is sought.
- Sometimes, primary documents attached as exhibits, such as contracts, letters or emails. An
  exhibit is evidence of its own contents, apart from the plaintiff's description of it.

## What it cannot tell you

- Whether any allegation is true.
- What the defendant says. Look for the answer or the motion to dismiss on the docket.
- How strong the evidence is.
- What the case is worth. The amount demanded is the plaintiff's request and is not a prediction.

## A reading routine

1. Note the court, the filing date and the case number.
2. Check the docket for later filings and the current status. See [the guide to reading a court
   docket](/guides/read-a-court-docket/).
3. Read the counts and the relief sought.
4. Read the factual allegations, marking each "upon information and belief."
5. Read any exhibits separately from the complaint's description of them.
6. Find the defendant's response and read it with the same care.

## How to describe a complaint accurately

- Attribute every allegation: "the complaint alleges," "according to the lawsuit."
- Keep the attribution through the whole passage, not just the first sentence.
- State the stage of the case and the outcome, if there is one.
- Include the defendant's response or note that none has been filed yet.
- Avoid "revealed," "showed" or "exposed" for the contents of a complaint. Those verbs assert truth.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting allegations as facts.
- Treating the damages figure as an award.
- Reading a denial of a motion to dismiss as a ruling on the facts.
- Citing an original complaint after it has been amended or dismissed.
- Assuming that a named defendant did what is alleged because a court accepted the filing. Courts
  accept filings without assessing their merit.
