A campus climate survey asks students, anonymously, about their experiences and perceptions: sexual
harassment and assault, knowledge of resources, and trust in how the institution responds. Because
it does not depend on anyone having made a report, it is the best available source on how common
these experiences are. Its figures are estimates, and they need to be read with the method in hand.

## Read the method section first

Before looking at any result, find these five things.

1. Who was surveyed. All students, or a sample? Undergraduates only, or graduate and professional
   students as well?
2. Response rate. The share of invited students who completed the survey. Rates for these surveys
   are often modest.
3. When it was fielded. The date matters for everything that follows.
4. Who designed and ran it. An institution's own office, an outside research firm, or a multi-campus
   consortium using a shared questionnaire.
5. Weighting. Whether results were adjusted so that respondents resemble the student body in
   characteristics such as gender and year.

If the report does not state the response rate or reproduce the questions, treat its numbers with
more caution.

## Understand nonresponse

When only some students respond, the question is whether those who responded differ from those who
did not. Students who have experienced harm may be more likely to respond because the topic matters
to them, or less likely because it is painful. Weighting corrects for known demographic differences.
It cannot correct for differences in experience.

A low response rate does not make a survey worthless. It widens the uncertainty, and it means small
differences should not be over-read.

## Check the definitions

Survey terms are not legal terms.

- Good surveys ask about specific behaviors, described plainly, instead of asking whether the
  respondent was "assaulted" or "harassed." Behaviorally specific questions capture experiences that
  people do not always label.
- "Nonconsensual sexual contact" in a survey usually covers a range from unwanted touching to
  penetration, and a range of tactics, such as physical force, incapacitation or coercion. Reports
  often give figures for several combinations. Know which one you are reading.
- Survey categories do not match criminal offenses, the institution's policy definitions or Clery
  Act categories.

When quoting a figure, quote the definition with it.

## Check the time frame

The same survey may ask about experiences since enrolling, during the current academic year, or in
the past twelve months.

- "Since enrolling" figures accumulate. Seniors have had more years of exposure than first-year
  students, so the figure rises with class year by construction.
- Annual figures are better for tracking change.

Do not compare a since-enrollment figure with an annual one.

## Check who is in the denominator

A percentage is a share of some group. Find out which.

- All respondents, or only women, or only undergraduates.
- All respondents, or only those who answered that question.
- All students, or only those who reported an incident. Questions such as "did you tell anyone" are
  asked only of the latter group.

Results nearly always differ by gender, by undergraduate or graduate status, and for students who
identify as transgender, nonbinary, or lesbian, gay or bisexual, and for students with disabilities.
An overall average conceals these differences.

## Small groups

Estimates for small subgroups rest on few respondents and are imprecise. Reports often suppress
cells below a minimum size to protect anonymity. Look for confidence intervals or margins of error,
and where none are given, be wary of fine distinctions.

## The sections beyond prevalence

The most actionable results are often not the headline figures.

- Whether students who experienced harm told anyone, whom they told, and why some did not.
- Whether students know where to get help and how to report.
- Whether students believe the institution would take a report seriously and handle it fairly.
- Who was responsible: another student, a faculty or staff member, someone outside the institution.
- Where incidents occurred.
- Harassment by faculty or advisers, which matters especially for graduate students.

## Comparing surveys

- Across years at one institution: valid if the questions, population and method were the same.
  Check whether the report says so. A change in response rate between rounds also affects
  comparison.
- Across institutions: valid mainly where both used the same instrument in the same period.
  Different question wording alone can produce large differences.
- Against official statistics: a survey estimate and a count of reported crimes measure different
  things. The gap between them is itself informative. See [the guide on comparing two
  universities' data fairly](/guides/compare-sexual-misconduct-data-fairly/).

## What a climate survey can tell you

- An estimate of how common defined experiences are among respondents, with stated uncertainty.
- Which groups report higher rates.
- How much of what happens is disclosed to the institution.
- How much students know and how much they trust.

## What it cannot tell you

- The facts of any individual incident. Responses are anonymous and unverified, which is by design.
- A precise prevalence for the whole student body, particularly at low response rates.
- Whether a change between years reflects a change in behavior, in willingness to disclose on a
  survey, or in who responded.
- Anything about legal responsibility.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting a figure without its definition, population or time frame.
- Applying a figure for undergraduate women to all students, or the reverse.
- Treating a survey estimate as a count of crimes.
- Dismissing the whole survey because the response rate is low.
- Reading only the institution's summary. Summaries select. The full tables and the questionnaire
  are where the detail is.
