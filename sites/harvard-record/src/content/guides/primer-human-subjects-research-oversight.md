Research that involves people, or information and samples that can be linked to them, is subject to
a system of prior approval and continuing oversight. In the United States the main rules are the
federal policy known as the [Common Rule](/law/common-rule/), which applies to research funded or
conducted by many federal agencies, and the [Food and Drug Administration's human-subjects
rules](/law/fda-human-subjects-rules/), which apply to studies of drugs, devices and biological
products. Many institutions apply the same standards to all research they host, whatever the
funding. This primer explains how the system works in practice.

## The ethical basis

The rules rest on the principles of respect for persons, beneficence and justice set out in the
[Belmont Report](/glossary/belmont-report/). In practice these become requirements for informed
consent, a favorable balance of risks and benefits, and fair selection of participants.

## The institutional review board

An [institutional review board](/glossary/institutional-review-board/) (IRB) is a committee that
reviews research involving people before it begins and while it runs. It includes scientists, at
least one member whose main concerns are not scientific, and at least one member not otherwise
affiliated with the institution. An institution that receives covered federal funding gives the
government a written assurance, often a Federalwide Assurance, that it will follow the rules and has
an IRB to apply them. Institutions may rely on another institution's IRB, or on an independent
commercial IRB. For many federally funded studies at several sites, the rules now expect a single
IRB to review the study for all of them.

The IRB has real power. It can approve a study, require changes, or disapprove it. It can suspend or
terminate approval of a study that is not being run as approved or that has caused unexpected
serious harm.

## Step one: is it human-subjects research?

The first question is whether a project counts. Under the Common Rule, [human-subjects
research](/glossary/human-subjects-research/) is a systematic investigation designed to produce
generalizable knowledge that involves interacting with living people or obtaining identifiable
private information or identifiable biospecimens. Quality-improvement work, some public-information
projects and research on fully de-identified data may fall outside the definition. Institutions
usually require the IRB office, not the researcher, to make that call.

## Step two: the protocol

The researcher, usually the [principal investigator](/glossary/principal-investigator/), submits a
[research protocol](/glossary/research-protocol/): a document that states the question, the
participants and how they will be recruited, the procedures, the risks and how they will be reduced,
how data will be protected, and how consent will be obtained. Recruitment materials, questionnaires
and the [consent form](/glossary/consent-form/) are attached.

## Step three: the level of review

Not every study goes to the full committee.

- [Exempt research](/glossary/exempt-research/). Certain low-risk categories, such as some surveys
  and some uses of existing information, are exempt from most of the rules. Exempt does not mean
  unreviewed; someone at the institution usually confirms the exemption.
- Expedited review. A study that involves no more than minimal risk and fits a federally listed
  category may be approved by the IRB chair or by one or more experienced members the chair
  designates.
- Convened review. Everything else goes to a meeting of the board with a quorum present.

## Step four: the approval criteria

The IRB must find, among other things, that risks are minimized and reasonable in relation to
expected benefits, that selection of participants is equitable, that consent will be sought and
documented as required, that data and privacy are protected, and that additional safeguards exist
for groups vulnerable to coercion or [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence-in-research/).
Those groups include children, prisoners and others the rules identify as [vulnerable
populations](/glossary/vulnerable-populations/).

## Consent

[Informed consent](/glossary/informed-consent/) is a process, not a signature. The rules list the
information a participant must receive, including the purpose, the procedures, the foreseeable risks
and benefits, alternatives, how confidentiality is handled, and the fact that participation is
voluntary and may be stopped. Revisions to the Common Rule that took effect in recent years require
the form to begin with a concise summary of the key information. In some circumstances the IRB may
waive or alter consent requirements, for example for some minimal-risk research with records, if
specified conditions are met. FDA rules on waivers are narrower.

## Step five: running the study

Approval comes with conditions.

- Amendments. Any change to the approved study must be approved before it is carried out, except
  where needed to remove an immediate hazard to participants.
- Continuing review. Many studies must be reviewed again at intervals. The revised Common Rule
  removed this requirement for many minimal-risk studies; FDA-regulated studies still generally
  require it.
- Reporting. Investigators must promptly report unanticipated problems involving risks to
  participants or others, and serious or continuing noncompliance.
- Deviations. A [protocol deviation](/glossary/protocol-deviation/) is any departure from the
  approved plan, such as using an outdated consent form or a missed safety test. Institutions
  classify deviations by seriousness. Minor ones may be logged and summarized at continuing review.
  Serious ones go to the IRB promptly.

Where the IRB finds serious or continuing noncompliance, or suspends or terminates approval, the
institution must report it to the funding agency and, for federally supported research, to the
federal Office for Human Research Protections. FDA has its own reporting and inspection system for
the studies it regulates.

## A worked hypothetical

A research team plans to collect cheek swabs and a questionnaire from adult volunteers to study a
common trait. The IRB office decides the project is human-subjects research because the samples are
linked to names. The protocol goes to expedited review as minimal risk. The reviewer asks the team
to explain how long samples will be kept and whether participants will be told about unexpected
health-related results, known as [incidental findings](/glossary/incidental-findings/). The team
revises the consent form and receives approval.

Six months later a research assistant enrolls several participants using the old consent form, which
lacks the paragraph on sample storage. The team reports the deviation. The IRB requires the team to
contact those participants, give them the current form and ask whether they still wish to take part.
It classifies the event as noncompliance but not serious, and no outside report is required.

## What the documents look like

- Protocol and attachments. Submitted to the IRB. Not public. Private institutions have no general
  duty to release them; public institutions may be reachable under state records laws, which vary.
- IRB minutes and approval letters. Internal records the rules require the institution to keep. Not
  public.
- Consent forms. Given to participants. For many federally funded clinical trials, the revised
  Common Rule requires one approved consent form to be posted on a public federal website.
- Deviation and noncompliance reports. Internal. Reports to federal agencies may be obtainable
  through records requests, with redactions.
- Federal determination letters. The Office for Human Research Protections has published letters
  describing compliance findings at institutions.
- FDA inspection results and warning letters. Warning letters are published by FDA.
- Trial registrations. Many clinical trials must be registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, which is
  public and shows the planned design and, for some trials, results.
- Grant records. Federal grant databases show funding, not IRB status.

## Questions worth asking

- Which IRB reviewed the study, and under which rules: the Common Rule, FDA rules, or the
  institution's own policy?
- Was the study exempt, expedited or reviewed by the convened board?
- What did participants agree to about storage, sharing and future use of their data and samples?
- Were there amendments after approval, and were they approved before being put into effect?
- Were any deviations or noncompliance reported, and how were they classified?
- Is the study registered, and does the registered design match what was published?
