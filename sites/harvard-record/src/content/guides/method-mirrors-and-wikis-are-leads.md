When a large set of documents becomes public, unofficial copies appear quickly: searchable mirrors, shared folders, crowd-edited wikis, spreadsheets and summary threads. They are often easier to use than the official release, and many are built with care. They are still leads, not sources. A lead tells you where to look. A source is something you can cite as the basis for a claim. This guide explains the difference and how to convert one into the other.

## What these sites are good for

It would be wasteful to ignore them. Unofficial mirrors and wikis can:

- make scanned pages searchable when the official release is not;
- point you to a document number, a page identifier or a date you can then look up officially;
- collect references to reporting and filings in one place;
- suggest connections between documents that you can test for yourself.

Use them the way you would use a tip from a well-read stranger. The tip may be right. You still have to check it.

## Why they are not sources

Five problems recur, and none depends on bad faith.

Unknown custody. You usually cannot tell who uploaded a file, where they got it, or whether anything was changed on the way. An official release has an accountable publisher. A mirror generally does not.

Alteration and mixing. Files can be cropped, re-ordered, merged, annotated or re-typed. Mirrors sometimes combine an official release with material from other origins, such as older leaks or unrelated court cases, with no visible seam. A page found on a mirror may not be from the release it is said to belong to.

Text errors. Search on these sites usually depends on optical character recognition (OCR), which is software that reads images of text. OCR misreads names, numbers and dates. Summaries written from OCR text inherit those errors.

Instability. Pages are edited, moved and removed without notice or version history. A citation to a wiki page points at whatever the page says on the day a reader clicks, which may not be what you saw.

Interpretation presented as fact. Wikis and indexes add labels, tags and summaries. "Associate", "linked to" and similar tags are a contributor's reading, not the document's content. Tags are also where mistaken identities spread, when two people share a name.

There is a further consideration. Some mirrors host material that was sealed, improperly redacted or private. Relying on them can spread information about victims and bystanders that the official release protected. Citing only official copies keeps you on the right side of that line.

## Circular confirmation

A claim can look well supported because it appears in many places, when every instance traces back to one unverified page. A wiki cites a blog, the blog cites a social media post, and the post cites the wiki. Before treating two references as independent, follow each to its origin. If they meet at the same unverified point, you have one lead, counted twice.

## Converting a lead into a source

1. Extract the identifiers. From the mirror or wiki, note the case name and number, the document or exhibit number, the page identifier, the date and the stated author.
2. Go to the official origin. For court documents, that is the court's docket. For agency material, the agency's site or reading room. For institutional reports, the institution's own domain.
3. Obtain the official copy and compare it with the unofficial one, page by page for the passages you need.
4. If they match, cite the official copy. The mirror does not appear in your citation at all, though your private notes can record how you found the document.
5. If you cannot obtain the official copy, look for established reporting that describes the document and quotes the passage. Cite that reporting, graded as reporting.
6. If neither exists, you do not have a source. Keep the item in your notes as an open lead and publish nothing that depends on it.

## How to treat what remains

Material you could not trace should be labeled plainly in your working files, for example "lead only, not verified". Do not let it drift into drafts. If you believe it is important enough to mention publicly, the honest form is to say that an unverified claim circulates and that you could not confirm it. Even that should be avoided where a named living person is concerned, because repeating an unverified claim still spreads it.

On this site's grading scale, set out at `/methodology/`, untraced material is at best B2 (thin or unverified), and a factual claim about a named living person needs A1 or A2. In practice this means that nothing from a mirror or wiki alone can support such a claim.

## Reference works in general

The same reasoning applies, more gently, to general online encyclopedias. A well-maintained article is a good map of a subject and its footnotes are a good reading list. Cite the footnoted sources after reading them yourself, not the article. The article may change tomorrow, and its summary of a source may differ from what the source says.

## A short test

Before citing anything, ask whether a stranger could use your citation to reach an accountable publisher's copy of the same words, and whether that copy will still say the same thing next year. If the answer to either is no, keep looking.
