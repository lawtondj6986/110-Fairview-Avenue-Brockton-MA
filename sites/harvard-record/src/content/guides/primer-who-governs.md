Universities are governed in a way that surprises people used to companies or government agencies.
Formal authority sits with a board that meets a few times a year. Day-to-day authority is delegated
to a president and a group of senior officers. Academic matters are shared with the faculty,
sometimes by formal rule and sometimes by custom. Large schools within the university can operate
with considerable independence. When something goes wrong, the question of who was responsible often
has more than one honest answer. This primer explains the usual structure. Private and public
institutions differ, and so do individual universities.

## The governing board

Every university has a [governing board](/glossary/governing-board/) that holds ultimate legal
authority. It may be called the board of trustees, the corporation, the board of regents, the board
of visitors or something else. A few institutions have two boards with different roles; see [board
of overseers](/glossary/board-of-overseers/).

At a private university, the board is the governing body of a nonprofit corporation. Its members are
usually chosen by the board itself, sometimes with seats elected by alumni. At a public university,
members are typically appointed by the governor, elected, or chosen under a process set by state
law, and the board operates within the state's legal framework.

Board members owe fiduciary duties to the institution, commonly described as the duty of care
(acting with reasonable diligence) and the duty of loyalty (putting the institution's interests
ahead of their own). Boards typically hire and evaluate the president, approve budgets and major
financial decisions, oversee the endowment, approve tenure and senior appointments in a formal
sense, and set high-level policy. Much of the work happens in committees, such as finance,
investment, compensation, governance and an [audit committee](/glossary/audit-committee/) that
oversees financial reporting, internal audit and compliance.

Board members are often major donors themselves. That overlap is normal in U.S. higher education,
and it is why conflict-of-interest policies for board members matter.

## The president

The [university president](/glossary/university-president/) is the chief executive, appointed by and
accountable to the board. The president leads fundraising at the highest level, represents the
institution publicly, appoints senior officers, and sets priorities. The president's office usually
includes or oversees the general counsel, communications, government relations and development. In
practice the president's power depends on the institution's culture and on the strength of its
schools and faculty.

## The provost and senior officers

The [provost](/glossary/provost/) is usually the chief academic officer and often the second-ranking
officer. The provost typically oversees faculty appointments and promotions, academic programs,
academic budgets and the deans. Other senior officers commonly include a chief financial officer, a
[general counsel](/glossary/general-counsel/) who leads the legal office, a vice president for
development, a vice president for research who oversees sponsored programs and research compliance,
and officers for student life, human resources and facilities. Titles vary.

## Deans and schools

A [dean](/glossary/dean/) leads a school or faculty, such as a school of medicine, law, engineering
or arts and sciences. At large research universities, deans often control their own budgets, faculty
hiring, fundraising staff and policies within limits set centrally. The [school and faculty
structure](/glossary/school-and-faculty-structure/) entry explains the variations. In a university
with [decentralized budgeting](/glossary/decentralized-budgeting/), schools keep most of the revenue
they generate and bear most of their own costs, which gives deans substantial independence. The
guide on [who decides in a decentralized
university](/guides/who-decides-in-a-decentralized-university/) covers the consequences.

Beneath deans, [department chairs](/glossary/department-chair/) manage departments, assign teaching,
oversee staff and play a central role in hiring and promotion. Centers, institutes and programs may
report to a dean, the provost or the vice president for research. Many have [advisory
boards](/glossary/advisory-board/) that include donors. Those boards usually advise and do not
govern.

## The faculty

[Shared governance](/glossary/shared-governance/) is the principle that faculty have primary
responsibility for academic matters: curriculum, degree requirements, standards for appointment and
promotion, and the conduct of research and teaching. In practice this works through faculty
meetings, committees and, at many institutions, a [faculty senate](/glossary/faculty-senate/) or
similar elected body.

How much power the faculty holds is set by the institution's statutes, bylaws and faculty handbook,
and by custom. On some questions the faculty decides. On many others it advises. A faculty vote of
no confidence in a leader, for example, usually has no binding legal effect, though it can carry
significant weight with a board. [Tenure](/glossary/tenure/) and [academic
freedom](/glossary/academic-freedom/) protect individual faculty members against certain kinds of
administrative pressure, which is one reason faculty governance has influence beyond its formal
powers.

## Where accountability sits

Inside the institution, accountability runs upward: chairs answer to deans, deans to the provost,
senior officers to the president, and the president to the board. The board answers to no one inside
the institution. Outside accountability comes from several directions, none of them comprehensive.

- State attorneys general oversee charitable assets and can act when a nonprofit's board breaches
  its duties or misuses restricted funds. See [state attorney general oversight of
  charities](/law/state-attorney-general-oversight-of-charities/).
- The Internal Revenue Service oversees tax-exempt status, and private universities generally file
  an annual [Form 990](/law/form-990/) that discloses governance information and compensation of top
  officials. See [the 501(c)(3) page](/law/irc-501c3-tax-exemption/).
- Federal agencies enforce conditions attached to federal funding and student aid, including civil
  rights laws such as [Title IX](/law/title-ix/) and the campus safety rules of the [Clery
  Act](/law/clery-act/).
- Accrediting bodies review institutions against standards that include governance, and federal
  student aid eligibility depends on accreditation.
- For public universities, state legislatures, public records laws and open meeting laws add further
  oversight. Their reach varies by state.
- Courts hear lawsuits by students, employees, donors and others.
- Congress can investigate and hold hearings; see the guide on [reading a congressional oversight
  letter](/guides/read-a-congressional-oversight-letter/).

Informal accountability comes from faculty, students, alumni, donors, the press and public opinion.
It can be powerful but has no formal mechanism.

## A worked hypothetical

A professor at a school within a university raises concerns that a donor is pressing to influence
which candidates a center hires. The professor tells the department chair, who consults the dean's
office. The dean's development staff note that the donor has an active pledge. The dean decides no
action is needed.

Months later, the matter becomes public. The faculty senate passes a resolution asking for a review.
The president, after consulting the board chair and the general counsel, announces an outside review
overseen by a committee of the board. The state attorney general's office requests information about
the donor's restricted gifts. Each actor in this sequence held a different kind of authority: the
dean over school matters, the president over the institution's response, the board over the
president, the faculty over its own resolution, and the attorney general over charitable assets.

## What the documents look like

- Charter, statutes and bylaws: define the board and officers. Often public.
- Board and committee minutes: usually internal at private universities. At public universities,
  open meeting laws often make some minutes public, with exceptions that vary by state.
- Faculty handbook and governance documents: usually public or available to faculty.
- Faculty senate agendas, minutes and resolutions: often public or available to the campus
  community.
- Delegation-of-authority policies: internal or public, stating who may sign which commitments.
- Organizational charts and reporting lines: usually public in outline.
- Form 990 and audited financial statements: public for private nonprofits that file.
- Accreditation self-studies and reports: sometimes public in summary.

## Questions worth asking

1. Who had formal authority over this decision, and who had practical authority?
2. Was the decision made centrally, or within a school or center?
3. Did the board, or a board committee, know of the matter, and when?
4. What role did the faculty play, and was it binding or advisory?
5. Which outside bodies have jurisdiction, and over which part of the matter?
6. Do any board members or officers have financial or personal ties to the people involved?
7. Which records of the decision are public, and which are internal?
