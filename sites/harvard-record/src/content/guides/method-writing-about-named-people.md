Naming a person in published research has consequences for that person that last far longer than the
research. Fairness here is not a courtesy added at the end. It is part of accuracy. This guide sets
out working rules for writing about named people from public records. It explains good practice and
is not legal advice. Defamation and privacy law vary by country and state, and anyone publishing
serious claims about individuals should get advice of their own.

## Decide whether the name is needed

Start by asking what the name adds. A name is needed when the person held a role that matters to the
subject, such as an official who made a decision, and when a strong source places them in that role.
A name is often not needed for junior staff, family members, people who appear only in passing, and
anyone whose presence in a document is incidental. "A staff member in the development office" may
tell the reader everything relevant.

Never name, or make identifiable, a victim or alleged victim of abuse who has not chosen to be
public. Avoid combinations of details (a job, a year, a town) that identify someone you have left
unnamed.

## Findings and allegations are different things

A finding is a conclusion reached by a body with authority to reach it: a court judgment, a verdict,
a regulator's determination, the stated conclusion of an official review. An allegation is a claim
made by a party that has not been tested, or not yet decided.

Keep them apart in every sentence.

- Attribute allegations to whoever made them, and name the document: "the complaint alleges", "the
  letter asserts".
- Do not let attribution fade in later sentences. After "the complaint alleges X", a following
  sentence that begins "After X happened" has quietly turned the allegation into a fact.
- Report the stage and outcome of any proceeding. Say whether a case is pending, was dismissed,
  settled or went to judgment. A settlement is generally not a finding of wrongdoing, and many
  settlements say so expressly.
- Being charged is not being convicted. Being investigated is not being charged. Use the exact term
  the record supports.
- Avoid loaded shorthand. "Linked to", "tied to" and "implicated" assert nothing checkable and
  suggest a great deal. State the specific documented fact instead.

A primary record of an allegation, such as a filed complaint, is a strong source for the fact that
the allegation was made. It is not a source for the truth of the allegation.

## Set a source threshold for people

Decide in advance what quality of source a claim about a named living person requires, and hold to
it. On this site, such a claim needs a primary official record or established reporting that tracks
the record (grades A1 and A2 at `/methodology/`). Thin or unverified material is not used for claims
about named people. If the only support for a claim about a person is a single uncorroborated
account, the fair course is usually not to publish it.

## Include denials and responses

When a person or institution has responded on the record, include the response near the claim, not
at the bottom of the page. Give it in their words where practical. If they have denied the claim,
say so plainly. If they declined to comment, or did not respond to a request by a stated date, say
that. Do not characterize silence as admission.

Look for responses actively. Check the reporting you rely on, the person's own published statements,
and any court filing in answer.

## Bystanders and recipients

Documents name many people who did nothing of note: recipients of a group email, guests on a list,
people mentioned by someone else. Appearing in a record is not conduct.

- Describe the capacity exactly: "is listed as a recipient", "is mentioned by the author".
- Say plainly that no wrongdoing is implied, where a reader might otherwise assume it.
- Consider whether to name them at all. This site names such people only where the cited reporting
  names them.
- Do not build lists of names from a document set. A list strips each name of its context and
  implies a shared status that the documents do not show.

## Right of reply

Right of reply means giving a person a fair chance to respond before you publish a serious claim
about them. It is a standard of practice in journalism and a sound one for any researcher.

1. Write to the person or their representative through a public, professional channel.
2. Set out the specific claims you intend to publish and the sources they rest on.
3. Give a reasonable deadline, stated clearly.
4. Publish the substance of any reply, and correct anything the reply shows to be wrong.
5. Keep a record of the invitation and any answer.

A template invitation is in the Templates series under `/guides/`. Contact should be a single,
courteous letter with perhaps one reminder. Repeated or personal approaches become harassment. Do
not contact victims, and do not approach family members to reach a subject.

## Time, proportion and the dead

People change roles. State when a person held the position you describe, and do not use the present
tense for a past role. Give proportionate space: a minor documented fact about a person should not
be presented with the weight of a major finding. Take the same care in writing about people who have
died. They cannot reply, and their families are living.

## Final read

Before publishing, read every sentence containing a name and ask four questions. What exactly does
the source say? Is this a finding or an allegation, and does the sentence say which? Is any response
included? Would the sentence be fair if it were about you?
