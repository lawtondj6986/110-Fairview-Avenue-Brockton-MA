When a university disciplines a faculty member, any public announcement is usually short. It may be
a message from a dean, a statement to a newspaper, or a line in a report. Personnel matters are
confidential by default, so the text reflects a negotiation between disclosure and privacy. The
words chosen are precise, and the terms have specific meanings.

## Identify the stage

The first task is to work out whether a process has ended.

- Administrative leave. Often a neutral measure while a matter is examined. Institutions commonly
  state that it is not disciplinary and implies no conclusion. It may be paid.
- Under review or investigation. No conclusion has been reached.
- Finding. A decision-maker has concluded that a policy was or was not violated.
- Sanction. A consequence imposed after a finding.
- Appeal or grievance pending. The outcome may change.
- Resignation or retirement. A departure, which may occur before, during or after a process. A
  process may end without a finding when a person leaves.

An announcement of leave is not an announcement of a sanction. Treat them differently.

## Find what was decided, and under which rule

Look for three things.

1. The policy. Universities have separate policies for sexual and gender-based harassment,
   discrimination, research misconduct (generally meaning fabrication, falsification or plagiarism),
   conflicts of interest, and general professional conduct. Each has its own procedures and
   definitions. A violation of a professional conduct policy is not a finding of harassment, and the
   reverse.
2. The finding. Whether the announcement says a violation was found, and of what. Some announcements
   describe sanctions without stating the finding.
3. The standard. Institutional processes typically decide on a preponderance of the evidence (more
   likely than not) or, at some institutions and for some matters, a higher standard. Neither is the
   criminal standard. An institutional finding is not a court judgment.

## Who decided

Faculty discipline often involves several steps: an investigating office, a faculty committee, a
dean, and for the most serious sanctions the provost, president or governing board. The announcement
usually comes from the person who imposed the sanction. In a decentralized university this is often
a dean acting for one school. See [the guide on who decides
what](/guides/who-decides-in-a-decentralized-university/).

## What the sanctions mean

Sanctions range widely. Common terms, in rough order of severity:

- Reprimand or letter of censure. A formal written rebuke placed in the file.
- Restrictions on duties: no teaching, no undergraduate teaching, no advising of new students, no
  supervisory role, removal from committees.
- Restrictions on contact or access: limits on meeting students alone, on presence in particular
  buildings, or on campus generally.
- Loss of title or position: removal as chair or center director, or loss of a named professorship.
  A person can lose an administrative post or an endowed chair and remain a tenured professor.
- Suspension, with or without pay, for a stated period.
- Loss of emeritus status. Emeritus is an honorary title for retired faculty. It can be revoked,
  usually along with associated privileges.
- Dismissal or revocation of tenure. The most serious sanction, usually requiring a lengthy formal
  process with faculty participation and board approval, and for that reason rare.

For each sanction, look for its duration (a term, "indefinitely," or until a condition is met) and
for who will review it. "Indefinitely" means no end date has been set, which is different from
permanently.

## What the announcement shows about the present

Translate the sanctions into practical terms.

- Is the person still employed? Still tenured?
- May they teach, advise or supervise? Whom?
- May they be on campus?
- What has been arranged for their current students and trainees?

The last question is often unanswered, and it matters a great deal to the people affected.

## What is usually left out

- The underlying facts. Announcements rarely describe the conduct in any detail.
- The identities and number of complainants, which are protected.
- The investigation report. It is almost never published.
- Related matters elsewhere: other institutions, funders, journals, professional societies or
  licensing bodies may conduct their own processes and reach their own conclusions.

Privacy law, employment law and fairness to complainants and respondents all limit disclosure. A
sparse announcement is not in itself evidence that the institution is hiding something. It does
leave open questions, and it is reasonable to ask the process questions that can be answered.

## Questions that can usually be answered

1. Under which policy was the matter decided?
2. Has the process concluded, and is an appeal available or pending?
3. Who imposed the sanction?
4. How long does it last, and who reviews it?

## What an announcement can tell you

- That the institution took a specific action on a specific date.
- Often, which policy was involved and whether a violation was found.
- The practical restrictions now in place.

## What it cannot tell you

- What happened, in any detail.
- Whether the sanction was proportionate. That requires facts the announcement omits.
- Whether a court or another body would reach the same conclusion.

## Common mistakes

- Reading administrative leave as a finding.
- Reading a resignation as an admission, or as an exoneration.
- Assuming a sanctioned professor has been dismissed. Most sanctions fall short of that.
- Describing an institutional finding as a legal verdict.
- Not checking back. Sanctions are appealed, modified, lifted and sometimes extended. See [the guide
  on following up on promises](/guides/follow-up-on-promises-after-a-scandal/).

## How to describe one

State who announced what, on which date, under which policy, and at what stage. Use the
institution's terms for the finding and the sanction. Include the faculty member's public response
if there is one. Say what is not known. The [glossary](/glossary/) defines terms such as emeritus,
tenure and administrative leave.
