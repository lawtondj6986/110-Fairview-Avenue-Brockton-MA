Research often ends in a negative result. You looked for a record and did not find one. That result is worth reporting, but it is the easiest kind of finding to overstate. "There is no record" and "we found no record" sound alike and mean different things. This guide explains how to word an absence so that it claims exactly what you know.

## Three different absences

Keep these apart. They support different sentences.

- You did not find a record. This is a statement about your search. It is true if you searched and found nothing, whatever exists elsewhere.
- No record exists. This is a statement about the world. You can rarely know it. It may be supportable when a body with full access says so, for example when an agency responds to a records request that it located no responsive records. Even then, the careful statement is that the agency reported finding none.
- The thing did not happen. This is a statement about events. An absence of records almost never establishes it by itself.

The principle used on this site is that absence of a record is not proof of absence. The evidence state "nothing on record", defined at `/methodology/`, means only that no public record was found.

## Why records go missing

Before reading meaning into a gap, consider the ordinary reasons a record might not be where you looked.

- It was never created. Many decisions and conversations leave no document.
- It exists but is not public: sealed by a court, private to an institution, or exempt from disclosure.
- It was public once and has been removed or moved.
- It is public but not indexed, not digitized, or filed under a name or spelling you did not try.
- It was destroyed under an ordinary retention schedule, the timetable an organization follows for discarding old files.
- It falls outside the scope of the collection you searched. A review commissioned to examine certain years will not mention others.

Each of these is far more common than deliberate concealment. Do not suggest concealment unless a source documents it.

## Describe the search

An absence is only as meaningful as the search behind it. State the search so the reader can judge.

1. Where you looked: the specific databases, dockets, reports or archives.
2. What you looked for: the names, spellings, terms and date ranges.
3. When you looked: the date, because collections change.
4. What you could not search: sealed material, non-digitized records, paywalled archives, languages you do not read.

A full statement might read: "We searched the published report and its appendices for the organization's name and two variant spellings on [date] and found no mention. We did not have access to the underlying records the report drew on."

For short text, a compact form works: "We found no public record of a response as of [date]."

## Wording that fits

Prefer the first-person, limited form.

- "We found no record of..." in place of "There is no record of...".
- "The report does not address..." in place of "The report found no evidence of...", unless the report itself says it looked and found none.
- "No response is on the public record as of [date]" in place of "They never responded".
- "The filing does not name..." in place of "They were not involved".

Distinguish between a source that is silent and a source that reports a negative. "The review does not mention the gift" is silence. "The review states that it found no record of the gift" is a reported negative, and a much stronger statement. Quote or cite the passage when you rely on a reported negative, and keep the reviewing body's own scope limits attached to it.

Avoid adverbs that add suggestion: "notably absent", "conspicuously silent", "curiously missing". They assert that the absence is meaningful without saying why. If you have a documented reason to expect the record, state that reason instead: "The policy in force required a written approval. We did not find one in the released material."

## Absence cuts both ways

The same discipline applies whichever direction the absence points.

An absence of evidence of wrongdoing does not clear a person or institution. It would be wrong to write that someone "had no involvement" because you found no record of any.

An absence of a denial is not an admission. An absence of a record of approval does not show that something was unapproved. An absence of an outcome does not show that a plan failed, only that you cannot show it was carried out. In that case the claim stays at stated intent.

Treat people fairly in both directions. Where you report that no record connects a named person to something, say so in plain terms and do not undercut it with hints.

## When an absence is informative

Sometimes a gap does carry weight. That requires three things: a documented reason the record should exist (a legal filing requirement, a written policy, a numbered sequence with a missing number), a search that covered the place it should be, and a statement of both. Even then, report the gap and the reason for expecting the record. Leave the explanation open unless a source supplies it. Where a public body ought to hold the record, a records request may settle the question. Templates for requests are in the Templates series under `/guides/`.

## Record it in the ledger

Enter each statement of absence in your claim ledger like any other claim, with its wording, the scope of the search as its source, and the date checked. Absences go stale faster than other claims, because one new release can end them. Recheck them on a schedule, and when a record turns up, update the text and note the change publicly.
