A statement issued during a crisis is a drafted document. It is usually written by communications
staff, reviewed by lawyers and approved by senior leaders. Every phrase has been weighed. Reading it
closely is not cynicism. It is treating the text as carefully as its authors did.

## First, the basics

1. Who issued it: the president, a dean, a spokesperson, or "the university"? A named leader's
   signature carries more weight and more accountability than an unsigned statement.
2. Who is the audience: students and staff, alumni, the press, or a regulator?
3. When was it issued relative to events, and what prompted it? A statement that follows a news
   report by hours is a reaction to that report.
4. Is it the first statement or a revision? Compare with earlier versions. Changes between versions
   are informative.

## Sort every sentence into one of four kinds

Go through the statement line by line and label each sentence.

- Fact. A checkable claim about what happened: dates, amounts, actions taken.
- Commitment. Something the institution says it will do.
- Value. A statement of principle or feeling.
- Framing. Language that shapes how the facts are understood.

Count them. A statement made mostly of values and framing has told you little. Extract the facts and
commitments into a short list. That list is the content.

## Read the facts for their edges

Facts in a crisis statement are usually accurate and narrowly drawn. Look at the boundaries.

- Time limits. "Since that year, the university has not accepted gifts from this source" says
  nothing about before.
- Entity limits. "The university did not" may not cover a separate school, an affiliated hospital, a
  research center or an individual faculty member.
- Knowledge limits. "We are not aware of" and "we have found no evidence of" describe the state of
  the speaker's knowledge at the time of writing. They are not denials.
- Category limits. "No gifts" may not address payments, investments, sponsored research or in-kind
  benefits.
- Qualifiers. "Directly," "formally," "currently," "to our knowledge" and "official" each narrow the
  claim. Read the sentence again without the qualifier and note the difference.

For each fact, ask what nearby question it does not answer.

## Read the commitments for their verbs

- "We will" with a date and an owner is a commitment.
- "We are reviewing," "we will examine" and "we are committed to" describe a process or an attitude.
- "We have asked" someone to conduct a review raises the questions covered in [the guide on whether
  a review was independent](/guides/was-an-internal-review-independent/): who, with what scope, and
  whether the result will be published.

Record each commitment with its date. See [the guide on following up on
promises](/guides/follow-up-on-promises-after-a-scandal/).

## Recognize common framing

- Passive voice without an actor. "Mistakes were made" and "gifts were accepted" omit who acted.
- Past tense that closes the matter. "This occurred under previous leadership."
- Scale-setting. "A small number of," "a fraction of" and "isolated" are characterizations, not
  counts. Look for the actual number.
- Distancing. Describing a long relationship as limited contact, or a person as having had "no
  formal role."
- Leading with values. Several sentences of regret before the first fact.
- Emphasis on process. Detailed description of the review to come in place of what is already known.

Framing is not dishonesty. It is the institution putting its case. The task is to notice it and set
it aside while you assess the facts.

## Notice what is absent

Compare the statement with what was publicly alleged or reported.

- Which specific points does it address, and which does it pass over?
- Does it confirm, deny or decline to comment on each?
- Does it say when more information will come?

There are legitimate reasons for silence. Privacy law limits what can be said about students and
employees. Pending litigation or an open investigation restricts comment. An institution may not yet
know the facts. A good statement says which of these applies. Silence without a stated reason is not
proof of anything, but it marks a question to keep open.

## Compare over time

Keep the statements in order. Early statements are often revised as more comes out. Note where a
later statement corrects an earlier figure, widens a time period, or acknowledges something first
described as not known. A pattern of successive enlargement tells you how much weight to give the
next "complete" account.

## What a statement can and cannot tell you

A statement tells you the institution's official position on a given date, which facts it is
prepared to confirm, and what it has committed to. Those are valuable. Confirmed facts from the
institution itself are strong evidence, because the institution has every reason to be careful.

A statement cannot tell you what was left out, and it is not a finding by any neutral body. An
apology is not a legal admission, and a denial is not a clearance.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting the values and missing the facts.
- Reading a narrow denial as a broad one.
- Treating "we found no evidence" as "it did not happen."
- Assuming bad faith in every hedge. Careful wording is also what accuracy looks like when facts are
  incomplete.
- Failing to save the original text. Statements on web pages are sometimes edited without notice.
