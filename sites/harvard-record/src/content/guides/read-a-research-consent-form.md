A consent form is the written part of informed consent, the process by which a person agrees to take
part in research after learning what it involves. In the United States, most research with human
participants that is federally funded or conducted at institutions applying federal rules falls
under regulations known as the Common Rule, and an ethics committee called an institutional review
board (IRB) reviews the study and its consent form. The form is a summary of an agreement. It
deserves a slow reading.

## First, is it research or care?

Check what kind of document you have.

- A research consent form describes a study designed to produce general knowledge. Taking part is
  optional, and the primary aim is not your personal benefit.
- A clinical consent form covers treatment or testing for your own care.
- A commercial agreement, such as terms of service for a consumer test, is a contract with a
  company. It may allow research use of your data under different rules.

The protections described here apply to the first kind. If you are unsure which you are holding,
ask.

## What the form should contain

Federal rules list required elements. Under current rules the form should begin with a concise
summary of the key information a person would need to decide. After that, look for each of these.

1. A statement that this is research, its purpose, how long participation lasts and what procedures
   are involved, identifying any that are experimental.
2. Reasonably foreseeable risks and discomforts.
3. Expected benefits, to you or to others. Many studies offer no direct benefit, and the form should
   say so.
4. Alternatives to taking part, where relevant.
5. How confidentiality of records will be protected, and its limits.
6. For research involving more than minimal risk, whether compensation or treatment is available if
   you are injured.
7. Whom to contact with questions about the study and about your rights as a participant. The second
   contact is usually the IRB office.
8. A statement that participation is voluntary, that refusing carries no penalty, and that you may
   stop at any time.

If any of these is missing or unclear, ask before signing.

## Sections that matter for samples and genetic data

Studies that collect blood, saliva, tissue or genetic information raise further points. Current
rules require statements on several of them.

- Future use. Whether your samples and data, with identifiers removed, may be used for other
  research or shared with other researchers without asking you again. Some forms offer a choice.
  Others say it will happen.
- Commercial use. Whether samples may be used to develop products for profit and whether you will
  share in any profit. The usual answer is that you will not.
- Return of results. Whether findings relevant to your health will be given to you, and under what
  conditions. Many studies return nothing. Research laboratories are often not certified to produce
  clinical results.
- Data sharing. Whether data will be placed in databases available to other researchers, and whether
  access is open or controlled.
- Re-identification. Removing a name does not make genetic data anonymous in a strict sense, because
  a genome is unique to a person. Good forms acknowledge this.
- Withdrawal. What happens to samples and data if you leave. Unused samples can usually be
  destroyed. Data already analyzed or shared generally cannot be recalled.
- Legal protections. Forms may mention a Certificate of Confidentiality, which limits compelled
  disclosure of identifiable research information, and a federal law (the Genetic Information
  Nondiscrimination Act) that restricts use of genetic information by health insurers and employers.
  That law generally does not cover life, disability or long-term care insurance.

## Who is asking, and who pays

- Find the name of the principal investigator, the institution, and the sponsor or funder.
- Consider your relationship to the person inviting you. A student asked by a professor, an employee
  asked by a supervisor, or a patient asked by their own doctor may feel less free to refuse. The
  rules call for extra care in such situations. You are entitled to say no, and to take the form
  away and think.

## Questions to ask

1. What exactly will be collected from me, and how many times?
2. Who will have access to my identifiable information?
3. Will my data or samples go to other institutions or to companies?
4. Will I be told about results that matter for my health?
5. If I withdraw, what can be destroyed and what cannot?
6. Who reviewed and approved this study, and how do I reach them?

## What a consent form can tell you

- What the researchers have committed to in writing.
- That an ethics committee reviewed the study, where an approval stamp or number appears.
- The scope of permission granted, which matters long afterward when questions arise about how
  samples were used.

## What it cannot tell you

- Everything that will happen. Protocols are amended, and forms are revised. Check the version date.
- Whether the study was conducted as described.
- Whether research took place without a form at all. Some research is exempt from these rules, and
  some activity is not classed as research.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the signature as the consent. Consent is the whole process, including the conversation.
- Assuming "de-identified" means untraceable.
- Assuming research participation includes medical care or individual results.
- Believing a signature waives legal rights. Federal rules bar consent forms from including language
  that waives a participant's legal rights.

This guide explains a type of document and is not advice about any particular study. The Common Rule
