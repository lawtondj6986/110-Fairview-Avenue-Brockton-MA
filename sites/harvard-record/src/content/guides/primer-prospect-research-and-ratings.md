Behind most major gifts is a research and planning function that donors rarely see. Universities
study potential donors, estimate what they could give and how interested they might be, and assign
staff to manage each relationship through a series of planned contacts. The resulting records can
surface in document releases, litigation or reporting. Read correctly, they show how an institution
thought about a person. Read carelessly, they are easily mistaken for facts about that person. This
primer explains the machinery in general terms. Terms and methods vary between institutions.

## What prospect research is

[Prospect research](/glossary/prospect-research/) is the work of identifying and profiling people,
foundations and companies who might give. It is usually done by a small team inside the [development
office](/glossary/development-office/), sometimes called research, prospect development or
advancement services. The team has three main jobs: finding new prospects, writing profiles of known
ones, and maintaining ratings and assignments in the [donor database](/glossary/donor-database/).

A [prospect](/glossary/prospect/) is anyone the institution has identified as a possible donor. The
term says nothing about whether that person has expressed any interest. Alumni, parents, grateful
patients at an academic medical center, people who attend events, and people introduced by trustees
or faculty can all become prospects.

## Where the information comes from

Researchers mostly use sources that are lawful to consult. Common categories include:

- The institution's own records: giving history, event attendance, alumni records, volunteer roles
  and contact reports written by staff.
- Public records: real estate holdings, corporate filings, securities filings that disclose holdings
  of company insiders, court records, and political contribution records where they are public.
- Philanthropic records: foundation tax returns (see the [Form 990 page](/law/form-990/)), donor
  lists published by other charities, and news of gifts elsewhere.
- News and biographical sources: articles, company biographies, obituaries and professional
  directories.
- Commercial screening services: vendors that match a database of names against public and
  proprietary data and return estimated wealth indicators in bulk.

Professional associations for prospect researchers publish ethics statements that call for lawful
collection, accuracy, confidentiality and limiting research to what is relevant to fundraising.
These are voluntary standards. How closely an office follows them depends on the office.

## Capacity ratings

A [capacity rating](/glossary/capacity-rating/) is an estimate of the largest gift a prospect could
make over a defined period, often several years, if fully motivated. It is usually expressed as a
band (for example, a rating code corresponding to a range) rather than a single figure. Each
institution sets its own bands and its own formulas.

Researchers derive capacity from visible assets and income markers, such as real estate value,
disclosed stock holdings, known business sales, compensation disclosed in public filings, and past
gifts to other organizations. Some offices apply rules of thumb that convert visible assets into an
estimated giving capacity. These methods are crude by design. They miss assets that do not appear in
public records, they cannot see debts, and they cannot account for family obligations or other
commitments. A capacity rating is a planning estimate, not a measure of net worth.

## Affinity and inclination

Capacity says what someone could give. Affinity says how connected they are to the institution, and
inclination or readiness says how likely they are to give now. An [affinity
rating](/glossary/affinity-rating/) usually draws on degrees held, past giving, volunteer roles,
event attendance, family ties and expressed interests. Some offices combine the two into a single
score or a grid that sorts prospects into tiers.

Some institutions also use statistical models, sometimes called predictive or propensity models, to
rank large populations of alumni by likelihood of giving. These models are trained on the
institution's own historical data. Their output is a probability-like score, useful for deciding
whom to contact first and nothing more.

## Moves management and portfolios

Once a prospect is rated, a gift officer may be assigned to manage the relationship. Each gift
officer holds a portfolio, a list of prospects for whom they are responsible. The practice of
planning and recording each step with a prospect is known in the field as moves management, and the
broader system as [prospect management](/glossary/prospect-management/).

A "move" is any planned contact meant to advance the relationship: a meeting, a tour, a dinner with
a dean, an invitation to join an [advisory board](/glossary/advisory-board/), or an introduction to
a faculty member. Prospects are typically tracked through stages such as identification,
qualification, [cultivation](/glossary/cultivation/), [solicitation](/glossary/solicitation/) and
[stewardship](/glossary/stewardship/). An [active prospect](/glossary/active-prospect/) is one
currently assigned and being worked. Managers review portfolios regularly, and gift officers are
often evaluated on the number of contacts and solicitations they complete.

Strategy is recorded too. A prospect record may include a target ask amount, a planned ask date, the
institutional priority the prospect is being matched with, and the names of faculty or leaders who
should be involved. Large universities often have a central committee or system to resolve which
school "owns" a prospect, because several schools may want the same donor.

## What ratings do and do not mean

A rating records what an institution believed, at a given time, about a person's potential as a
donor. It does not establish what the person owns, what they intended, or what they knew about how
they were being classified. Most prospects never see their own records.

Several common misreadings are worth avoiding.

- A high capacity rating is not evidence of a gift. Many highly rated prospects never give.
- A rating is often stale. Records are updated irregularly, and a figure entered years earlier may
  remain in the database long after circumstances changed.
- A rating reflects the researcher's sources and method. Two institutions can rate the same person
  very differently.
- Being a prospect is not a relationship. Most prospects have no ongoing contact with the
  institution.
- Planned moves are plans. A contact report saying a meeting was proposed does not show that it
  happened.

At the same time, prospect records can be highly informative about the institution's own conduct:
which people it sought out, how it justified that pursuit, who internally knew what, and which
faculty or leaders were asked to help. The record is strongest as evidence about the institution and
weakest as evidence about the prospect.

## A worked hypothetical

A university's screening service flags a person who sold a company and lives in a high-value home. A
researcher writes a two-page profile and assigns a high capacity band. Affinity is low: the person
has no degree from the institution and has never given. The profile notes that a professor once
coauthored a paper with the person's company.

A gift officer is assigned and records a plan: an introduction through the professor, a lab visit,
then an invitation to an advisory council, with a target ask in two years. Over the next year the
contact reports show one lunch and one visit. The person declines the council invitation and stops
responding. The gift officer moves the person to an inactive status. The capacity band is never
updated.

Years later, a reader who finds only the profile might conclude that the institution and this person
had a significant relationship. The full record shows two contacts, a declined invitation, and no
gift.

## What the documents look like

- Research profile: a short internal biography with wealth indicators, giving history, connections
  and sources. Often dated, sometimes not.
- Screening output: a spreadsheet or database import with codes or bands for many names at once.
- Rating fields: codes in the donor database for capacity, affinity and readiness, with entry dates
  and the staff member who entered them.
- Assignment record: which gift officer manages the prospect, and since when.
- Contact reports: short narratives written after each substantive interaction, with date,
  participants and next steps.
- Strategy or action plan: target amount, purpose and timeline.
- Portfolio and pipeline reports: summaries prepared for managers and leadership.

All of these are internal. They become visible mainly through litigation, investigations, document
releases or leaks. The site's [methodology](/methodology/) explains how it treats internal records
of this kind, and the guide on [reading a released email](/guides/read-a-released-email/) covers the
related problem of correspondence.

## Questions worth asking

1. When was this rating entered or last updated, and by whom?
2. What sources does the profile cite, and are any of them checkable?
3. Is this a capacity estimate, an affinity score, a model output, or a target ask?
4. Did the prospect ever give, and do gift records exist to show it?
5. Do contact reports show actual meetings, or only plans for them?
6. Which faculty or leaders were asked to help with cultivation, and did they know they were part of
   a plan?
7. What does the record show about the institution's choices, as distinct from the prospect's?
