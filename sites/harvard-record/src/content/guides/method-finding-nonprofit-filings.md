Most tax-exempt organizations in the United States file an annual information return with the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), and the law makes those returns open to public inspection. They are a primary source on an organization's finances, governance and grants. This guide explains what exists, where to find it and how far it can take you. Filing rules and thresholds change, so check current IRS guidance for specifics.

## What organizations file

The main federal filing is the Form 990 series.

- Form 990 is the full annual return filed by larger public charities and other exempt organizations.
- Form 990-EZ is a shorter version for smaller organizations.
- Form 990-N is a brief electronic notice filed by the smallest organizations. It contains almost no financial detail.
- Form 990-PF is filed by private foundations, whatever their size. It lists the foundation's grants.
- Form 990-T reports income from business activity unrelated to the organization's exempt purpose.

Which form applies depends on the organization's type and on financial thresholds set by the IRS.

Some organizations do not file a Form 990 at all. Churches and certain religious bodies are generally exempt from the filing requirement. Government bodies are not charities filing returns, which means a public university usually does not file one, although a separately incorporated foundation that supports it usually does. Private universities generally do file.

An organization's application for tax-exempt status and the IRS letter recognizing it are also open to public inspection.

## Where to find them

- The IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search at `https://apps.irs.gov/app/eos/` confirms an organization's exempt status and provides copies of many filed returns.
- Free third-party databases republish IRS data in a more searchable form. A widely used one is ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer at `https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/`. These are convenient, but the IRS record or the organization's own copy is the primary source.
- The organization itself. Exempt organizations are generally required to provide copies of their recent annual returns and their exemption application to anyone who asks, and many post them on their websites.
- State charity regulators. Many states require charities that operate or raise funds there to register and file annual reports, commonly with the attorney general or secretary of state. Some states publish these filings online, and they may include audited financial statements.

Search by the organization's exact legal name and, better, by its Employer Identification Number (EIN), the nine-digit number the IRS assigns. Names are often similar, and a university may have many related entities, each with its own EIN and its own return.

## Reading a Form 990

The form has a core section and lettered schedules. The parts most useful to researchers include:

- the summary and the statement of program accomplishments, for what the organization says it does;
- the governance section, for board size, policies and whether the organization has written conflict of interest and whistleblower policies;
- the list of officers, directors, trustees, key employees and highest-paid employees, with reported compensation;
- the statements of revenue, expenses and the balance sheet;
- the schedule on grants made to other organizations in the United States;
- the schedule on transactions with interested persons, such as loans or business dealings with insiders;
- the schedule on related organizations, which maps affiliated entities;
- the schedule on endowment funds and other supplemental financial detail.

For a private foundation, the Form 990-PF list of grants paid shows each recipient and amount for the year. This is one of the few public places where a specific gift from a foundation to a university can be confirmed from the giver's side.

## What the filings do not show

The limits are significant, and they matter most for research on donors.

- Donor names are generally not public. Public charities report major contributors to the IRS on a schedule, but the names and addresses are withheld from the public copy. Private foundations are the main exception: their contributors are disclosed.
- Gifts received are not itemized by purpose. A university's return shows total contributions, not which donor funded which program.
- Individual donors do not file public returns. A gift made personally, as opposed to through a foundation, will not appear in any public filing by the giver.
- Intermediaries obscure origins. A gift routed through a donor-advised fund (an account at a sponsoring charity from which a donor recommends grants) appears as a grant from the sponsor, without the donor's name.
- Categories are broad and self-reported. Organizations classify their own expenses, and practices differ.
- The data is late. Returns are due months after the fiscal year ends, extensions are common, and publication takes longer still. The most recent available return may describe a period two or more years past.

## Checks before you rely on a number

1. Confirm the entity. Match the EIN, not only the name.
2. Confirm the period. Note the fiscal year start and end dates printed at the top of the return. Many organizations do not use the calendar year.
3. Check for an amended return for the same year.
4. Read the narrative schedule at the end, where organizations explain entries elsewhere on the form.
5. Compare with the audited financial statements where available. The two follow different rules and their totals often differ for legitimate reasons.
6. Compare both sides of a grant where possible: the foundation's list of grants paid and the recipient's reporting.

## How to use them in research

A filed return is a primary official record of what the organization reported under penalty of perjury. Cite it as "the organization's Form 990 for the fiscal year ending [date] reports...". It documents the reported activity. It is not an independent audit of the facts, and an entry on a return is not a finding of anything.

When a return does not show something, word the absence carefully. "The foundation's returns for those years list no grant to the university" is a checkable statement. It does not show that no money moved by another route.
