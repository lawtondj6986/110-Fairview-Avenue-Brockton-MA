A large gift to a university is rarely a single event. It is a process that can run for months or
years and pass through several offices, each with its own records. Knowing the sequence helps a
reader place any document they find: a contact report, a pledge, a gift agreement or a line in a
financial statement each belongs to a particular stage. What follows describes a common pattern at
U.S. research universities. Practice varies by institution, and the names of offices and documents
vary even more.

## The offices involved

The [development office](/glossary/development-office/) (sometimes called advancement or university
relations) is the fundraising arm. At a large university it is usually split between a central
office and fundraising staff embedded in individual schools, and the two do not always coordinate
closely. Within or alongside it sit prospect research, gift processing (which records and receipts
money), and donor relations or [stewardship](/glossary/stewardship/).

Other offices join at specific points. The general counsel's office reviews agreements and unusual
terms. The finance or controller's office sets up the account that will hold the money. A treasurer
or investment office manages endowed funds. The receiving school, department or faculty member is
consulted on what the gift will pay for. For gifts above a certain size or with unusual features, a
[gift acceptance committee](/glossary/gift-acceptance-committee/) or a senior officer must approve.
The thresholds for each step are set by each institution's own policy and are not uniform.

## Identification and cultivation

Most major gifts begin with identification. A fundraiser, a faculty member, a trustee or an existing
donor suggests that someone might give. [Prospect research](/glossary/prospect-research/) staff
compile a profile from public sources and the institution's own records. The person becomes a
[prospect](/glossary/prospect/) and is assigned to a fundraiser, often called a gift officer, who
manages the relationship.

[Cultivation](/glossary/cultivation/) is the period of building interest. It can include lunches,
campus tours, meetings with faculty, invitations to lectures and events, and conversations about
what the person cares about. Each substantive contact is usually recorded in a contact report in the
[donor database](/glossary/donor-database/). Cultivation can run for years. Its purpose is to match
a donor's interests with an institutional priority, and the faculty members who meet prospects
during this period are often part of the plan, whether or not they know the details. The [primer on
prospect research](/guides/primer-prospect-research-and-ratings/) covers this stage in more depth.

## The ask and the proposal

When the gift officer judges the timing right, the institution makes a
[solicitation](/glossary/solicitation/), or ask. For a major gift this is usually a written proposal
following a conversation. The proposal names a purpose (a professorship, a fellowship fund, a
research center, a building), an amount and often a recognition opportunity such as a name. Senior
leaders, a dean or the president may join the meeting for the largest asks.

The donor may accept, counter-propose, or ask for changes to the purpose. Negotiation over purpose
and terms is normal. It is also the point at which a donor's wishes are most likely to shape what
the institution does, so the record of this negotiation matters when a reader later asks how much
influence a gift carried. The guide on [what a donor can and cannot
buy](/guides/what-a-donor-can-and-cannot-buy/) addresses the limits.

## Pledge and gift agreement

A donor who agrees to give over time signs a [pledge](/glossary/pledge/), a written commitment to
pay a stated amount on a schedule. Larger or restricted gifts are documented in a [gift
agreement](/glossary/gift-agreement/), a contract-like document that sets out the amount, the
payment schedule, the purpose, any restrictions, naming rights, reporting the donor will receive,
and what happens if the purpose becomes impossible. Many universities use templates and require the
general counsel's office to review deviations from them.

Gifts arrive in many forms: cash, publicly traded securities, real estate, closely held business
interests, art and other [in-kind gifts](/glossary/in-kind-gift/), and future gifts through [planned
giving](/glossary/planned-giving/) such as bequests. Non-cash and future gifts raise extra questions
about valuation and risk and usually receive extra review. Money may also come not from the donor
personally but through a family foundation, a [donor-advised fund](/glossary/donor-advised-fund/) or
another [intermediary](/glossary/intermediary/), which affects whose name appears on the paperwork.

## Acceptance review

Before or alongside signing, the institution decides whether to accept. Routine gifts are accepted
under standing policy without individual review. Gifts that are large, restricted in unusual ways,
from sources flagged for reputational concern, or that carry naming rights or obligations typically
go to a review step. The [primer on gift acceptance and
vetting](/guides/primer-gift-acceptance-and-vetting/) describes how these decisions are made and
where they tend to fail.

## Receipting, counting and recognition

When money arrives, gift processing records it, links it to the donor's record and the right fund,
and issues a receipt. U.S. federal tax law requires certain written acknowledgments for deductible
gifts and, where a donor receives something in return, a statement of the value of that benefit. The
details are explained in the [law page on charitable deductions and quid pro quo
contributions](/law/charitable-contribution-deduction-and-quid-pro-quo/); the thresholds are set by
statute and regulation and should be checked in the current text.

Institutions also count gifts for internal goals and [capital
campaigns](/glossary/capital-campaign/). Counting rules, often based on [industry counting
standards](/glossary/gift-counting-standards/), decide when a pledge counts toward a campaign total
and how future gifts are valued. A campaign total and the cash actually received in a year are
different figures, which is a common source of confusion. See the guide on [reading a donation
statistic](/guides/read-a-donation-statistic/).

Recognition follows. A donor may be listed in an honor roll, invited to events, or have a space,
fund or professorship named. Naming is usually governed by a separate policy and often requires
approval at a senior level or by the governing board.

## Setting up the fund

The finance office creates an account for the gift. Two broad kinds matter most.

A [current-use gift](/glossary/current-use-gift/) is spent on its purpose, usually within a few
years. An [endowment](/glossary/endowment/) gift is invested, and only a portion of its value is
spent each year under a payout rule set by the institution. The principal is meant to last
indefinitely. In most states, the management and spending of endowment funds held by charities is
governed by a version of the Uniform Prudent Management of Institutional Funds Act, explained on the
[UPMIFA page](/law/upmifa/). The adopted versions differ in detail from state to state.

Either kind can be a [restricted gift](/glossary/restricted-gift/), limited to a purpose the donor
named, or an [unrestricted gift](/glossary/unrestricted-gift/) that the institution can use as it
decides. An [endowed chair](/glossary/endowed-chair/) is a common restricted endowed fund whose
annual payout supports a faculty position. Restricted funds are tracked separately so that spending
can be matched to the donor's terms.

## Stewardship and later changes

After the gift, the institution reports to the donor on how the money is used, sends financial
statements for endowed funds, and keeps the relationship going, often in the hope of a further gift.
The stewardship record tends to be detailed for large donors.

Purposes sometimes become outdated. A gift agreement may allow the institution to redirect funds to
a related purpose. Without such a clause, changing a restriction may require the donor's consent or,
if the donor has died, a court or the state attorney general, depending on state law. Disputes over
[donor intent](/glossary/donor-intent/) arise here. A gift can also be returned or redirected after
the fact, although agreements rarely plan for that; see [gift return](/glossary/gift-return/).

## A worked hypothetical

A donor attends a public lecture by a professor and mentions to a dean's office staff member that
the topic interests them. A gift officer is assigned. Prospect research produces a short profile.
Over eighteen months the gift officer arranges two lab visits, a dinner with the professor, and an
invitation to an advisory council. Each meeting produces a contact report.

The gift officer then proposes a gift to create an endowed fund supporting graduate fellowships in
the professor's field, named for the donor. The donor asks that the fund support the professor's own
students specifically. After discussion with the dean, the agreement says the fund will support
students in the field, with the department deciding awards. Because the amount exceeds the school's
review threshold and includes naming, the gift goes to a review committee, which approves it. The
donor signs a pledge payable over several years. The finance office opens an endowed fund. Each year
the donor receives a report naming the fellows supported.

## What the documents look like

- Identification and cultivation: a prospect profile, a rating in the donor database, contact
  reports, event invitations and attendance lists, and email between fundraisers and faculty. These
  are internal.
- Solicitation: a proposal letter or deck, often with a draft budget for the gift's purpose.
  Internal.
- Commitment: a signed pledge form and gift agreement, sometimes with a naming approval memo.
  Internal, though terms are occasionally disclosed.
- Acceptance: a committee agenda, memo or minutes, or a sign-off form. Internal.
- Receipt: a gift receipt or acknowledgment letter sent to the donor. The donor holds a copy.
- Accounting: a fund record in the general ledger and a fund description. Internal. Aggregate
  figures appear in audited financial statements and in the institution's [Form 990](/law/form-990/)
  if it files one.
- Recognition: honor rolls, press releases, named-space signage and websites. Public.
- Stewardship: annual fund reports and donor correspondence. Internal.

Most of the paper trail is internal. Public traces are mostly recognition, aggregate financial
reporting, and occasional disclosures such as federal reporting of certain foreign gifts under
[Section 117 of the Higher Education Act](/law/higher-education-act-section-117/). The site's [donor
pathways](/donor-pathways/) section traces funding routes in the record it covers.

## Questions worth asking

1. At what stage was a given document produced, and which office would hold the related records?
2. Was the gift documented by a gift agreement, and what does it say about purpose, naming and
   reporting?
3. Did the donor give personally, or through a foundation, fund or intermediary?
4. Was the gift reviewed individually, and by whom?
5. Is a quoted figure a pledge, a campaign count, or cash received?
6. Is the fund current-use or endowed, and restricted or unrestricted?
7. Who decides how the money is spent, and does the donor receive reports on it?
