Comparing two institutions by their sexual misconduct numbers is harder than it looks. The figures
come from different kinds of documents, use different definitions, and are shaped by how willing
students are to come forward. A fair comparison is possible, but only between like and like.

## Three kinds of numbers

1. Reported crime statistics. These appear in the annual security report required by the Clery Act.
   They count incidents reported to campus authorities that fall within federal crime definitions
   and within defined locations (roughly, campus property, certain nearby public property, and
   certain other property the institution controls).
2. Complaint and case data. Some institutions publish annual figures from the office that handles
   misconduct complaints. These count disclosures, formal complaints and outcomes under the
   institution's own policy, which usually covers more conduct and more places than the crime
   statistics.
3. Survey estimates. A campus climate survey asks students anonymously about their experiences. It
   estimates how common an experience is, whether or not anyone reported it.

Never set a number of one kind against a number of another kind.

## Why higher reported numbers are not simply worse

Research on sexual violence has long found that most incidents are not reported to any authority.
The count of reports therefore depends on two things: how much harm occurs, and what share of it is
reported.

An institution can have a higher count because more harm occurs there. It can also have a higher
count because students know where to report, trust that they will be taken seriously, and face fewer
obstacles. Training, visible staff and a well-publicized process tend to raise reports. An
institution that discourages reporting, or makes it confusing, will tend to show fewer.

A rising count after a new reporting system, a publicized case or a round of training often reflects
more reporting, not more incidents. The count alone cannot tell you which. This applies in both
directions: a high number is not proof of a healthy reporting culture either.

## Why even the same kind of number differs

Reported crime statistics follow federal definitions, but several things still vary.

- Size. A campus with five times the students will usually have more reports. Convert to a rate per
  thousand enrolled students, and note that enrollment is an imperfect denominator because staff and
  visitors are also present.
- Housing. Many incidents occur in residences. A campus where most students live in university
  housing will capture incidents that, at a commuter campus, happen in private housing outside the
  counted area.
- Geography. An urban campus with scattered buildings and a self-contained rural campus draw their
  boundaries very differently.
- Year of report. Incidents are counted in the year they were reported, not the year they occurred.
  A single person reporting several past incidents can move one year's figure.

Survey estimates vary for other reasons.

- Different surveys word questions differently. Asking about specific behaviors produces higher
  estimates than asking whether someone was "assaulted."
- Time frames differ. "Since entering college" and "in the past twelve months" are not comparable.
- Response rates differ, and the students who respond may not resemble those who do not.
- Results are often broken out by gender, year and student type. Compare the same subgroup.

## A checklist for a fair comparison

1. Identify the kind of number: reported crime, case data or survey estimate.
2. Confirm that both figures use the same definition and the same time frame.
3. Use rates, not raw counts, and state the denominator.
4. Use at least three years. Small counts jump around from year to year by chance.
5. Where both institutions took part in the same multi-campus survey with the same instrument,
   prefer that comparison over any other.
6. Compare the reporting gap. Set each institution's survey estimate beside its own reported count.
   A smaller gap suggests more of what happens is reaching the institution.
7. Compare the response, not only the count: time to resolution, share of reports that proceed to a
   formal process, outcomes, and whether the institution publishes these at all.
8. Write down what could not be compared and why.

## What a comparison can and cannot tell you

A careful comparison can show that one institution publishes more, that its students report a larger
share of what they experience, or that students there express more or less trust in the process.
With a shared survey instrument it can show a difference in estimated prevalence for a given group,
within the survey's margin of error.

It cannot produce a reliable ranking of which campus is safer. It cannot tell you what an individual
student's experience will be. It cannot tell you why a number differs without further information.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the campus with the lowest reported count as the safest.
- Treating the highest count as evidence of good reporting culture without checking survey data.
- Comparing a university's whole-institution figure with a single school's figure.
- Ignoring differences in the wording of survey questions.
- Reading a one-year change as a trend.
