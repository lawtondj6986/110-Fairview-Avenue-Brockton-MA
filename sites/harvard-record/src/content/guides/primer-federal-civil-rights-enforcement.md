Colleges and universities that receive federal money agree to follow federal civil-rights laws. The
main ones for education are [Title VI](/law/title-vi/) (race, color and national origin), [Title
IX](/law/title-ix/) (sex), Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act (disability), and the Age
Discrimination Act. The U.S. Department of Education's [Office for Civil
Rights](/glossary/office-for-civil-rights/) (OCR) enforces these laws administratively. The
Department of Justice also enforces several of them, and other agencies that fund research have
civil-rights offices of their own. This primer describes the administrative path at OCR, which is
the one most often seen in university records.

## Administrative enforcement is not a lawsuit

OCR does not award money to the person who complained and does not decide who was right in an
individual incident. Its question is whether the institution complied with the law, for example
whether it responded properly to reports it knew about. That is an administrative judgment. A person
may also sue an institution in court, where different standards apply. In damages cases under Title
IX, for example, courts apply a demanding test called [deliberate
indifference](/glossary/deliberate-indifference/). OCR uses its own regulatory standards, which are
not the same.

OCR's procedures are set out in a case processing manual that it revises from time to time. Its
priorities, staffing and the guidance it applies have also changed between administrations. Read the
version of the manual and guidance in force for the period you care about.

## Two ways a case begins

- A complaint. Anyone may file one, not only the person harmed. Complaints generally must be filed
  within a set time after the alleged discrimination, and OCR may grant a waiver. The current manual
  states the period and the grounds for waiver.
- An agency-initiated case. OCR may open a [compliance review](/glossary/compliance-review/) or a
  directed investigation without any complaint, often on an issue it has chosen to examine
  nationally.

## Evaluation and opening

OCR first evaluates a complaint. It asks whether it has jurisdiction over the institution and the
subject, whether the complaint is timely, and whether it states enough to suggest a possible
violation. Many complaints are dismissed or closed at this stage, for example because the same
matter is already being handled elsewhere.

If OCR opens a complaint for investigation, it sends letters to the complainant and to the
institution identifying the issues it will investigate. Opening a case is not a finding. It means
the complaint met the threshold for review. OCR may offer mediation between the parties for some
kinds of complaints.

## Investigation

The investigation is mostly documentary. OCR sends the institution a data request asking for
policies, training records, case files, logs and correspondence for a defined period. It may
interview the complainant, staff and students, and may visit the campus. The investigation can take
months or years. The scope is set by the issues stated in the opening letter. Matters outside those
issues are not examined.

## How a case closes

An investigation can end in several ways, and the difference matters.

- Resolution before a finding. The institution may ask to resolve the case before OCR completes its
  investigation. OCR and the institution sign a [resolution
  agreement](/glossary/resolution-agreement/), and OCR issues a letter describing concerns it had
  identified. There is no finding of violation.
- Finding of violation. OCR concludes that the institution failed to comply, and issues a [letter of
  findings](/glossary/letter-of-findings/). OCR then seeks a resolution agreement to remedy the
  violation.
- Insufficient evidence. OCR concludes that the evidence does not establish a violation on some or
  all issues.
- Administrative closure, for reasons such as the complainant withdrawing or the matter being
  resolved through another process.

A single letter may reach different conclusions on different issues. Read the outcome issue by
issue.

## When an institution will not agree

If OCR finds a violation and the institution will not enter a resolution agreement, OCR may start
administrative proceedings that could end in the termination of federal financial assistance, or may
refer the case to the Department of Justice for litigation. Termination is rare, because the
possibility gives institutions a strong reason to settle. The existence of the remedy explains why
resolution agreements are so common.

## Monitoring

A resolution agreement opens a monitoring period. The institution sends OCR reports and documents
showing that it has done what it promised: revised policies, delivered training, reviewed past
files, provided remedies to individuals. OCR may ask for changes before approving a revised policy.
The case stays open until OCR decides every term is met, which can take years. OCR then sends a
closure letter. If the institution falls short, OCR may take further enforcement action.

## A worked hypothetical

A graduate student files a complaint alleging that her department ignored repeated reports of
harassment by a lab supervisor. OCR evaluates the complaint, finds it timely and within its
jurisdiction, and opens an investigation into two issues: whether the university responded promptly
and equitably to the reports, and whether its grievance procedures met the regulations.

OCR asks for three years of complaint files, the department's training records and the Title IX
office's correspondence about the reports. It interviews the Title IX coordinator and several staff.
Before OCR completes its work, the university asks to resolve the case. The resulting agreement
requires a revised policy, training for department staff, a review of prior complaints and periodic
reports to OCR. The accompanying letter says OCR identified concerns about the timeliness of the
response. It makes no finding of violation. Two years later, after reviewing the reports, OCR sends
a closure letter.

A newspaper describes the case as the university being "found to have mishandled" complaints. The
documents do not support that wording.

## What the documents look like

- Complaint. Written by the complainant. Not published by OCR; may be requested under the [Freedom
  of Information Act](/law/freedom-of-information-act/) and is likely to be redacted.
- Opening letters. Sent to both sides, stating the issues. Not usually published; sometimes released
  by the institution or the complainant, or obtained through a records request.
- Data request and institutional response. Often large. Not published; parts may be obtainable
  through records requests.
- Interview notes and investigative memoranda. Internal. Often withheld in part under FOIA
  exemptions for deliberative or personal material.
- Resolution letter or letter of findings, and the resolution agreement. OCR has published many of
  these in its online reading room. They are the core public record of a case.
- Monitoring reports and OCR's responses. Not routinely published. Can be requested.
- Closure letter. Sometimes published; often must be requested.

OCR has also maintained a public list of pending investigations showing the institution, the type of
discrimination and the date opened. A listing tells you a case is open, nothing more. How to request
these records is covered in the [civil-rights office records
template](/guides/template-foia-civil-rights-office-records/) and the guide to [federal agency
reading rooms](/guides/method-federal-agency-records-and-foia-reading-rooms/).

## Policy guidance sits alongside enforcement

OCR also issues guidance documents, such as a [Dear Colleague
letter](/glossary/dear-colleague-letter/), explaining how it reads the law. Guidance is not a
regulation. It has been issued, withdrawn and replaced repeatedly. Formal regulations go through
[notice and comment rulemaking](/glossary/notice-and-comment-rulemaking/) and can be challenged in
court. When a letter cites guidance, check whether that guidance is still in effect.

## Questions worth asking

- Was the case opened from a complaint or by the agency itself, and what issues did the opening
  letter list?
- Did the case close with a finding of violation, a resolution before a finding, or insufficient
  evidence, issue by issue?
- What period did the investigation cover, and which rules and guidance applied then?
- Has a closure letter been issued for every resolution agreement the institution has signed?
- What did the monitoring reports say, and did the agency ask for changes?
- Has a court or another agency examined the same conduct, and did it apply a different standard?
