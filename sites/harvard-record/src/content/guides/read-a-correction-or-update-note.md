Published work changes after publication. Newspapers correct errors, websites revise pages,
institutions edit statements, and journals correct or retract articles. A note explaining the change
is a sign that the publisher keeps an honest record. Reading such notes carefully tells you what was
wrong, what is now claimed, and how much of the original still stands.

## The main types

Terms vary by publisher, but these distinctions are widely used.

- Correction. A statement of fact in the original was wrong and has been fixed. The note usually
  says what was wrong and what is right.
- Clarification. The original was not strictly false but was unclear, incomplete or open to a
  misleading reading.
- Update. New information was added after publication, such as a response received later or a
  subsequent development. An update does not imply an error.
- Editor's note. A broader statement about the piece: a problem with sourcing, a conflict of
  interest, a decision to remove a name, or a concern about standards.
- Retraction. The publisher withdraws the piece, or a central claim, as unreliable.
- Removal. The piece is taken down. Good practice leaves a note explaining why. Sometimes there is
  none.

In scholarly publishing the parallel terms are correction or erratum (for errors), expression of
concern (a warning while a problem is investigated) and retraction. A retracted paper usually
remains available, marked as retracted, so the record is preserved.

## Where to look

- At the top or bottom of an article.
- On a separate corrections page, in print or online.
- In the dateline: "updated" followed by a later date or time.
- In a version history, on sites that keep one.
- For journal articles, in a linked notice on the article's page.

A changed time stamp with no note means something was altered and you have not been told what.

## How to read the note

1. Find the date of the note and compare it with the publication date. A correction within hours
   suggests a slip caught quickly. One made months later may follow a complaint or new evidence.
2. Identify exactly what changed. A good note states the original error. A weak one says only that
   "an earlier version misstated" something.
3. Judge how central the change is. A misspelled name or a wrong date is peripheral. A corrected
   amount, a changed attribution or a withdrawn allegation may affect the main point.
4. Ask whether the headline or opening was changed as well. If the article's conclusion depended on
   the corrected fact, check whether the conclusion was revised.
5. Look for more than one note. Several corrections on one piece may indicate a contested or
   difficult story.
6. Consider what prompted the change, if stated: a reader, the subject of the article, a legal
   demand, or the outlet's own review.

## What a correction does and does not mean

- A correction means one stated thing was wrong. It does not mean the rest is wrong. It does not
  certify that the rest is right.
- An outlet that corrects openly is generally more trustworthy than one that never does. All
  publishers make errors. The difference is whether they are recorded.
- A correction made at a subject's request is not an admission that the whole story was false.
- An update adding a denial does not weaken the original reporting. It completes it.
- A removal without explanation tells you very little. Articles are taken down for legal, privacy
  and editorial reasons, and occasionally because they were wrong.

## Changes without notes

Some changes are made silently. To detect them:

- Compare the page with an archived copy in a public web archive, if one exists.
- Compare with quotations of the original in other outlets or on social media.
- Check whether a syndicated or print version differs.

Institutional web pages, such as statements and policy pages, are edited without notes more often
than news articles are. When a page matters to you, save a copy with the date and web address the
first time you read it.

## When your source has been corrected

1. Reread the current version in full.
2. Check whether the specific fact you relied on changed.
3. If it did, update your own work and say so.
4. Cite the current version, and mention the correction if it bears on your point.
5. If you need to refer to the original text, for example to show what was first claimed, cite an
   archived copy with its date and make clear that it was later corrected.
6. Check whether other sources you use repeated the uncorrected version. Errors travel further than
   corrections.

## Writing your own correction notes

If you keep a public record of your own, the same standards apply.

- Fix the error promptly.
- Leave a dated note stating what was wrong and what is now right.
- Distinguish corrections from updates.

## What a note can and cannot tell you

A note can tell you that the publisher changed a specific thing on a specific date, and usually why.
It shows that someone is maintaining the record.

It cannot tell you about errors that were never noticed or never acknowledged. It rarely tells you
how the error happened. It does not tell you how many readers saw the first version and never saw
the fix.

## Common mistakes

- Citing a version that has since been corrected.
- Treating any correction as discrediting the entire piece.
- Treating an update as a correction.
- Assuming that an article with no correction note contains no errors.
- Overlooking a retraction notice on a scholarly article because the original remains online.
