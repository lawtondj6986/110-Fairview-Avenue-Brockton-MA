A research lab is a small enterprise inside a university. Its leader, the principal investigator, is
responsible for finding most of the money that pays for people, equipment and supplies. Where that
money comes from affects what students work on, how secure their funding is, and what obligations
come with it.

## The main sources

- Government grants. In the United States, agencies such as the National Institutes of Health and
  the National Science Foundation award grants through competitive peer review. A grant funds a
  described project for a set period, with a budget and reporting requirements.
- Private foundations. Charitable foundations fund research in their areas of interest, usually
  through an application process, sometimes by invitation.
- Industry. Companies fund research through sponsored research agreements, which are contracts. They
  commonly address who owns resulting inventions, whether the sponsor may review manuscripts before
  publication, and confidentiality.
- Gifts. A gift from an individual or foundation, given to support a lab or a field, with few or no
  deliverables. Gift money is flexible, which makes it prized. It also tends to receive less formal
  review than grants and contracts.
- Institutional funds. Start-up packages for new faculty, income from endowed chairs, internal seed
  grants, and departmental support.
- Funding that follows the student. Fellowships won by the student, training grants awarded to a
  program, and teaching assistantships paid by the department.

Most labs combine several of these.

## Direct and indirect costs

A grant budget has two parts. Direct costs pay for the project itself: salaries, stipends, supplies,
equipment and travel. Indirect costs, also called facilities and administrative costs or overhead,
are paid to the university to cover shared expenses such as buildings, utilities, libraries and
compliance offices. The indirect rate is set by agreement between the institution and the funder and
varies by funder.

For a student, the practical point is that grants benefit the institution as well as the lab.
Faculty who bring in large grants often have corresponding standing.

## Why the source matters to a student

- Stability. A stipend paid from a single grant depends on that grant. Ask when it ends and what
  happens if it is not renewed. A fellowship or a departmental guarantee is more secure.
- Freedom of topic. A grant or contract pays for a specific project. A student paid from it is
  expected to work on that project. Fellowship and gift funding usually allow more latitude.
- Publication. Academic norms and most university policies protect the right to publish. Industry
  agreements may allow a sponsor a short period to review a manuscript. Ask whether any agreement in
  the lab restricts or delays publication, because a thesis depends on publishable work.
- Intellectual property. University policy usually assigns ownership of inventions to the
  institution, with a share of income to inventors. Sponsored agreements may give the sponsor
  licensing rights. Ask before joining a project with commercial ties.
- Conflicts of interest. A principal investigator may hold equity in, or consult for, a company
  connected to the lab's work. Universities require such interests to be disclosed and managed. A
  student should know whether their project touches the adviser's outside interests.
- Dependence. When one person controls the money that pays a student's stipend, tuition and research
  costs, that person holds considerable power. See the guide on adviser power.
- Donors. A lab supported by a private donor may host that donor for visits, updates and events, and
  students may be asked to present their work. This is ordinary stewardship. A student is entitled
  to know who funds the lab and to raise concerns through the department or an ombuds office if an
  interaction feels inappropriate.

## Questions to ask before joining

Ask the principal investigator.

1. What funds would pay my stipend and tuition, and for how long?
2. What happens to my funding if that source ends?
3. Is my project tied to a specific grant's aims? How much room is there to change direction?
4. Does any funder have rights over publication or data?
5. Are there company relationships I should know about?

Ask current and former lab members.

1. Has anyone had a gap in funding? How was it handled?
2. How long do students take to graduate, and where do they go afterward?
3. Do students attend meetings with funders or donors, and how is that experienced?

Ask the department or program.

1. What funding does the program guarantee, and for how many years?
2. Is there bridge funding if an adviser loses a grant?
3. What is the process for changing labs?

## Where to find funding information yourself

- Acknowledgment and funding sections of the lab's published papers list sources.
- Federal agencies publish searchable databases of the grants they award, including project titles,
  amounts and dates.
- Conflict of interest disclosures appear in journal articles.
- The lab's website and the principal investigator's curriculum vitae often list current support.
- Tax filings of private foundations list the grants they made.

## What funding information can and cannot tell you

It can tell you how long a lab's money is likely to last, how concentrated it is in one source, and
what outside parties have a stake in the work.

It cannot tell you how the lab treats its members. It will often miss gifts, which are not always
public. A well-funded lab is not necessarily a good place to train, and a modestly funded one may be
excellent.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming admission to a program guarantees funding for the whole degree. Read the offer letter.
- Not asking about money because it feels impolite. It is a normal question.
- Equating a large lab budget with good mentoring.
- Overlooking restrictions that come with industry funding until a paper is ready to submit.
