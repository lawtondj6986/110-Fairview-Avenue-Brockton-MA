Many news reports rest on documents: court filings, released emails, financial records, internal
reports. Such reporting is often more reliable than reporting based on recollection, because a
document can be checked. It still passes through a reporter's reading, selection and summary. A
careful reader separates three layers: the document, what the document says, and what the article
says about it.

## Layer one: how the outlet knows

Look for the phrase that describes the reporter's access. The wording is chosen carefully.

- "Documents reviewed by" or "obtained by" the outlet. The reporters read the documents themselves.
- "According to court filings" or "public records show." The source is on a public record that you
  may be able to find.
- "According to a person familiar with the documents." The reporters may not have seen them. They
  are relaying a description.
- "Documents described to" the outlet. The same, stated openly.
- "As first reported by" another outlet. The claim is second-hand, and the original report is the
  one to read.

Each step away from the document adds a chance of error. Note which applies to each key claim, since
a single article may mix them.

## Layer two: what the document is

A claim "based on documents" is only as strong as the type of document.

- An allegation in a lawsuit is one party's unproven claim. See [the guide to reading a civil
  complaint](/guides/read-a-civil-complaint/).
- A finding in a court judgment or an agency decision is a conclusion reached after a process.
- An email shows what someone wrote. See [the guide to reading an email released in a
  document production](/guides/read-a-released-email/).
- An invoice or proposal shows what was priced or billed. See [the guide to reading an invoice
  or proposal as evidence](/guides/read-an-invoice-or-proposal/).
- An internal report reflects its scope and its author.
- A draft is not a final version.

Good articles name the type of document. If an article says only "documents show," look further down
for the detail, and be cautious if it never comes.

## Layer three: what the article adds

Sort each sentence.

- Direct quotation from a document. Strongest, though still selected.
- Paraphrase. Check whether hedges in the original survived. "Proposed" can become "arranged" in a
  paraphrase.
- Characterization. "Close ties," "extensive," "repeatedly." These are the writer's words. Look for
  the count or the example behind them.
- Inference. "The emails suggest" or "appear to show" signals the reporter's reading of the
  document.
- Context from other sources. Background and reaction, with their own sourcing.

Verbs carry weight. "Shows," "reveals" and "confirms" assert that something is true. "Says,"
"states" and "alleges" attribute it. "Suggests" and "indicates" mark interpretation. Notice which
verb is attached to which claim.

## Check the structure

- Headline. Often written by an editor, compressed, and stronger than the article. Do not cite a
  headline for a fact.
- First paragraphs. The summary. The qualifications tend to come later.
- The response. A fair article reports what the people and institutions concerned said, or that they
  declined or did not reply. Read the response in full. A specific denial, a narrow denial and a
  refusal to comment are three different things.
- What the outlet could not verify. Careful reports say so. That candor is a mark of reliability.
- Date. The article reflects what was known when it was published. Check for later updates and
  corrections. See [the guide to reading a correction or an update
  note](/guides/read-a-correction-or-update-note/).

## Trace the document

1. Look for a link, an embedded file or a named source. Many outlets publish the documents they rely
   on.
2. If the source is a court case, find the case name, court and number in the article, then look up
   the docket. See [the guide to reading a court docket](/guides/read-a-court-docket/).
3. If the source is an agency or a congressional committee, check its website for the release.
4. If the document is an institutional report, look on the institution's site.
5. If you cannot find it, note that. The claim then rests on the outlet's reading alone.

When you find the document, read the passage in context. Check the quotation, the date, the author,
and what comes before and after.

## Weigh independent confirmation

Many outlets repeating one outlet's report is one source, not many. Look for whether a second outlet
says it independently reviewed the documents, or whether a party to the documents confirmed them.
Republication adds reach, not evidence.

## Consider how the documents arrived

Documents reach reporters from litigants, lawyers, officials, committees and people with grievances.
The provider may have selected what to share. This does not make the documents false. It means the
set may be partial. Good reporting says what it can about provenance and seeks the other side.

## What a document-based report can tell you

- That specific documents exist and what they say, to the extent quoted.
- The reporter's informed reading of them.
- The responses of those involved.
- Where to find the primary source.

## What it cannot tell you

- More than the documents themselves support.
- What other documents, not obtained, would show.
- Whether statements inside the documents are true.

## Common mistakes

- Citing the headline.
- Dropping the article's attribution, so that "the lawsuit alleges" becomes a flat statement.
- Treating a quoted email as proof of what the email asserts.
- Counting repeats of one report as corroboration.
- Citing the article when the document is available. Where you can read the primary source, cite it,
  and credit the reporting that surfaced it.
- Ignoring the subject's response.
- Missing a later correction.

## How to cite one

Give the outlet, headline, date and web address. Describe the claim with the same degree of
certainty the article used, and name the kind of document behind it. This site's [sources
page](/sources/) separates primary documents from reporting about them, and the [methodology
page](/methodology/) explains why.
