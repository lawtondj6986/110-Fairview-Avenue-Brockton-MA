A chronology is a list of dated events, each tied to a document. It is the most useful working tool
in document research, because it forces every event to have a date and a source, and because gaps
and conflicts become visible when events are laid in order. It is also easy to misuse. This guide
covers how to build one and where its limits lie.

## Start with entries, not a story

Do not begin by writing a narrative and then looking for dates. Begin by reading documents and
pulling out one entry per event. Each entry should hold:

- the date of the event, as precisely as the document gives it;
- one plain sentence saying what happened, using the document's own level of certainty;
- the source, with a page or paragraph number;
- the source's grade (A1 to B2) and the claim's evidence state (stated intent, documented activity,
  verified implementation);
- the date you opened the source and checked it.

A fill-in version is in the [chronology entry template](/guides/template-chronology-entry/).

## Three dates that are often confused

Every document involves at least three dates, and mixing them is the most common chronology error.

- The event date: when the thing described took place.
- The document date: when the record was written or signed.
- The publication date: when the record became public, for example when a court unsealed it or an
  agency released it.

An email written in one year, describing a meeting the year before, and released a decade later has
three different dates. Enter the event under the event date, and note the other two in the source
field. Readers often assume that something "came out" at the time it happened. Stating the
publication date corrects that.

## Recording uncertain dates

Documents frequently give partial dates. Record exactly what you have and no more.

- If only the month is known, write the month and year. Do not supply a day.
- If a document says "last spring", record "spring of [year], per document dated [date]" and explain
  the inference.
- If two sources give different dates, enter both, each with its source, and flag the conflict. Do
  not average or pick silently.
- Use "on or before" and "on or after" when a document only sets a bound. A reply dated the 10th
  shows the original message existed on or before the 10th.
- Record time zones for emails when the hour matters. A message can appear to be sent a day earlier
  or later depending on how the headers were rendered.

Sort uncertain entries by the earliest possible date and mark them, so they do not appear more exact
than they are.

## Keep each entry at its evidence state

A chronology mixes kinds of events: proposals, acts and findings. Label them. "Proposed a gift" and
"gift received" are different entries with different states, even when they concern the same money.
If you only have the proposal, the chronology has no entry for the receipt. Leave that gap visible.
It is a finding in its own right.

Also separate what happened from when it was alleged. An allegation in a complaint filed in one year
about conduct in an earlier year can be entered twice: once under the filing date ("complaint filed
alleging...") and, if you include the underlying event at all, under the alleged date with the word
"alleged" and a pointer to the complaint.

## Sequence is not cause

Laying events in order invites the reader to see one causing the next. The chronology itself does
not show that. Two entries a week apart may be unrelated. Before writing "after", "following" or "in
response to" in any prose drawn from the chronology, check whether a document actually connects the
two events. If none does, state the two facts separately and let the dates speak. [The guide on
avoiding false patterns](/guides/method-avoiding-false-patterns/) in this series goes further.

Be equally careful with gaps. A year with no entries may mean nothing happened, or that records from
that year were not released, or that you have not found them. Say which, if you know.

## Practical steps

1. Collect your documents and give each a short, stable identifier.
2. Read each one fully and extract entries as you go. Do not rely on search hits alone.
3. Put entries in a spreadsheet or plain table, one row per event, sorted by date.
4. Merge duplicates. When two documents record the same event, keep one entry and list both sources.
5. Mark conflicts between sources in a notes column.
6. Reread the whole list in order. Look for impossible sequences, such as a reply dated before the
   message it answers. These usually point to a misread date, a time zone issue or a mislabeled
   document.
7. Only then write prose, citing entries as you go.

## What a chronology can and cannot tell you

It can tell you the order in which recorded events occurred, where sources agree and disagree, and
where the record goes silent. It can show that something was impossible, for example that a decision
could not have relied on a report written later.

It cannot tell you why anyone acted, what anyone knew at a given moment unless a document shows it,
or what happened in the periods the record does not cover. It reflects the documents that exist and
were released, which is a subset of what occurred. Keep a note at the top of the chronology stating
what document sets it draws on and the date it was last updated, so a reader knows its scope.
