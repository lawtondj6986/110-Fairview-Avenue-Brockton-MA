Alumni have standing that the general public lacks. Institutions want their goodwill, their gifts
and their votes in alumni elections where those exist. That standing does not create a legal right
to information, but it does mean a well-framed request is often answered.

## Know what kind of institution you are asking

Public universities are generally subject to their state's public records law. Anyone can file a
request, and the institution must respond within the time the statute sets, subject to exemptions.
The laws vary considerably by state. Some states exempt certain donor records or records of
affiliated fundraising foundations. Check the current statute and the university's public records
office page.

Private universities are generally not subject to public records laws. They are still required to
make certain things public: the annual security report, their annual tax return if they file one
(Form 990), and various consumer disclosures tied to federal student aid. Beyond those, disclosure
is voluntary.

## Requests that tend to get answers

These ask for things that already exist, are not confidential, and cost little to provide.

- Current policies: gift acceptance, naming, conflict of interest, sexual misconduct, research
  misconduct. Ask for the version in force and its adoption date.
- The date and substance of the last revision to a named policy.
- Published reports you cannot locate: a climate survey, an annual report of the misconduct office,
  a review report that was announced.
- The membership and charge of a committee.
- Whether a specific public commitment was carried out, and where that is documented.
- The three most recent Form 990 returns, which a filing organization must provide on request.
- Aggregate figures: number of complaints received in a year, number of gifts reviewed by a gift
  committee, number declined.
- The process: who decides, in what order, and with what appeal.

## Requests that usually do not

- Student records. Federal privacy law (FERPA) generally bars release of a student's education
  records without consent.
- Personnel files and the details of individual disciplinary cases.
- Legal advice and documents prepared for litigation.
- Identities of anonymous donors and the terms of individual gift agreements.
- Unpublished drafts and internal deliberations.

Asking for these is not wrong, but expect a refusal, and do not read the refusal as concealment. A
better approach is to ask for the aggregate or the process instead of the individual record.

## How to write the request

1. Address it to a specific office: the office that owns the policy, the secretary of the governing
   board, or the alumni association. Copy the communications office if the matter is of public
   interest.
2. Identify yourself and your connection: degree and class year.
3. Ask for a document or a fact, not an explanation of motives. "Please send the gift acceptance
   policy currently in force and the date it was adopted" will be answered sooner than "Why did the
   university take this money."
4. Number your questions and keep them few. Three precise questions are better than ten broad ones.
5. Cite the institution's own words where relevant, with date and source.
6. Give a reasonable date by which you hope to hear back, and say what you will do with the answer.
7. Keep the tone neutral. The person reading it probably had no role in the events.

## If you get no answer

- Follow up once, politely, after the date you gave, attaching the original.
- Try a second route: an elected alumni representative, the board secretary, a dean.
- Ask with others. A letter from a class committee or an alumni group carries more weight than one
  from an individual.
- Raise the question at a forum where leaders take questions, such as a reunion or town hall.
- Share the unanswered request with the student newspaper, which may have asked the same thing.
- At a public university, file a formal public records request. Follow the statute's format, and
  note the appeal route if it is denied.

Keep a dated log of what you sent, to whom, and what came back.

## Other levers alumni hold

- Votes, where alumni elect members of a governing or advisory board. Candidates can be asked for
  their positions.
- Giving. Some alumni restrict gifts to specific purposes or pause giving and say why. The effect of
  one donor is small, but development offices do record stated reasons.
- Volunteer roles: interviewing applicants, class committees, visiting committees. People in these
  roles are heard.

## What a response can and cannot tell you

A response gives you the institution's current official position and, with luck, a document. A
policy tells you what is supposed to happen.

A response does not show what happened in a specific case. Aggregate numbers depend on definitions,
so ask what is counted. A courteous reply with no document attached has answered a different
question from the one you asked. Note that and ask again.

## Common mistakes

- Sending a long letter that mixes requests with argument.
- Asking for confidential individual records and stopping at the refusal.
- Assuming a private university has the same disclosure duties as a public one.
- Failing to keep copies and dates.
