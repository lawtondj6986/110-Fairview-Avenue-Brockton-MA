Commercial paperwork such as quotes, proposals, purchase orders and invoices often surfaces in
document releases. It looks precise: line items, quantities, prices, names. That precision invites
over-reading. Each type of document proves one step in a transaction and no more. A price on a page
is not a payment, and a payment is not proof that anything was done.

## The stages of a transaction

A typical purchase of goods or services passes through these steps. Each produces its own record.

1. Inquiry. Someone asks what something would cost. Record: an email or message.
2. Quote, estimate or proposal. The seller states what it offers and at what price. This is an
   offer.
3. Acceptance. The buyer agrees. Record: a signed proposal, a contract, a purchase order, or a reply
   saying yes.
4. Performance. The goods are delivered or the work is done. Record: delivery receipts, shipping
   records, reports, results, work product, time sheets.
5. Invoice. The seller requests payment. Record: the invoice, with a number and date.
6. Payment. The buyer pays. Record: bank or card records, a receipt, a remittance advice, a ledger
   entry, an invoice marked paid.

A document from one stage is evidence of that stage. It is, at most, an indication about the others.

## What each document shows

- A quote or proposal shows that the seller offered specific items to a specific party at a stated
  price on a stated date. It does not show that the offer was accepted.
- A purchase order or signed contract shows agreement. It does not show delivery or payment.
- An invoice shows that the seller billed for the listed items. Invoices are sometimes issued before
  work is done (for deposits or prepayment), sometimes revised, and sometimes never paid.
- A pro forma invoice is a preliminary document, closer to a quote than a bill.
- A receipt or bank record shows that money moved. It may not show what for.
- A delivery record, lab report or other work product shows performance.

## How to read the document itself

1. Find the title. "Estimate," "quote," "proposal," "pro forma," "order confirmation," "invoice,"
   "statement" and "receipt" mean different things. If the title is missing, look for other cues,
   such as a validity period ("valid for thirty days" marks a quote) or payment terms and a due date
   (which mark an invoice).
2. Identify the parties. Who issued it, and to whom is it addressed? The party billed is not
   necessarily the person who used or benefited from the item. An assistant, a company or a third
   party may be named.
3. Note dates: issue date, validity period, due date, service or delivery dates.
4. Read each line item. Note the description, quantity, unit price and total. A listed item was
   priced or billed. Whether it was provided is a separate question.
5. Look for status marks: "paid," "void," "draft," "revised," a balance due, a signature, a stamp.
6. Check the document's number against others in the same set. Multiple versions with different
   items suggest negotiation.
7. Read the surrounding messages. The cover email often says whether the document is an offer, a
   reminder or a confirmation.

## What you need to go further

To say an item was ordered, look for an acceptance: a signature, a purchase order, a written yes, or
a deposit.

To say it was paid for, look for a payment record that matches the amount, date and parties. Partial
payments, deposits and payments by third parties are common.

To say the work was performed, look for the result: a delivered product, a report, a shipping
record, a later message that refers to the outcome.

Where these are absent, say so. "A proposal priced the service at a stated amount. The released
records do not show whether it was accepted, paid for or carried out" is a complete and accurate
sentence.

## Words that match the evidence

- Quote or proposal only: "was offered," "was priced," "was quoted."
- Signed order: "was ordered," "agreed to buy."
- Invoice only: "was billed for," "was invoiced."
- Payment record: "paid."
- Evidence of delivery or results: "received," "was performed," "was carried out."

Avoid "bought," "purchased," "obtained" and "underwent" unless you have evidence of both payment and
performance. Avoid attaching a line item to a named individual unless the document itself does.

## Special cautions

- Bundles and menus. Proposals often list options. A list of available services is not a list of
  services chosen.
- Technical items. A line item for a laboratory test, for example, describes a category of service.
  It does not show whose sample was tested, or that any sample was received.
- Authorship and authenticity. Establish where the document came from, whether it is complete, and
  whether other copies differ. See [the guide to reading an email
  released in a document production](/guides/read-a-released-email/).

## What commercial documents can and cannot tell you

They can establish contact between parties, the nature of goods or services under discussion,
prices, and dates. A series of them can show a relationship developing over time.

They cannot, alone, establish intent, use, or outcome. They do not show what the buyer knew or
wanted beyond what is written. One document without its counterparts leaves the transaction
unproven.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting a quoted price as an amount spent.
- Reporting an invoiced item as a completed service.
- Assuming the addressee was the beneficiary.
- Treating every line on a proposal as something that happened.
- Dropping the caveat when the claim is repeated later in the same piece.
