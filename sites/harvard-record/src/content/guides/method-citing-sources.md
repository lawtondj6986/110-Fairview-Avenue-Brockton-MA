A citation has one job. It should let a reader who has never met you, and has no reason to trust
you, find the exact words your claim rests on. Citation style matters far less than that. A
perfectly formatted reference to a 400-page report with no page number fails. An informal note that
gives the document, the page and where to get it succeeds.

## The stranger test

Imagine a careful reader with ordinary internet access and no special knowledge. Give them your
sentence and your citation. Within a few minutes they should be able to:

1. identify which document you mean, with no ambiguity;
2. obtain it, or learn exactly where it is held and what access requires;
3. go to the specific passage;
4. see that the passage says what you say it says.

If any step fails, the citation needs work. Test a sample of your own citations this way, ideally by
asking someone else to try.

## What every citation needs

- Who produced the source: the author, or the issuing body for an official document.
- What it is called: the exact title, and the type of document if the title does not make it clear.
- When it is dated: the publication or filing date. For web pages with no date, say so.
- Where the passage is: a page, paragraph, section, exhibit or entry number. This is called a
  pinpoint, and it is the part most often left out.
- Where to get it: a stable address or a formal identifier.
- When you checked it: the date you last opened the source and confirmed the passage.

## Pinpoints

Point as precisely as the document allows.

- Use the page numbers printed on the document. If the printed number and the file's page count
  differ, as they often do in a PDF with a cover and preliminary pages, give the printed number and
  say so, or give both.
- For court filings, cite numbered paragraphs where they exist. Paragraph numbers survive
  reformatting. Page numbers may not.
- For large document productions, use the page identifier stamped on each page.
- For statutes and regulations, cite the section and subsection.
- For audio and video, give a timestamp.
- For web pages without pagination, name the section heading and quote a few distinctive words so
  the reader can search for them.
- For tables and spreadsheets, give the table name, row and column.

## Citing particular kinds of source

Court records. Give the court, the case name, the case number, the document title, the docket entry
number, the filing date and the page or paragraph. With these, anyone can retrieve the document from
the court's own system.

Official reports. Give the issuing body, the title, the date and the page. Link to the copy on the
issuer's own site.

Statutes and regulations. Give the formal citation, such as the title and section of the United
States Code. The law can change, so note the date you consulted it.

News reporting. Give the outlet, the author, the headline, the publication date and the address. If
the article has been updated or corrected since publication, note the version date. If it sits
behind a paywall, say so.

Nonprofit filings. Give the organization's legal name, its Employer Identification Number, the form
type, the fiscal year end and the part or schedule.

Records obtained by request. Give the agency, the request number, the date of the release letter and
the page. Where possible, publish the released records so readers can see them.

Archived web pages. Give the original address, the archived address and the capture date.

## Cite what you actually read

If you read a newspaper's description of a court filing and did not read the filing, cite the
newspaper. Do not cite the filing as though you had opened it. The honest form is "as reported by"
or "quoted in". This keeps the source grade accurate, since reporting and a primary record are
graded differently (see `/methodology/`), and it tells the reader where a transcription error could
have entered.

When you later obtain the original, upgrade the citation and change the date checked.

## Quotation and paraphrase

Quote exactly, including spelling and punctuation. Mark any omission with an ellipsis and any
insertion with square brackets, and make sure neither changes the meaning. Keep enough of the
surrounding sentence that the quotation is not misleading on its own.

A paraphrase needs a pinpoint just as much as a quotation does. It is in paraphrase that wording
most often drifts upward from what the source says, for example from "discussed" to "agreed".
Compare the verbs.

Attribute the words to the right speaker. In a filing, a passage may be the party's own argument, a
quotation from a witness or an excerpt from an exhibit. In an email thread, text may belong to an
earlier author than the person who sent the message.

## Links, identifiers and permanence

A web address is a convenience, not a citation. Addresses break. Always give enough descriptive
detail that the source could be found if the link died. Prefer formal identifiers where they exist,
such as a docket and entry number or a digital object identifier (DOI) for scholarly work. Add an
archived copy for web pages. Link to the official origin, not to an unofficial mirror.

## Keep a source register

Maintain one list of every source you cite, each with a short identifier, full details, a grade and
the date checked. Cite by identifier in your working files, so that correcting a source's details in
one place fixes it everywhere. This site keeps such a register at `/sources/`. A fill-in source log
entry is in the Templates series under `/guides/`.

## What a citation does not do

A citation shows where a claim comes from. It does not make the claim true, and it does not transfer
the source's authority to your interpretation. Stacking many citations behind one sentence does not
help if they all trace back to a single origin. One precise pointer to the strongest source, with
its limits stated, serves the reader better.
