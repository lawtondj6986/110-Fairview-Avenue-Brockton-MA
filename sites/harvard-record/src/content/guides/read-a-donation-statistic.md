Figures about donations appear in announcements, reports, news articles and tax filings. Two figures
that seem to describe the same giving can differ widely and both be accurate, because they count
different things. Before using a donation statistic, establish four things: what was counted, over
what period, from whom, and to whom.

## What was counted

"Gave," "donated," "pledged," "committed" and "raised" are not synonyms.

- Cash received. Money that actually arrived in a given period.
- Pledge. A promise to give in the future, often paid in installments over years. Some pledges are
  legally binding and some are statements of intent. Pledges are sometimes not paid in full.
- Commitment. A fundraising term that can combine cash, pledges and expected bequests.
- Bequest intention. A stated plan to leave money in a will. It may be revocable and may be many
  years from being realized.
- Gift in kind. Property, equipment, securities, artwork or services, counted at an appraised or
  estimated value.
- Sponsored research and contracts. Payments for agreed work. These are not gifts, though they are
  sometimes mixed into totals of "funding" or "support."

Campaign totals announced by institutions generally follow fundraising counting conventions, which
may include pledges and planned gifts. Audited financial statements follow accounting rules and will
show different numbers for the same period. Neither is wrong. They answer different questions.

Find the verb and the noun in the claim. "Raised" in a campaign is not "received." "Funding" is not
necessarily "gifts."

## Over what period

- A single year, a multi-year campaign, or a lifetime total.
- Fiscal or calendar year. Many institutions use a fiscal year that does not match the calendar.
- Start and end points. A statement about giving "since" or "after" a date is silent on the period
  before. A statement about giving "between" two dates is silent on both sides.
- Date of what. A gift may be pledged in one year, paid across several, and announced in another.
  Figures dated by pledge and figures dated by payment will not align.

## From whom

A donor is not always one legal person.

- The individual.
- A spouse or family members.
- A private foundation controlled by the individual or family.
- A company the individual owns or controls.
- A donor-advised fund. This is an account at a sponsoring charity. The donor recommends grants, and
  the payment comes from the sponsor, so the recipient's records may show the sponsor's name.
- Intermediaries and associates. Gifts made by others at a person's suggestion or introduction are
  sometimes described as "facilitated," "arranged," "directed" or "secured" by that person. That
  money did not come from the person.

A figure may cover the individual only, or the individual and all related entities, or may add
facilitated gifts. Statements that say a person "gave" a sum and statements that say a person "gave
or arranged" a sum describe different things. Keep the two categories separate, and report both if
both are known.

## To whom

A recipient is not always one legal entity either.

- The university as a whole, or its central administration.
- One school, department, center or laboratory.
- An affiliated but legally separate body, such as a hospital, research institute or foundation.
- An outside organization associated with a faculty member, which is not the university at all.

A figure for gifts "to the university" may leave out affiliates. A figure in news coverage may
gather them all together. Check the scope before comparing.

## Who is reporting, and from what records

- The recipient's announcement. Usually limited to what the recipient's own records show, under the
  recipient's definitions.
- The donor's statement. May reflect intentions or totals across many recipients.
- A tax filing. A private foundation's annual return lists its grants by recipient and amount. A
  public charity's return gives total contributions but not donor names in the public copy. See the
  guide to reading a Form 990.
- A news report. Check which of the above it relies on.

Institutional gift records, especially older ones and those kept by individual units, can be
incomplete. A total "identified" in a review is the total found.

## A checklist before repeating a figure

1. Write out the claim in full: who gave what to whom, in what form, over what period, according to
   whom.
2. Mark each element the source leaves unstated.
3. Find the original source of the number, not a repetition of it.
4. Check whether the figure is a floor ("at least"), an estimate ("about") or an exact amount.
5. If comparing two figures, confirm they match on all four dimensions. If they do not, say how they
   differ.
6. Keep the qualifiers when you restate it.

## What a donation statistic can tell you

- The scale of a financial relationship, within its stated definitions.
- That a named source has put a number on record and can be held to it.
- With several sources, how the relationship was distributed over time and across units.

## What it cannot tell you

- What the donor received in return, such as access, titles or recognition. See /donor-pathways/.
- Why the gift was made or accepted.
- Who knew about it.
- Gifts that left no record, or records that were not searched.

## Common mistakes

- Treating pledges as payments.
- Adding figures from different sources that overlap.
- Comparing a lifetime total with an annual figure.
- Counting facilitated gifts as the facilitator's own money, or leaving them out without saying so.
- Treating "to the university" as including every affiliate.
