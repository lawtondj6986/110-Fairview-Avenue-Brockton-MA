A research university is full of people who are neither students nor regular employees. Visiting
scholars, research affiliates, collaborators, contractors, alumni, donors, guests of faculty and
members of the public all move through its buildings. The systems that decide who may enter where,
and with what title, are spread across departments, central offices and building managers. Because
those systems are decentralized and lightly documented, a person can acquire an affiliation, an ID
card and access to a lab without any central review. This primer explains the usual machinery.
Arrangements vary widely between institutions and even between schools within one.

## Standing versus physical access

It helps to separate two things. Standing is a person's formal relationship with the institution: a
title, an appointment or an affiliation. Physical access is the ability to enter buildings, labs and
other spaces. Standing usually leads to an ID card and some physical access, but each is granted by
a different office, and either can exist without the other. A person with no title can be escorted
into a lab as a guest. A person with a title may hold an ID card that opens almost nothing.

## Kinds of affiliation

Universities use many titles for people outside regular employment. The glossary entries on
[academic appointment](/glossary/academic-appointment/), [affiliate](/glossary/affiliate/) and
[visiting fellow](/glossary/visiting-fellow/) explain common ones. Typical categories include:

- Visiting scholars, fellows and professors, usually appointed for a fixed term to pursue research
  or teach.
- Research affiliates or associates, who collaborate with a faculty member and may or may not be
  paid.
- Courtesy, adjunct or lecturer appointments, which may carry teaching duties or none.
- [Honorary titles](/glossary/honorary-title/), which recognize a person without conferring
  employment.
- [Emeritus status](/glossary/emeritus-status/) for retired faculty.
- Sponsored guests or associates, a residual category for people a department wants to give an ID
  card or network account.
- Members of [advisory boards](/glossary/advisory-board/), councils and committees, who may be given
  recognition and invitations rather than a formal title.

Unpaid titles are usually processed by a department administrator and approved by a department chair
or dean. Some require a short form, a curriculum vitae and the signature of a sponsoring faculty
member. Paid appointments go through human resources and, for faculty-rank titles, the provost's
office.

## Who can grant them

In practice, the most important figure is usually the sponsoring faculty member. A professor who
wants a collaborator, visitor or supporter to have standing typically initiates the request. The
department chair approves, often on the professor's word. Larger schools may have an office of
academic affairs that reviews titles for consistency. Central review of unpaid affiliations is
uncommon.

Several features of this system matter.

- Affiliations may have no fixed end date, or an end date that is renewed routinely.
- Requirements for background checks vary. They may apply to employees but not to unpaid affiliates,
  or only to people working with minors or in sensitive areas.
- An affiliation may be granted to a donor or a donor's associate without any connection to the gift
  review process. The [primer on gift acceptance](/guides/primer-gift-acceptance-and-vetting/)
  explains why those systems rarely talk to each other.
- The institution may not keep a central list of all affiliates.

## ID cards and key cards

Once a person has standing, an ID office issues a card. Access privileges on the card are usually
set separately by whoever manages each building or space: a facilities manager, a department
administrator or a lab manager. Electronic [access control](/glossary/access-control/) systems log
each card swipe at controlled doors. Keys, codes and propped doors leave no such record.

Access tiers commonly distinguish public spaces, general building access, after-hours access, office
suites, labs and restricted areas. Restricted areas, such as biosafety labs, animal facilities,
clinical spaces and data centers, usually require training and approval by the responsible safety or
compliance office in addition to building access. Residence halls are usually managed by student
housing and have separate rules.

Network accounts, email addresses and library privileges are granted through further systems, often
automatically once an affiliation exists. A university email address can lend a person apparent
standing beyond what the affiliation actually confers.

## Visitors and guests

A [visitor policy](/glossary/visitor-policy/) sets the rules for people with no affiliation. Many
campuses are physically open, especially outdoor spaces and some buildings during business hours.
Others require visitors to register, sign in at a front desk, wear a badge or be escorted. Labs and
offices commonly let faculty bring guests, sometimes with no record beyond a calendar entry. Events
bring in large numbers of people under event-specific rules.

A broader [campus access policy](/glossary/campus-access-policy/) may set out who may use which
spaces and when, and the conditions under which access can be restricted. Under the [Clery
Act](/law/clery-act/), institutions that take part in federal student aid programs must describe
their policies on security of and access to campus facilities in an [annual security
report](/glossary/annual-security-report/). Those statements tend to be general.

## Revoking access

Access can be removed by ending an affiliation, deactivating a card or issuing a formal notice that
a person is no longer allowed on campus. [Campus police](/glossary/campus-police/) or security
usually handle such notices, often called trespass warnings or persona non grata notices. The legal
basis and procedure differ between public universities, which are government entities, and private
ones, which act as property owners, and they also differ by state. A [no-contact
order](/glossary/no-contact-order/) may restrict contact with particular people without barring
someone from campus.

Revocation depends on each system being updated. An affiliation can end while the ID card, email
account or a lab's door access stays active.

## A worked hypothetical

A professor asks the department to name an engineer from a partner company as a research affiliate
so that the engineer can attend lab meetings. The department administrator completes a form, the chair signs
it, and the ID office issues a card. The lab manager adds the card to the lab's door list. The
affiliation has no end date. No background check is required for unpaid affiliates in this
department.

Two years later, the partnership ends. Nobody updates the affiliation.
The engineer's card still opens the lab, and the email account remains active. When the matter is
later reviewed, the only records are the original form, the card system's swipe log, a few calendar
invitations and the engineer's university email.

## What the documents look like

- Appointment or affiliation request: a form or memo naming the person, the title, the sponsor and
  the term. Internal.
- Approval signatures: chair, dean or academic affairs. Internal.
- Appointment letter: issued to the person, stating title and conditions. Held by the person and the
  department.
- Directory listing: a public web page or directory entry. Public while it exists, sometimes
  recoverable later from web archives.
- ID card record: issue date, photo and category. Internal.
- Access control records: door lists, permission changes and swipe logs. Internal and often kept
  only for a limited period under the institution's [records retention
  policy](/glossary/records-retention-policy/).
- Training and safety approvals for restricted spaces: internal.
- Visitor logs and event lists: internal, often brief and short-lived.
- Revocation or trespass notices: internal, sometimes reflected in a [daily crime
  log](/glossary/daily-crime-log/) if police were involved.
- Policy statements: visitor and access policies and the annual security report. Public.

## Questions worth asking

1. What title or affiliation did the person hold, who sponsored it, and who approved it?
2. Did the affiliation have an end date, and was it renewed?
3. Was any background check or conduct review required for that category?
4. What physical access came with the affiliation, and who set it?
5. Do access logs exist for the relevant period, and how long are they kept?
6. Was the affiliation connected to a gift or donor relationship, and did gift review consider it?
7. When the relationship ended, were the title, card, email and door access all revoked?
