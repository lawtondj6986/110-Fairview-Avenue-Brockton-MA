Before a document can support a claim, you need reasonable grounds to believe it is what it appears
to be: produced by the stated author, on roughly the stated date, and not altered since. This is a
question about the document, and it comes before any question about whether its contents are true. A
genuine letter can contain false statements. A forged letter is evidence of nothing but the forgery.

## Start with provenance

Provenance means the chain of custody from the document's origin to you. It is the strongest test,
and it should come first.

- Find the official origin. A court filing should be obtainable from the court's own docket system.
  An agency record should appear on the agency's site or in its response to a records request. An
  institutional report should be on the institution's own domain.
- Download from that origin yourself. If you first saw the document on social media, a blog or a
  mirror, treat that copy as a lead and go and get the official copy.
- Compare the two copies. Page counts, exhibit numbers and visible stamps should match. Any
  difference needs an explanation.
- Record the route. Note the address, the date you downloaded the file and any identifier such as a
  docket entry number.

If you cannot trace a document to an official origin, you may still be able to rely on established
reporting that describes it, graded as reporting and cited as such. You should not present the
untraced file as an authenticated record.

## Examine the document itself

Internal checks cannot prove a document genuine, but they catch many problems.

- Formal features. Court filings in electronic systems typically carry a header or footer with a
  case number, a document number, a filing date and a page count. Check that these are present,
  consistent across pages, and consistent with the docket.
- Letterhead, signatures and reference numbers. Compare with other documents known to come from the
  same office in the same period.
- Internal consistency. Dates should fall in a possible order. Titles and office names should match
  those in use at the time. Addresses and phone formats should fit the period.
- Language. A document that uses a term, a product name or an organizational name that did not exist
  on its stated date deserves a hard look.
- Completeness. Check page numbering and exhibit lists. A missing page or attachment can change the
  meaning of what remains.

## Examine the file, with caution

Digital files carry metadata, such as creation dates, software names and author fields. Metadata can
be useful and is easily misread.

- A creation date often records when a file was converted or saved, not when the document was
  written. A scanned letter from decades ago will show the scan date.
- Author fields often hold the name of whoever owns the software license or last exported the file.
- Metadata can be edited and can be stripped. Its absence is not suspicious by itself.

Treat metadata as a source of questions, not conclusions. If you draw on it, say exactly what field
you read and what it does and does not show.

For images of documents, look for signs of cropping or of text placed over a background. A
screenshot removes context by design. Find the full item before relying on it.

## Test the contents against other records

A genuine document sits in a web of other records. Check a few points where it touches them.

- If it mentions a meeting, a filing or a payment, look for an independent record of that event.
- If it is part of a thread or series, find the neighboring items. Replies and forwards should quote
  it consistently.
- If an official body has referred to it, for example by quoting it in a report or a court opinion,
  compare the quotation with your copy.
- If established outlets have reported on it, check whether they describe obtaining it from an
  official source and whether the passages they quote match.

Agreement on several independent points raises confidence. A single disagreement should stop you
until it is explained.

## Redactions and versions

Released documents are often redacted, which means parts have been blacked out or removed before
release. Do not guess what lies under a redaction, and do not attempt to recover redacted text from
a file. Note that the redaction exists and where.

Documents also exist in versions: drafts, signed copies, amended filings. Establish which version
you have. A draft agreement shows intent. Only a signed and dated copy, or another record of
execution, shows that it was entered into. An amended complaint replaces an earlier one, and the two
may differ in what they allege.

## Decide, and record the decision

After these checks, place the document in one of three groups.

1. Authenticated: obtained from the official origin, or matched to an official copy.
2. Probably authentic but not traced: consistent in every check, reported by established outlets,
   but you have not obtained an official copy. Cite the reporting, and say so.
3. Unverified: origin unknown or checks failed. Do not rely on it. Keep it as a lead.

Write down what you checked, when, and the result. A [checklist for
this](/guides/template-document-verification-checklist/) is in the Templates series.

## What authenticity does not establish

An authentic document shows that its author wrote those words. It does not show that the words are
accurate, complete or sincere. A genuine email proposing a plan is evidence of stated intent,
nothing further. A genuine complaint is evidence that allegations were made. Authenticity is the
first gate, and the claim still has to pass the others.
