Every university accepts most gifts without anyone looking closely at the donor. That is by design:
the great majority of gifts are small, routine and unremarkable. A smaller set of gifts is meant to
receive individual review, and a still smaller set is meant to reach senior leaders or the governing
board. Whether a problematic gift is caught depends on whether it falls into the right set, and
whether the people reviewing it have the information they need. This primer explains how the system
is usually built and where it tends to fail. Structures vary considerably between institutions.

## The policy framework

Most universities have a written [gift acceptance policy](/glossary/gift-acceptance-policy/). It
typically says who may accept gifts on the institution's behalf, which kinds of gifts need special
approval, and which kinds the institution will not accept at all. Common triggers for review
include:

- gifts above a size threshold set by the institution;
- non-cash gifts such as real estate, private company stock, art or other [in-kind
  gifts](/glossary/in-kind-gift/);
- gifts with unusual restrictions, obligations or naming rights;
- gifts that would create a new program, center or position;
- gifts from foreign sources, which may carry federal reporting obligations (see [Section
  117](/law/higher-education-act-section-117/));
- gifts from donors or sources flagged for possible reputational, legal or ethical concern;
- gifts where the donor stands to benefit, such as a company funding work in which it has a
  commercial interest (see [donor conflict of interest](/glossary/donor-conflict-of-interest/)).

The policy is usually supplemented by a naming policy, an anonymous-gift policy,
conflict-of-interest rules and sometimes a separate procedure for [reputational risk
review](/glossary/reputational-risk-review/). The guide on [reading a gift acceptance
policy](/guides/read-gift-acceptance-policy/) explains how to read such documents closely.

## Who does the vetting

[Donor due diligence](/glossary/donor-due-diligence/) is the work of checking a donor and a gift for
problems. It usually starts in the development office. Prospect researchers may run searches for
litigation, regulatory actions, sanctions lists and adverse news. If something is found, the matter
is escalated.

Escalation paths differ. Common arrangements include a [gift acceptance
committee](/glossary/gift-acceptance-committee/) made up of senior development, finance and legal
staff; a separate reputational review group that may include communications staff, the general
counsel and senior academic officers; and, for the largest or most sensitive gifts, the president or
a committee of the [governing board](/glossary/governing-board/). The general counsel's office often
reviews legal risk, and the finance office reviews valuation and accounting.

Some institutions keep a list of donors who require pre-approval before any further solicitation or
acceptance. Such a list only works if fundraisers across the institution check it and if it is
updated.

## How a decision is made

A typical review asks a set of practical questions. Is the money lawfully obtained and lawfully
given? Does the gift comply with tax and charitable law? Can the institution meet the conditions?
Does accepting it compromise academic freedom or independence? Would association with the donor harm
the institution's reputation or its relationship with students, faculty and the public?

Possible outcomes include acceptance, acceptance with conditions (such as no public recognition, a
changed purpose, or anonymity), deferral pending more information, and refusal. Anonymity is itself
a contested condition. An [anonymous gift](/glossary/anonymous-gift/) is anonymous to the public but
almost never to the institution, and anonymity can be offered as a way to accept money from a donor
the institution does not want to be seen with. Professional [fundraising ethics
codes](/glossary/fundraising-ethics-codes/) address honesty and donor rights, but they leave
acceptance decisions to each institution.

## Where it breaks down

Failures tend to cluster at a few recurring points.

Threshold gaps. Review is often triggered by the size of a single gift. A series of smaller gifts,
gifts split across schools, or gifts routed to a faculty member's discretionary account may never
reach the threshold.

Decentralization. In a decentralized university (see [who decides in a decentralized
university](/guides/who-decides-in-a-decentralized-university/)), schools and centers may accept
gifts under their own authority. Information about a donor held in one school may not reach another.
A central list may exist but not be consulted.

Intermediaries. Money that arrives through a foundation, a [donor-advised
fund](/glossary/donor-advised-fund/), a company or another person can hide the original source.
Vetting the named donor does not vet the money behind it. See
[intermediary](/glossary/intermediary/) and [introduced donor](/glossary/introduced-donor/).

Who raises the flag. Escalation often depends on the fundraiser or faculty member closest to the
donor deciding that a concern exists. Those people also have the strongest incentive to see the gift
succeed.

Timing. Review may happen after cultivation is advanced, a pledge is agreed, or the donor has
already been introduced to faculty and leaders. Refusing at that stage is harder, and the
institution may already have conferred access the gift review never considered.

Non-gift relationships. Gift review covers gifts. It may not cover a donor who sits on an advisory
board, visits a lab, or is given an affiliation or campus access without giving at that time. The
[primer on campus access](/guides/primer-campus-access/) covers that machinery.

No look-back. Once a donor is cleared, later information may never prompt a fresh review. Some
institutions require periodic re-review of repeat donors. Many do not.

Thin records. When a decision is made by a small group or by one senior officer, the reasons may be
recorded briefly or not at all. Later, no one can reconstruct who knew what.

## A worked hypothetical

A company offers a gift to an engineering center through its corporate foundation. The amount is
below the institution's threshold for central review, so the center's director and the school's
development officer process it locally. The development officer checks the foundation, which raises
no concerns. Nobody checks whether the parent company sells equipment the center evaluates in its
published testing work, because the foundation, not the company, is the legal donor.

Two years later, the company offers a larger gift that does trigger central review. The committee
notices the overlap between the company's products and the center's testing work. It accepts the new
gift on condition that the center disclose the funding in its publications. The minutes record the
decision but not the discussion, and nobody revisits the earlier gift.

In this example each step followed some rule, and the outcome still raises the questions a reviewer
would most want answered: who knew, when, and why acceptance was judged acceptable.

## What the documents look like

- Policy documents: the gift acceptance policy, naming policy and related procedures. Often public,
  sometimes internal.
- Due diligence memo: a short internal summary of searches run on a donor and what they found.
- Escalation request: an email or form sending a gift to committee review.
- Committee materials: agenda, briefing memo, minutes and decision record. Internal. Minutes are
  often brief.
- Approval or sign-off form: the signatures required under policy for a given gift.
- Restricted-donor or pre-approval list: an internal list of names requiring special handling.
- Gift agreement with conditions: the signed agreement, which may record anonymity or recognition
  limits.
- Later reviews: if a gift is questioned, an internal or external review report, sometimes public.
  See the [primer on internal investigations](/guides/primer-internal-investigations/).

Federal reporting of certain foreign gifts and contracts, and an institution's Form 990, are among
the few public traces. Most vetting records are internal.

## Questions worth asking

1. What does the policy require for a gift of this type, size and source, and was that process
   followed?
2. Who was the legal donor, and who was the source of the money?
3. Who ran the due diligence, which searches were run, and what did they find?
4. Who decided, at what level, and is there a written record of the reasons?
5. Were conditions such as anonymity imposed, and why?
6. Did the donor receive access, appointments or advisory roles that the gift review did not
   consider?
7. Were earlier gifts from the same source reviewed again when new information emerged?
