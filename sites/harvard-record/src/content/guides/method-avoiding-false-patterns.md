People are very good at seeing patterns, including patterns that are not there. In document research
the risk is specific. Two true facts placed side by side can imply a third claim that no source
supports: that the facts are connected, that one caused the other, or that the people involved acted
together. This guide explains how that happens and how to test a pattern before you write it.

## What adjacency looks like

Adjacency means two facts are near each other in some respect.

- In time: a gift is recorded in one month and an appointment is made the next.
- In place: two people attended the same event or worked in the same building.
- In documents: two names appear in the same email, list or filing.
- In networks: two people share an acquaintance, a board or a funder.
- In topic: two projects concern the same field.

Coordination means people acted together by agreement toward a shared end. Causation means one event
brought about another. Neither follows from adjacency. Each needs its own evidence.

## Why adjacency is cheap

In any large body of records, coincidences are certain. Universities, foundations and professional
fields are small worlds. Prominent people attend the same conferences, sit on overlapping boards and
know the same intermediaries. A donor who gives widely will have given near almost any event you
choose. A person with a large address book will have many notable names in it.

Three effects make false patterns especially likely.

Selection. You started with a subject and collected records about it. Everything you hold is
connected to that subject by the way you gathered it. The connections among the items may be an
artifact of the collecting.

Base rates. A base rate is how often something happens in general. If most gifts to a department are
followed within a year by some appointment, because departments make appointments every year, then a
gift followed by an appointment tells you nothing.

Hindsight. Once you know how events turned out, earlier facts seem to point there. People at the
time did not have that knowledge.

## Tests to apply

Before writing a sentence that connects two facts, run these checks.

1. Is there a document that connects them? Look for a record in which someone states the connection:
   an email saying the appointment was made because of the gift, an agreement conditioning one on
   the other, an official finding. If such a document exists, cite it and attribute the connection
   to it.
2. What is the ordinary explanation? Write down the most mundane account of each fact separately. If
   each is fully explained without the other, the pattern has no work to do.
3. How often does this pairing occur anyway? Consider how many similar gifts, meetings or mentions
   exist that led to nothing.
4. Does the timing actually work? Check exact dates. Many apparent sequences collapse when the
   second event turns out to have been decided before the first.
5. Are these the same people and the same things? Confirm identities. Shared names, similar
   organization names and reused project titles create false links.
6. What would you expect to see if the connection were real, and is it there? Coordination usually
   leaves traces, such as scheduling, instructions and follow-up. Their absence does not disprove a
   link, but it leaves the link unsupported.

If only the first test can turn adjacency into a stated connection, that is the right outcome. The
others tell you how much weight even a curiosity deserves.

## How implication gets into text

You can imply coordination without ever asserting it. Watch for these habits.

- Juxtaposition. Placing two unrelated facts in consecutive sentences, or in one sentence joined by
  "while", "as" or "meanwhile".
- Connective words. "After", "following", "amid" and "in the wake of" describe sequence but are read
  as cause.
- Loaded nouns. "Network", "circle", "web", "orbit" and "ties" assert a structure that the records
  may not show.
- Diagrams. A line between two names on a chart is a claim. If you cannot label the line with a
  specific, sourced relationship, do not draw it.
- Lists and counts. Grouping people under one heading implies they share something beyond the
  heading.
- Rhetorical framing. "It is unclear whether the two were connected" plants the connection while
  appearing cautious. If you have no evidence of a link, do not raise one.

## Writing adjacency honestly

When two nearby facts are both relevant, state them separately, each with its source, and say what
the record does and does not show about any link.

A workable form is: "The gift was recorded in [month]. The appointment was made in [month]. We found
no document connecting the two." The last sentence matters. It tells the reader you looked, and it
stops the juxtaposition from doing unspoken work. If the facts are not both relevant on their own
terms, leave one out.

This site's editorial rule, set out at `/methodology/`, is that two facts side by side are not
presented as connected unless a source connects them.

## Fairness to people

False patterns do the most harm when they involve named individuals. A person who shared a room, an
email or a funder with someone else has not thereby done anything. Describe the specific documented
fact and stop. Where a reader might infer more, say that no wrongdoing is implied.

## A final check

Read your draft as a skeptical stranger would. Mark every place where you feel the text suggesting
more than it states. For each, either find the document that supports the suggestion and cite it, or
rewrite until the suggestion is gone.
