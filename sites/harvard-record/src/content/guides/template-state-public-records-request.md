Every U.S. state has a law giving the public a right to request records from state and local
government bodies. The laws go by different names, including public records, open records, freedom
of information, right to know and sunshine laws. They differ in real ways: who may request, which
bodies are covered, how long an agency has to answer, what may be charged, what is exempt and how to
appeal.

This template is deliberately generic. Before sending it, read the current text of the law in the
state concerned, or the guidance published by that state's attorney general, secretary of state or
public records office, and adjust the letter to match. This is not legal advice.

## Who is covered, and who is not

These laws apply to government bodies: state agencies, cities, towns, counties, school districts,
public authorities and, in most states, public colleges and universities. Coverage of legislatures
and courts varies and is often limited.

Private universities are generally not covered. A private institution is not a government body, and
a state public records law usually gives no right to its internal records, even if it receives
public funds. There are narrow exceptions in some states for particular functions or records, and
these vary too much to summarize. Check the law of the state concerned.

Records about a private university may still be held by a public body that deals with it. Examples
include:

- a state agency that licenses, regulates or funds the institution;
- a state attorney general's office that oversees charities;
- a city or town that issued permits or signed agreements with it;
- a municipal police department or prosecutor that handled a matter involving it;
- a public university that collaborated with it or shared a grant.

A request to that public body can reach its correspondence with the private institution, subject to
the exemptions in that state's law.

## The template

```text
[Date]

[Records Access Officer / Public Records Officer / Custodian of Records]
[Agency or municipal department name]
[Address or email, from the agency's website]

Re: Public records request under [name of the state's public records
    law and its citation]

Dear [title or name]:

Under [name of law], I request [copies of / the opportunity to inspect]
the following public records:

1. [Specific description: type of record, subject, the office or
   official that created or received it, and any reference number.]

2. [Further category, if any.]

Date range: [start date] through [end date].

To help locate the records: [identifying details, such as the name of
a program, a contract or permit number, or the names of the offices
that corresponded].

Format
Please provide the records in electronic form, by email, if they exist
electronically. [If not, please tell me the cost of paper copies before
making them.]

Fees
If there is a charge for this request, please send me a written
estimate before doing any work that would incur a fee. [If the law
allows a waiver or reduction for requests in the public interest: I
request a fee waiver because (reason).]

Exemptions
If you conclude that any record or part of a record is exempt from
disclosure, please identify the specific exemption that applies,
release the non-exempt portions, and tell me how I may appeal.

Timing
I understand that [name of law] requires a response within [the period
stated in the law]. If you expect to need longer, please let me know
when I can expect the records.

[If the law requires it: a statement of residency, a statement that the
request is not for a commercial purpose, or another required element.]

If any part of this request is unclear, or could be narrowed to make it
easier to fulfil, please contact me at [email / phone].

Thank you for your help.

Sincerely,
[Full name]
[Mailing address]
[Email address]
[Phone number]
```

## Notes on adapting it

Name the law. Cite the state statute correctly in the subject line and first sentence. Many states
publish a model request letter. If one exists, prefer it.

Find the designated officer. Many agencies and municipalities name a records access officer or
custodian on their website, and some require requests to go to that person or through an online
portal.

Ask for records, not answers. Like the federal law, state laws generally give access to existing
records and do not require an agency to answer questions or compile new documents.

Fill in the response period from the statute. Do not rely on memory or on another state's rule.
Deadlines range widely, and some laws only require a response within a "reasonable" time.

Check the fee rules. States differ on whether agencies may charge for search time, redaction time or
only copies, and on waivers. Always ask for an estimate first.

Check who may request. A few states limit the right to their own residents or citizens. Most do not.
Most do not require you to give a reason, though some treat commercial requests differently.

Expect exemptions. Common ones cover personal privacy, personnel and student records, active
investigations, attorney-client communications and internal deliberations. Federal student privacy
law also restricts what public colleges may release about identifiable students. The details are
state-specific.

Keep it narrow. A request for one contract, or for correspondence between two named offices over six
months, is answered far faster than "all records concerning" a subject.

## After you send it

- Note the date sent and the response date the law sets.
- If the deadline passes, send a short, polite reminder citing your original request.
- If you receive a denial or heavy redactions, the response should cite the exemption. Appeal routes
  differ: some states have an administrative appeal to a state official or commission, and others
  require going to court. The agency's response or the state's guidance will say which.
- Keep the agency's cover letter with the records and cite both.

## Using the records

Records released by a public body are primary official records of what that body created or
received. Correspondence from an outside party found in the file shows what that party wrote. A
response that no records were found describes the agency's search, and should be reported in those
terms.
