Lesson 1 sorted sources by kind. This lesson sorts claims by how far the evidence reaches. Most mistakes in reading a university's record are not invented facts. They are real facts reported one step further along than any document supports.

## What you will learn

- The three evidence states: stated intent, documented activity and verified implementation
- The fourth label, nothing on record, and why it is not the same as "did not happen"
- Which verbs fit each state
- How a claim gets upgraded by accident as it is retold

## Three states and a fourth label

Stated intent means a record shows that something was proposed, promised, planned or announced. The record does not show that it happened. A proposal, a draft agreement, a pledge, a strategic plan and a statement that a university "will" do something all sit here.

Documented activity means a record shows that something was done. A signed agreement, a payment in a financial statement, an appointment letter or a published policy shows the act. It does not show what the act produced.

Verified implementation means an official finding or an independent result confirms both the activity and what came of it. An audit, a court finding, an agency's monitoring report or a commissioned review that traced what happened belongs here.

The fourth label is nothing on record. You looked in defined places and found no public document. That is a statement about your search, not about the world.

This site applies these states to every claim. The definitions are on the [methodology page](/methodology/), and the full method is in the guide to the [three-state discipline](/guides/method-three-state-discipline/). The glossary entry on [stated intent versus documented activity](/glossary/stated-intent-vs-documented-activity/) gives a short version.

## An ordinary example

Suppose a company that makes laboratory equipment and a hypothetical university discuss a research partnership. Four documents exist.

A slide deck from the company describes a five-year program with a shared laboratory. That is stated intent. It shows what the company pitched.

A university news release says the two parties "have agreed to launch" the program. This is still close to stated intent. A news release records what the institution announced. It may refer to a signed agreement, but it is not the agreement.

The university's sponsored research report for the following year lists an award from the company. That is documented activity. Money was committed or received under an agreement. The report does not say whether the shared laboratory was built.

Three years later, a published review by the university's research office describes the laboratory, its staff and its output, and an outside auditor's report covers the spending. That is verified implementation, to the extent the review and audit actually address each point.

Each document is genuine. Each supports a different sentence. A reader who had only the slide deck and wrote "the university opened a shared laboratory" would be making a claim that none of the evidence in hand supports.

## Match the verb to the state

The discipline shows up in verbs.

- For stated intent: proposed, offered, planned, announced, pledged, said it would.
- For documented activity: signed, paid, received, appointed, adopted, published.
- For verified implementation: an audit found, the review reported, the court held, the agency confirmed.
- For nothing on record: "A search of the following sources on this date found no record of".

Watch for words that sound like activity but record intent. "Launched", "established" and "committed" often appear in announcements before anything exists. An amount "of up to" a figure is a ceiling, not a payment. A guide on [invoices and proposals](/guides/read-an-invoice-or-proposal/) covers documents that look like proof of a transaction and are not.

## How claims get upgraded by accident

Upgrades rarely come from dishonesty. They come from compression.

A summary drops the qualifier. "The university announced plans for a center" becomes "the university's center" in the next article and "the center's work" in the one after. A headline uses a stronger verb than the text under it. A later writer cites the earlier article, not the document, and inherits the upgrade without seeing it.

Time does the same thing. An announcement that is several years old feels like history, and readers assume the thing was done. The record may show no such thing. The guide on [following up on promises](/guides/follow-up-on-promises-after-a-scandal/) explains how to check whether a public commitment was carried out.

The defense is mechanical. For each claim, find the most advanced thing that a document shows directly, read that document yourself, and write the claim at that level.

## Exercise

Use the university you chose in Lesson 1.

1. Find an announcement by the university that is at least two years old and that promises something specific: a new center, a new policy, a new office, a review, or a fundraising goal.
2. Write down, in the announcement's own words, each distinct thing it says will happen. Aim for three to six items.
3. For each item, search the university's website, its later annual or financial reports, its policy pages and news coverage for later documents.
4. For each item, record: the best later document you found, its date, its source grade from Lesson 1, and the state it supports.
5. Where you found nothing, write the nothing-on-record sentence in full, naming the places you searched and the date.
6. Write one sentence for each item using a verb that fits its state.

## Check your work

The exercise was done well if every sentence could be defended by pointing at one passage in one document, and if at least one item ended up in a lower state than you expected when you started.

Common mistakes:

- Treating a second announcement as proof of the first. A progress statement by the same office is still the institution's account, and may still be about plans.
- Reading a job posting or a web page for an office as proof that a program operates. It shows that a posting or a page exists.
- Writing "the university never did this" where the honest sentence is that you found no record.
- Assigning one state to the whole announcement. Different promises in one document often end up in different states.
- Upgrading because the claim is plausible. Plausibility is not a state.
