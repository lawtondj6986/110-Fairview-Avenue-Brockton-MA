When a news organization publishes a story built on a document, it has made two separate judgments.
The first is that the document is genuine: it is what it appears to be, created by the stated author
and not altered. The second is that what the document says is accurate, or at least that the article
describes its contents fairly. These are different questions, as explained in [authenticity versus
accuracy](/glossary/authenticity-vs-accuracy/). A genuine memo can contain false statements.
Practices vary between outlets, and no single procedure is universal. This primer describes the
steps that careful newsrooms commonly take and the signals in a published story that show how far
they went.

## Step one: how the document arrived

Verification starts with [provenance](/glossary/provenance/): where the document came from and how
it reached the reporter. A filing downloaded from a court's own docket needs little authentication.
A file sent by an anonymous source needs a great deal. Reporters ask who had access to the document,
how the source obtained it, and whether the source could have altered it. Newsrooms often keep the
original file and any envelope, email or message that delivered it.

## Step two: examining the document itself

Reporters and editors look at the document for internal consistency.

- Format. Letterhead, fonts, signature blocks, page numbering and document identifiers are compared
  with known genuine examples from the same source.
- Content. Names, titles, dates and references are checked against the public record. A memo dated
  before the author held the title it gives is a warning sign.
- File properties. For digital files, [metadata](/glossary/metadata/) such as creation dates and
  software may support or undercut the story the file tells. Metadata can be altered and is often
  stripped, so its absence proves little.
- Language. Phrasing, jargon and abbreviations are compared with other writing from the same author
  or office.

Some organizations consult outside document examiners or digital forensics specialists for
high-stakes material.

## Step three: independent confirmation

The strongest checks come from outside the document.

- [Corroboration](/glossary/corroboration/). A second, independent source confirms that the document
  exists or that its contents are accurate. Two sources who both saw the same leaked copy are not
  independent of each other.
- The purported author or recipient. Reporters often ask the person or office that supposedly
  created the document whether it is genuine. A refusal to deny can be informative; a denial must be
  weighed.
- Related records. A document referring to a meeting can be checked against calendars, travel
  records or other documents.

A story resting on one source with no confirmation is [single-source
reporting](/glossary/single-source-reporting/). Careful outlets either avoid it for serious claims
or say plainly that they rely on one source.

## Step four: asking for comment

Before publication, careful outlets contact the people and organizations the story describes and
invite them to respond, a practice known as the [right of reply](/glossary/right-of-reply/). They
usually give a reasonable time to respond and describe the claims in enough detail to allow a real
answer. The response, or the lack of one, appears in the story.

## Step five: editing and legal review

Editors challenge the reporting, ask what each sentence rests on, and look for overstatement. For
stories that could be defamatory, a lawyer may review the draft. Legal review tends to make wording
more cautious and more precise, which is why sourcing language is chosen so carefully.

## Reading the signals in a story

The words an outlet chooses tell you what it did.

- "Obtained by" or "reviewed by" the outlet. Reporters saw the document themselves.
- "Verified" or "authenticated," with a description of how. The outlet is vouching for authenticity
  and telling you its basis.
- "Could not independently verify." The outlet has the document or the claim but could not confirm
  it. That is a real limitation, stated openly.
- "According to a person familiar with" the matter. The source is anonymous and may not have seen
  the document. See [second-hand account](/glossary/second-hand-account/).
- "On the record." A named person stands behind the statement. See [on the
  record](/glossary/on-the-record/).
- "Declined to comment" and "did not respond." These record a response, or its absence, and say
  nothing about the truth of the claim. See [declined to comment](/glossary/declined-to-comment/).
- A [denial](/glossary/denial/). The subject says the claim is false. A denial is a claim too.
- A link to the document. Publishing the document, or a redacted version, lets readers check the
  outlet's reading for themselves.

Silence is also a signal. A story that quotes a document at length but never says how the outlet
came to have it, or whether anyone confirmed it, leaves the reader unable to judge the basis. When
several outlets report the same document, the one that explains its checks most fully is usually
the best one to cite. Later stories that say "as first reported by" another outlet add no new
verification unless they describe checks of their own.

## After publication

Stories change after publication, and responsible outlets say how.

- An update adds new information, such as a later response, and is usually labeled with a time. See
  [update log](/glossary/update-log/).
- A [correction](/glossary/correction/) fixes an error of fact and says what was wrong.
- A clarification adds context to something accurate but misleading.
- An editor's note explains a larger problem, such as a sourcing issue.
- A [retraction](/glossary/retraction/) withdraws a story or a central claim. It is rare and
  serious.

An outlet with a visible corrections policy and a record of using it is generally more trustworthy,
not less. A story that has quietly changed without a note is a warning sign. Comparing an [archive
snapshot](/glossary/archive-snapshot/) of an earlier version with the current page can show what
changed.

## A worked hypothetical

A regional news site receives, from an anonymous source, a scanned letter in which a manufacturing
company appears to ask a state university lab to delay publishing test results on one of its
products. The reporters compare the letterhead and signature with letters the company filed in a
public regulatory proceeding. They find a reference in the letter to a project review meeting and
confirm the date from the lab's published sponsored-research agreement, which a records request
produced. A former lab employee, who did not see the leaked copy, confirms that such a letter was
discussed. The reporters send the company and the university detailed requests for comment. The
company declines to comment on the letter's authenticity.

The published story says the site "reviewed" the letter and describes the checks. It says the
company declined to comment. A day later the company says the letter was a draft never sent. The
site adds a time-stamped update with that statement and changes a sentence that had said the letter
"was sent," with a correction note explaining the change.

## What the documents look like

- The document as received. Held by the newsroom. Sometimes published in full or in part.
- Reporting notes and communications with sources. Held by the newsroom. Not public, and often
  protected by newsroom policy and, in some places, by law.
- Comment requests and responses. Not usually public, though subjects sometimes publish their own
  responses.
- The published story, with its sourcing language. Public.
- Update and correction notes. Public, on the story or on a corrections page.
- Earlier versions. Not always kept by the outlet; often recoverable from web archives.
- Ethics and corrections policies. Many outlets publish these.

## Questions worth asking

- Did the outlet see the document, and does it say how it established authenticity?
- Is any key claim attributed to a single anonymous source?
- Were the people described asked for comment, and what did they say?
- Is the document published or linked, so the outlet's reading can be checked?
- Has the story been updated or corrected, and are the changes labeled?
- Do other outlets that obtained the same document describe it the same way?
