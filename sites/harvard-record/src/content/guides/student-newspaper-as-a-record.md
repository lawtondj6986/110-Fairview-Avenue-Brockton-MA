A student newspaper is often the most continuous written account of a university's daily life. It
covers meetings, minor controversies and administrative changes that no other outlet records. It is
also written by people who are learning the job. Used with care, it is a strong source for
chronology and a useful pointer to primary documents.

## What it is good for

- Dates and sequence. When a policy was announced, when an office opened, when a person took or left
  a role.
- Contemporaneous statements. What an administrator said at the time, before later accounts were
  shaped.
- Coverage of local bodies: faculty meetings, student government, town halls.
- Follow-up. Student reporters often return to a story a year later, when national media have moved
  on.
- Leads. Articles cite reports, emails and court filings that you can then obtain and read directly.
- Campus reaction, through news coverage of protests and petitions and through opinion pages.

## Its limits

- Inexperience and turnover. Staff change every year. Reporters may lack background on a
  long-running story, and institutional memory is short.
- Access. Student reporters depend on the administration they cover for interviews and are members
  of the community they report on.
- Independence varies. Some papers are financially and editorially independent of the university.
  Others are funded by it or advised by staff. The paper's "about" page usually says which.
- Anonymous sourcing. Policies on unnamed sources differ, and student sources are often unnamed for
  good reason.
- Online changes. Articles may be updated, corrected or, occasionally, removed or anonymized at a
  subject's request. The version you read may not be the one first published.
- Uneven coverage. Graduate and professional schools, research centers and affiliated hospitals
  usually receive less attention than undergraduate life.

## How to search the archive

1. Start with the paper's own website search, then use a general search engine restricted to the
   paper's site, which often works better.
2. Search for offices and titles as well as names. People change roles, and names are misspelled.
3. Try the terms used at the time. Offices are renamed and policies retitled.
4. Bracket your dates. Search the weeks around a known event, then a year later for follow-up.
5. Check for a digitized print archive for older material. University libraries and archives often
   hold these, sometimes as scanned pages with imperfect text search.
6. Look for a corrections page or correction notes on individual articles. See the guide to reading
   a correction or update note.
7. Save what you find with its web address and the date you retrieved it. A public web archive may
   hold earlier versions.

## How to weigh an article

Sort the article by type first.

- News report. Written to a standard of verification and attribution.
- Editorial. The view of the paper's editorial board.
- Opinion column or letter. The view of its author alone.
- Magazine or feature writing. Longer and more interpretive.

Then, for a news report, ask.

1. What is the article's own source for each key claim: a document, a named person, an unnamed
   person, or the reporter's observation of an event?
2. Did the reporter see the document or rely on someone's description of it?
3. Was the subject given a chance to respond, and is the response included?
4. Is the claim something the reporter was positioned to know directly, such as what was said at a
   public meeting?
5. Has any other outlet, or any primary document, confirmed it?
6. Does the article carry a correction?

A report that a dean said something at an open meeting, written by a reporter who was present, is
good evidence that the dean said it. It is not evidence that what the dean said was true.

## How to cite it

- Cite the paper for what it directly establishes, in words that match: "the student newspaper
  reported that," "according to a statement quoted in."
- Where the article rests on a document, find the document and cite that instead, crediting the
  paper for the lead if appropriate.
- Give the headline, publication date and web address, and the date you accessed it.
- Do not present an opinion piece as reporting.
- Do not convert an allegation described in an article into a fact. Keep the attribution attached.

## Pairing it with other records

The student newspaper is most useful alongside other sources.

- Use official announcements to confirm dates and titles.
- Use the newspaper to see how an announcement was received and what questions were asked.
- Use court dockets, agency records and published reports to check claims about legal or regulatory
  matters.
- Use the newspaper's follow-up coverage when checking whether commitments were kept. See the guide
  on following up on promises.

## What the archive can and cannot tell you

It can give you a dated, public, contemporaneous account of what was said and done in view of the
campus. Over many years it shows patterns: recurring issues, repeated promises, changes in tone.

It cannot give you what happened in private, and its silence on a matter shows only that it was not
covered. A story that was never written is not a finding that nothing occurred.

## Common mistakes

- Citing an article for more than it says.
- Quoting an opinion piece as if it were the paper's reporting.
- Missing a correction appended after publication.
- Treating a single unnamed source as confirmation.
- Dismissing student journalism wholesale. On campus matters it is often first, and sometimes the
  only record.
