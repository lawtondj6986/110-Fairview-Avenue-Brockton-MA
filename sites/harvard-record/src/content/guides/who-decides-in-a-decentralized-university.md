A large research university is less a single organization than a federation. Schools control their
own budgets, centers raise their own money, and faculty members run labs with wide autonomy. To
understand how a decision was made, or why a rule was not applied, you first need to know where the
decision actually sat.

## The usual layers

Structures and titles vary by institution. This is the general pattern.

- Governing board. Often called trustees, regents, a corporation or overseers. It holds legal
  responsibility for the institution, appoints the president, approves budgets and major policies,
  and oversees the endowment. Some institutions have two boards with different roles. Boards rarely
  make operational decisions.
- President and central administration. The president is the chief executive. The provost is usually
  the chief academic officer. Central offices include the general counsel, finance, development
  (fundraising), research administration, human resources, compliance and communications.
- Schools and their deans. Each school (arts and sciences, medicine, law, business and so on) is led
  by a dean. In a decentralized university, schools keep much of their own revenue, do their own
  fundraising, hire their own faculty, and may have their own versions of policies.
- Departments and chairs. Departments manage teaching, hiring recommendations and day-to-day
  academic life. Chairs often rotate and have limited authority over tenured colleagues.
- Centers, institutes and programs. These are research units, often built around a director and
  funded by grants and gifts. Some sit inside a department or school. Others report to the provost.
  Their oversight varies widely.
- Principal investigators. A faculty member who leads a lab controls its research direction, its
  hiring of students and postdoctoral researchers, and much of its spending, within the terms of
  each funding source.
- Faculty governance. A senate or faculty council sets or advises on academic policy, including
  standards for appointment, promotion and discipline of faculty.
- Affiliated entities. Teaching hospitals, research institutes, foundations and alumni associations
  may be legally separate organizations with their own boards.

## Who typically decides what

- Admitting students: admissions offices and faculty committees within each school.
- Hiring and tenure: department vote, then dean, then provost or president, sometimes the board.
- Accepting gifts: central development office and school development offices, under a gift
  acceptance policy. Small gifts to a lab may be accepted locally.
- Accepting research grants and contracts: the central sponsored research office signs on behalf of
  the institution.
- Granting titles such as visiting scholar or fellow: often the department, center or school, with
  varying central review.
- Access to buildings and offices: local administrators.
- Approving research with human participants: the institutional review board.
- Handling misconduct complaints: a central or school-level office, depending on the type of
  complaint and who is involved. Faculty discipline often involves a separate faculty process.
- Speaking for the institution: central communications, though schools often have their own.

The pattern to notice is that many decisions with reputational consequences are made low in the
structure, by people who are not thinking about the institution as a whole.

## Why decentralization matters for accountability

- A central policy may not reach local practice. A rule adopted by the president's office binds a
  lab only if the lab knows about it, and someone checks.
- Information stays local. A center director may know a visitor well while the central
  administration has never heard the name. Central records can be accurate and still incomplete.
- "The university" is ambiguous. A statement that the university did or did not do something may be
  true of the central administration and silent about a school, a center or an affiliate. See [the
  guide on reading a crisis statement](/guides/read-a-crisis-statement/).
- Money is not all one pot. Each school and many centers manage their own funds. A gift declined by
  one part can be offered to another.
- Responsibility diffuses. When several offices each hold part of a decision, each can reasonably
  say the decision was not theirs.
- Affiliates are separate. A hospital or institute that shares a name and faculty with a university
  may have different policies, different records and different legal duties.

Decentralization also has well-known benefits. It protects academic freedom, lets fields govern
themselves, and keeps decisions close to expertise. The accountability gaps are a cost of that
design, not evidence of bad intent.

## How to find where a decision sat

1. Identify the unit: which school, department, center or lab.
2. Find its place in the structure. Organizational charts, "about" pages and annual reports show
   reporting lines.
3. Find the governing policy and check its scope clause, which states whom it covers.
4. Identify the approval the decision required and who held that authority at the time. Titles and
   office-holders change, so match the date.
5. Check for a separate legal entity. An affiliate's own tax filings, board list and policies are
   the place to look.
6. Ask the institution, if the documents do not settle it. "Which office approves this kind of
   appointment?" is a process question and is usually answered.

## What an organizational chart can and cannot tell you

A chart shows formal reporting lines and where authority is supposed to lie. It lets you address a
question to the right office.

It does not show informal influence, such as a senior faculty member's standing or a major donor's
access to leadership. It does not show whether oversight was exercised. It reflects the present
structure, which may differ from the one in place when the events occurred.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the university as a single actor with a single mind.
- Assuming the president knew of, or approved, a decision made within a lab.
- Assuming the opposite: that senior leaders could not have known.
- Applying one school's policy to another.
- Confusing an affiliated hospital or institute with the university itself.
