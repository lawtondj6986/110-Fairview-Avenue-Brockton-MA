Anyone who publishes research will publish errors. What separates trustworthy work from the rest is
what happens next. A correction made openly, quickly and without defensiveness strengthens the work,
because it shows readers that the stated standards are applied to the author too. A silent edit does
the opposite. Once a reader notices that text has changed without notice, nothing else on the page
can be relied on.

## Types of change

Not every edit is a correction. Sort changes into kinds, and handle each consistently.

- Correction. A published statement was wrong: a date, a figure, a name, a role, an attribution, or
  a claim placed at a higher evidence state or source grade than the record supports.
- Clarification. The statement was accurate but could reasonably be misread, for example because it
  implied a connection it did not intend.
- Update. The statement was accurate when published, and the record has since changed: a new
  document was released, a case was decided, a response was received.
- Retraction. A claim is withdrawn entirely because its basis has failed.
- Minor edit. Typos, formatting and broken links that do not affect meaning.

Corrections, clarifications, updates and retractions should all be recorded publicly. Minor edits
usually need no notice, but set that rule in advance and do not use it to hide substantive changes.
If in doubt, record it.

## When you learn of a possible error

1. Take it seriously whatever the source. Errors are reported by subjects, readers, critics and your
   own rechecks. The identity or tone of the person reporting does not change whether the statement
   is right.
2. Go back to the source. Open the original document and compare it with what you published.
3. Decide on the evidence. If you were wrong, correct. If you were right, keep the text, and
   consider whether a clarification would prevent the misreading. If the sources conflict, say so in
   the text.
4. Act promptly. An error about a named person is urgent, because the harm continues while it
   stands.
5. Reply to the person who raised it, saying what you did and why.

## Trace the error everywhere

An error rarely lives in one place. Use your claim ledger to find every use of the claim: other
pages, summaries, tables, timelines, captions, descriptions and any downloadable files. Fix all of
them in one pass.

Then ask how the error happened. Common causes are relying on a summary without opening the
original, misreading a date, confusing two entities with similar names, and carrying a claim forward
after its source was revised. If the cause could have affected other claims, recheck those as well.
Record the cause in your notes. It is the most useful part of the exercise.

## Writing the notice

A good correction notice is short and factual. It should contain:

- the date of the correction;
- where the error appeared;
- what the text said;
- what it says now;
- the source for the corrected statement, where that helps.

For example: "Correction, [date]: An earlier version of this page gave the year of the report as
[year]. The report was published in [year]. The text has been corrected."

Guidelines:

- Say plainly that it was an error. Avoid "the text has been updated to reflect" when the text was
  simply wrong.
- Do not explain at length or justify. A notice is not a defense.
- Do not blame the person affected or the person who reported it.
- Be careful about restating a harmful error. If an incorrect claim about a person was damaging,
  describe the correction in a way that does not repeat the claim more prominently than the original
  did. "An earlier version misstated [person]'s role" may be better than quoting the mistake in
  full.
- An apology is appropriate where an error caused harm to someone. Keep it direct.

## Where the notice goes

Place the notice on the page where the error appeared, close to the corrected passage or at the foot
of the page, and change the page's "last reviewed" or "last updated" date. Also record it in a
central update log, so that a reader can see every change in one place. This site records each
change in its update log and describes the process at `/methodology/`.

If the erroneous claim was spread beyond the page, for example in a summary sent to others, send the
correction to the same audience by the same route.

Do not delete a page to make an error disappear. If a page must be withdrawn, leave a notice at its
address saying that it was withdrawn, when and why.

## Keep the history

Preserve earlier versions privately, with dates, so you can show exactly what was published and
when. In the claim ledger, do not overwrite the old row. Mark it corrected or withdrawn, add the
date and reason, and link it to the new row. The log of your own corrections is a record like any
other and deserves the same care.

## Updates are not failures

When a new document moves a claim, say from stated intent to documented activity, record the change
as an update with its source. Treat downgrades the same way. If a claim you labeled verified turns
out to rest on less than you thought, lower it and say so. Readers should be able to see the claim's
history as well as its current state.

## Make it easy to report errors

Publish a clear way to contact you about errors. State what you need (the page, the passage and the
source that shows the error) and what the person can expect in return. People named in your work
have the strongest interest in its accuracy. An accessible corrections process, together with a
right of reply before publication, is how their knowledge gets in.
