Every college or university in the United States that takes part in federal student aid programs
must publish an annual security report under a federal law known as the Clery Act. The report is due
by October 1 each year. It is the one safety document that exists in roughly the same form at nearly
every institution, which makes it a natural starting point, provided you know what the numbers
count.

## Find the right document

1. Search the institution's website for "annual security report." It is usually posted by the campus
   police, public safety or compliance office.
2. Check for separate reports. An institution with more than one campus generally publishes
   statistics for each one, sometimes in separate documents.
3. If the institution has on-campus student housing, the report is usually combined with an annual
   fire safety report.
4. The U.S. Department of Education also publishes the statistics for all institutions in an online
   campus safety and security data tool, which is convenient for viewing several years at once.

## What the report contains

The report has two parts, and the second is often skipped.

- Crime statistics for the three most recent calendar years.
- Policy statements. These describe how to report crimes, how the institution issues warnings,
  security of facilities, the authority of campus police, alcohol and drug policies, prevention
  programs, and the procedures the institution follows when dating violence, domestic violence,
  sexual assault or stalking is reported.

## How to read the tables

The statistics are sorted by offense, by location and by year.

### Offense categories

- Criminal offenses, including murder and manslaughter, sex offenses (rape, fondling, incest and
  statutory rape), robbery, aggravated assault, burglary, motor vehicle theft and arson.
- Offenses added by the Violence Against Women Act amendments: domestic violence, dating violence
  and stalking.
- Hate crimes, meaning listed offenses motivated by bias, reported by category of bias.
- Arrests and referrals for disciplinary action for weapons, drug and liquor law violations.

Definitions come from federal rules, not from state criminal law or the institution's own conduct
code. A given act might be named differently under each.

### Location categories

- On campus.
- On-campus student housing, which is a subset of the on-campus figure, not an addition to it.
- Noncampus property: certain buildings or land off the main campus that the institution or a
  recognized student organization owns or controls.
- Public property: streets, sidewalks and similar public areas within or immediately next to the
  campus.

Together these are called Clery geography. An incident outside them is not counted, whoever was
involved.

### What a count means

Each number is a count of incidents reported to campus police, local police, or designated officials
known as campus security authorities, a group that includes many staff with responsibility for
student and campus activities. Three points follow.

- A report is counted whether or not anyone was charged, disciplined or convicted. It is not a count
  of findings.
- An incident is counted in the calendar year it was reported, which may be later than the year it
  occurred.
- Reports made only to confidential counselors are generally not included.

Look also for a line or note on unfounded crimes. These are reports that sworn law enforcement
determined, after investigation, to be false or baseless. They are removed from the main counts and
tallied separately.

## A reading routine

1. Note the campus, the enrollment and how many students live in university housing.
2. Read across the three years for each offense. Look for sharp changes and for any footnote
   explaining them.
3. Compare the on-campus housing figure with the on-campus total to see where incidents concentrate.
4. Read the footnotes and any caveats about data from local police, which institutions must request
   but cannot always obtain.
5. Read the policy statements on reporting and on sexual assault procedures. Check that they name
   offices, describe options, and state the standard of evidence used in disciplinary proceedings.
6. Look at the daily crime log if you want recent, incident-level entries. Institutions with a
   police or security department must keep one and make it available for public inspection.

## What the report can tell you

- How many incidents in each federal category were reported within the defined locations.
- Whether reports are rising or falling over three years, and where they concentrate.
- What the institution has formally committed to do when a crime is reported.
- Whether the institution takes the document seriously. Clear, specific policy statements are a sign
  of care.

## What it cannot tell you

- How much crime actually occurs. Many incidents, particularly sexual offenses, are never reported.
- Anything about most private off-campus housing, study abroad sites not controlled by the
  institution, or incidents elsewhere in the surrounding city.
- Sexual harassment that does not meet a listed crime definition.
- Outcomes. The tables do not show what happened after a report.
- Who was involved. Counts include incidents with no student victim or perpetrator, if they occurred
  within the geography.

## Common mistakes

- Adding the housing column to the on-campus column, which double counts.
- Reading zero as proof of safety. At a large residential campus, several years of zero reported sex
  offenses more plausibly indicates that reports are not reaching officials.
- Reading an increase as a rise in crime. It may reflect better reporting. See [the guide on
  comparing two universities' data fairly](/guides/compare-sexual-misconduct-data-fairly/).
- Comparing raw counts between campuses of different size, setting and housing pattern.
- Treating the report as the institution's full record on sexual misconduct. Complaint offices
  handle a wider range of conduct and places.
