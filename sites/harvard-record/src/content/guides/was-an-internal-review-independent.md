When something goes wrong, institutions often commission a review of their own conduct. The word
"independent" is used freely in announcements and has no fixed meaning. Independence is a matter of
degree, and it can be assessed from details that are usually disclosed in the report itself or in
the announcement that launched it.

## Who conducted it

Reviews fall roughly into four types.

1. Internal staff, such as the general counsel's office or internal audit.
2. A committee of faculty or trustees.
3. An outside law firm or consultant hired by the institution.
4. An external body the institution does not control, such as a regulator, a court-appointed monitor
   or a legislative committee.

Only the fourth is independent in the full sense. The third is the most common type to be labeled
independent, and it deserves the closest reading.

## Questions to ask about an outside reviewer

- Who selected and hired the reviewer: the administration whose conduct is under review, or a
  committee of the governing board with no involvement in the events?
- To whom did the reviewer report?
- Has the reviewer done other work for the institution, before or since? A firm that regularly
  defends the institution has an ongoing commercial relationship with it.
- Was the reviewer retained as legal counsel? If so, the institution is the client. The work may be
  covered by attorney-client privilege, which lets the institution decide what to release, and
  counsel owes duties to the client. A fact-finder engaged on non-privileged terms is in a different
  position.
- Who paid? The institution nearly always pays. That alone does not defeat independence, but it is
  why the other safeguards matter.

## Scope

The scope, sometimes called the mandate or terms of reference, determines what a review can find.

- Was the scope written down and published?
- Who set it, and could the reviewer expand it if the evidence led elsewhere?
- What period, which units and which people did it cover?
- Was the reviewer asked to find facts only, or also to assess responsibility and recommend changes?

A narrow scope produces a narrow report. A review limited to whether written policy was violated
will say little if no policy existed at the time. Read the scope before the findings, and note what
lay outside it.

## Access

- Did the reviewer have access to all relevant documents, including email, financial records and
  files held by individual units?
- Who searched for the documents: the reviewer, or staff of the institution?
- How many people were interviewed? Were former employees and people outside the institution
  included?
- Could anyone decline to cooperate? Unlike a court or a regulator, a private reviewer cannot compel
  testimony. A good report says who declined.
- Could people speak in confidence, and was there a way for people to come forward on their own
  initiative?

A report that describes its method in detail, including its limits, is more credible than one that
states conclusions without saying how they were reached.

## Control of the report

- Was the full report published, or only a summary or a letter from leadership describing it?
- Did the institution review a draft? Review for factual accuracy is normal. A right to edit
  findings is not.
- Were the people named or described given a chance to respond? That is fair practice, not a
  weakness.
- Was the release timed or packaged in a way that limited scrutiny?

A summary written by the institution about a report it has not released is the institution's
account, not the reviewer's.

## What followed

- Did the report make recommendations, and did the institution accept them publicly?
- Was someone assigned to carry them out, with a timeline?
- Was there any later public accounting? See [the guide on following
  up on promises](/guides/follow-up-on-promises-after-a-scandal/).

## A short scoring list

Count how many of these are true.

1. The reviewer was chosen by people not involved in the events.
2. The reviewer had no significant prior relationship with the institution.
3. The scope was published and could be widened.
4. The reviewer controlled the document search.
5. The report states who was interviewed, in number or by category, and who declined.
6. The full report was published.
7. The institution could not alter findings.
8. Recommendations were tracked publicly afterward.

Few reviews meet all eight. A review that meets two or three is best read as the institution's
considered statement about itself.

## What an internal review can and cannot tell you

A well-run review can establish a detailed factual record that would otherwise never be public:
dates, amounts, who knew what, and which rules applied. Institutions hold records no outsider can
obtain, and a review is often the only route by which those records reach the public.

A review cannot establish what its scope excluded, what its sources did not disclose, or what
documents no longer exist. Its conclusions are judgments by the reviewer, not legal findings.
Statements such as "we found no evidence that" describe the limits of what was examined. They are
not the same as a finding that something did not happen.

## Common mistakes

- Accepting the label "independent" from the announcement.
- Dismissing a review entirely because the institution paid for it. Facts set out with sources can
  be checked on their own merits.
- Reading the executive summary only. Qualifications tend to sit in the body and the footnotes.
- Overlooking the scope and then faulting, or crediting, the report for matters it never examined.
