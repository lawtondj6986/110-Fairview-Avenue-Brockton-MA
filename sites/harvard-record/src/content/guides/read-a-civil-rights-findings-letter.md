Federal agencies enforce civil-rights laws that apply to schools receiving federal funds. In
education, the main office is the U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights, which
enforces laws including Title IX (sex discrimination), Title VI (race, color and national origin)
and Section 504 (disability). The Department of Justice also has a role. When an investigation ends,
two documents often appear together: a letter and a resolution agreement. They do different jobs.

## The two documents

- The letter. Written by the agency and addressed to the institution's president. It describes the
  complaint or review, the law, what the agency examined, and its conclusions or concerns. It may be
  called a letter of findings or a resolution letter.
- The resolution agreement. Signed by the institution. It lists the steps the institution commits to
  take and the reporting it owes the agency.

The letter is the agency's account. The agreement is a set of promises. Read both.

## First question: was there a finding?

Cases close in different ways, and the difference matters.

- Resolution before the investigation is complete. The institution may offer to resolve the matter
  voluntarily while the investigation is still open. The letter will then describe concerns the
  agency had identified so far, and will not state a finding of violation.
- Finding of noncompliance. The agency completed its investigation and concluded that the
  institution violated the law in specified ways.
- Insufficient evidence. The agency concluded that the evidence did not establish a violation, on
  some or all issues.
- Administrative closure or dismissal, for reasons such as timeliness or jurisdiction.

Find the sentence that states the outcome. Phrases such as "identified concerns" and "found
insufficient evidence" are precise terms. Many letters contain different outcomes for different
allegations.

## Reading the letter in order

1. Opening. Case number, date, the law at issue and how the case began: an individual complaint or a
   compliance review initiated by the agency.
2. Issues investigated. The specific questions the agency set out to answer. Everything else is
   outside the document.
3. Legal standards. The agency's statement of what the law requires. Regulations and agency guidance
   in this area have changed several times, so the standards described are those the agency applied
   to the period reviewed. They may differ from current rules.
4. Method. Documents reviewed, people interviewed, site visits, and the years covered.
5. Facts. Often the longest section. It may describe policies, training and a sample of case files.
   Individuals are not named. Complainants and students appear as numbers or letters.
6. Analysis and conclusions, issue by issue.
7. Closing notes. Standard language usually says that the letter addresses only the issues
   investigated, is not a formal statement of agency policy, and that the complainant may have a
   right to sue in court regardless.

## Reading the resolution agreement

1. Look for a statement that the agreement is not an admission of liability or wrongdoing. Most
   contain one.
2. List each action item: policy revisions, training, a climate survey, review of past complaints,
   remedies for specific individuals, record keeping.
3. Note each deadline and each reporting obligation. The agency typically requires documents showing
   compliance.
4. Note whether the agency must approve revised policies before adoption.
5. Find the monitoring clause. The agency keeps the case open until it decides the terms are met,
   and may reopen enforcement if they are not.

The action items tell you what the agency considered deficient, even where the letter is cautious.

## What happens afterward

An agreement begins a monitoring period that can last years. The agency issues a closure letter when
it is satisfied. Monitoring correspondence is not always published, though it may be requested under
the federal Freedom of Information Act. To learn whether an old agreement was completed, look for a
closure letter, ask the institution, or check the agency's current list of open cases.

## What these documents can tell you

- The agency's detailed, documented account of how an institution handled a category of matters
  during a defined period.
- Specific deficiencies in policy or practice, as the agency saw them.
- What the institution formally committed to change, and by when.
- How the institution's files looked to an outside reviewer with authority to demand them.

## What they cannot tell you

- Whether a court would agree. An agency determination is an administrative conclusion. It is not a
  court judgment.
- Who was right in any individual case. The agency reviews the institution's response, not the
  underlying incident.
- Anything outside the stated issues and period.
- The institution's present practice. Policies may have been rewritten more than once since.
- That an institution with no letter has no problems. Investigations depend heavily on whether
  someone filed a complaint.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a voluntary resolution a finding of violation.
- Calling an open investigation a finding of anything. An investigation means the agency has decided
  a complaint warrants review.
- Reading "insufficient evidence" as a declaration that nothing happened. It means the standard for
  a violation was not met on the evidence gathered.
- Treating the agreement's no-admission clause as proof of innocence, or its existence as proof of
  guilt. It is neither.
- Applying the legal standards quoted in an older letter to the present day.
- Citing the press release instead of the letter. Press summaries from either side compress and
  emphasize.
