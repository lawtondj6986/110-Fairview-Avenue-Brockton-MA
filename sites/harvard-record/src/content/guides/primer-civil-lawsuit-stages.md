A civil lawsuit is a dispute between private parties, or between a party and a government, decided
under rules of civil procedure. It is not a prosecution. Nobody goes to prison as a result, though a
court may order money paid or conduct stopped. Many filings in a civil case are public and are
quoted in the press. Most of them establish much less than their tone suggests. This primer follows
a case through a U.S. court, using the federal courts as the model. State courts follow a broadly
similar sequence, but their names for filings, deadlines and access rules differ.

## Before the case

A person who believes they were wronged must sue within the [statute of
limitations](/glossary/statute-of-limitations/), a deadline set by law that varies by claim and by
state, and that legislatures sometimes change. They must also have [standing](/glossary/standing/),
meaning a concrete injury the court can address. Many disputes are settled before any suit is filed,
often after a demand letter. Those settlements usually leave no court record.

## The complaint

The case begins when the plaintiff files a [complaint](/glossary/complaint-civil/) with the court.
The complaint names the [plaintiff and defendant](/glossary/plaintiff-and-defendant/), states the
facts the plaintiff says are true, lists the legal claims, and asks for relief. The court assigns a
case number and opens a [docket](/glossary/docket/), the running list of filings.

What it establishes: that the plaintiff, through a lawyer, made these
[allegations](/glossary/allegation/) on this date. Lawyers must have a reasonable basis for what
they file, but no court has tested the facts. A complaint can be detailed, specific and emotionally
powerful, and still be wrong. The [fair report
privilege](/law/defamation-and-fair-report-privilege/) lets the press report what a complaint says,
which is why allegations often appear in news coverage word for word.

## Service and response

The plaintiff must formally deliver the complaint and a summons to each defendant, called service.
The defendant then responds in one of two main ways.

- An [answer](/glossary/answer/). The defendant responds to each paragraph: admitting, denying, or
  saying it lacks enough information to respond. The answer may add defenses. An admission in an
  answer is significant. A denial establishes only that the point is disputed.
- A [motion to dismiss](/glossary/motion-to-dismiss/). The defendant argues that even if every
  factual allegation were true, the complaint does not state a valid legal claim, or that the court
  lacks jurisdiction, or that the claim is too late.

When a judge rules on a motion to dismiss, the judge usually must assume that the complaint's
factual allegations are true. A denial of the motion means only that the claim may go forward. It is
not a finding that anything alleged happened. A dismissal may be with or without leave to file an
[amended complaint](/glossary/amended-complaint/), and a [dismissal with
prejudice](/glossary/dismissal-with-prejudice/) ends the claim for good.

## Discovery

If the case continues, the parties exchange information in [discovery](/glossary/discovery/). Each
side can ask the other for documents, written answers to questions, and admissions. Lawyers question
witnesses under oath in a [deposition](/glossary/deposition/), recorded by a court reporter. A
[subpoena](/glossary/subpoena/) can require people who are not parties to produce documents or
testify.

Most discovery material is never filed with the court and is not public. It becomes public only if a
party attaches it to a filing, as an [exhibit](/glossary/exhibit/), or uses it at trial. Courts
often enter a [protective order](/glossary/protective-order/) limiting the use of confidential
material, and parties may ask to file some documents under seal. A [sealed
record](/glossary/sealed-record/) can later be unsealed by the court.

What it establishes: that a document existed in someone's files or that a witness said something
under oath. A deposition transcript is sworn testimony, but it is one person's account, given under
questioning by an opposing lawyer, and it is not a finding.

## Summary judgment

After discovery, either side may ask for [summary judgment](/glossary/summary-judgment/). The
question is whether there is any genuine dispute about a material fact that a trial would need to
resolve. If not, the judge decides the case on the law. Supporting papers often include an
[affidavit](/glossary/affidavit/) or declaration and selected exhibits.

What it establishes: a ruling granting summary judgment decides the claim. A ruling denying it means
a reasonable factfinder could go either way; it is not a finding for the party who survived the
motion. Judges' opinions at this stage often summarize the evidence in detail, but they describe it
in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion.

## Trial, judgment and appeal

Few civil cases reach trial. When one does, a jury or judge hears evidence and decides the facts
under a [burden of proof](/glossary/burden-of-proof/), usually [preponderance of the
evidence](/glossary/preponderance-of-the-evidence/). The verdict leads to a judgment. A judgment is
the first point at which a court has found facts after hearing both sides. Either side may appeal,
and an appeals court reviews mainly for legal error. A judgment may be reversed, or later set aside
by [vacatur](/glossary/vacatur/).

## How most cases actually end

Most civil cases end without a trial. They end in a [settlement](/glossary/settlement/), a contract
in which the parties resolve the dispute, often with a payment, a confidentiality clause and a
statement that the defendant admits no wrongdoing ([without admission of
liability](/glossary/without-admission-of-liability/)). The plaintiff then files a [voluntary
dismissal](/glossary/voluntary-dismissal/). Sometimes the court enters the agreement as a [consent
judgment](/glossary/consent-judgment/) or consent decree. A settlement establishes that the parties
chose to end the case on agreed terms. It is not an admission or a vindication. In a [class
action](/glossary/class-action/), the court must approve the settlement, and the approval papers are
public.

## A worked hypothetical

A former employee sues a research institute, alleging she was fired for reporting safety problems.
Her complaint describes meetings and emails in detail. A news article reports the allegations. The
institute moves to dismiss. The judge dismisses one claim as filed too late and lets the retaliation
claim go forward, noting that the court must accept the allegations as true at this stage. The
parties take depositions. The institute moves for summary judgment, attaching emails that it says
show the firing was planned before her report. The judge denies the motion, finding a genuine
dispute about timing. Two weeks before trial the case settles on confidential terms, and a voluntary
dismissal appears on the docket.

At no point did a court find that the institute retaliated against her, or that it did not.

## What the documents look like

- Complaint, answer and amended complaints. Public, on the docket, unless sealed.
- Motions, briefs and the judge's orders and opinions. Public, unless sealed. Orders show what the
  court actually decided.
- Exhibits and affidavits attached to motions. Public when filed, often with redactions.
- Discovery requests, responses, document productions and most deposition transcripts. Not filed,
  not public.
- Protective orders and sealing orders. Usually public, even when the material they protect is not.
- Trial transcripts and verdict forms. Public, though transcripts may cost money to obtain.
- Settlement agreement. Usually private. The dismissal on the docket is public.

Federal dockets and filings are available through the courts' electronic records system, PACER, for
a fee. State court access varies widely. See [how to find court
records](/guides/method-finding-court-records/) and [how to read a court
docket](/guides/read-a-court-docket/).

## Questions worth asking

- Is this statement from a complaint, an answer, a sworn statement, or a judge's finding?
- What standard did the court apply when it ruled, and did it assume the allegations were true?
- Was the claim dismissed, and if so, with or without prejudice?
- Which documents in the case are sealed, and has anyone asked to unseal them?
- How did the case end: judgment, settlement or dismissal, and what does the docket show?
- Was there an appeal, and did it change the result?
