A fact-finding report is the written result of a review an institution commissions into its own
conduct, usually prepared by its lawyers, an outside law firm or a committee. It is often the most
detailed public account of a matter, drawn from records no outsider can see. It is also a document
produced for a client. Both things are true, and a careful reading keeps both in view.

## Before the findings: four things to locate

1. The author and the client. Who wrote the report, who commissioned it, and to whom it was
   delivered.
2. The scope. The questions the reviewers were asked to answer, the period and the units covered.
   This is sometimes in the first pages and sometimes in an appendix or the cover letter.
3. The method. How many documents were collected and from where, how many people were interviewed,
   who declined, and what could not be obtained.
4. The standard. What the conduct was measured against: written policy in force at the time, law, or
   a general sense of good practice.

[The guide on judging whether a review was independent](/guides/was-an-internal-review-independent/)
covers how to assess the first three. Here the question is how to read the text.

## Read in this order

1. Scope and method.
2. The full findings, including footnotes.
3. The recommendations.
4. The executive summary, last.
5. The cover letter or leadership message that accompanied the release, after everything else.

The executive summary and the cover message are the most carefully worded parts and the most widely
quoted. Reading them last lets you check them against the body.

## Separate three kinds of sentence

- Findings of fact. "The center received a gift of a stated amount on a stated date." These are the
  durable content. Good reports tie each to a document or interview.
- Characterizations. "Limited," "modest," "a small number," "no significant." These are the authors'
  judgments about the facts. Wherever one appears, look for the underlying number or event and form
  your own view.
- Conclusions about knowledge or intent. "Senior leaders were not aware." These rest on what people
  told the reviewers and on what documents survive. They are the least certain kind of statement.

Mark each as you read. A report can be reliable on facts and still generous in its
characterizations.

## Watch the wording of negative findings

Negative statements come in several strengths.

- "We found no evidence that X occurred." The reviewers looked, within their scope and sources, and
  did not find it.
- "We did not find that X occurred." Similar, sometimes used where evidence was mixed.
- "X did not occur." A positive assertion, which is rare.
- "We were not asked to examine X." Outside scope.

Only the third says that something did not happen. The first is the most common, and its weight
depends on how thorough the search was.

## Follow the sourcing

- Check whether statements are attributed: to a document, to "several witnesses," to one person's
  recollection.
- Notice where the report relies on the account of a person whose own conduct is at issue.
- Notice where documents and recollections conflict and how the report resolves the conflict.
- Check whether exhibits or source documents were published with the report. If they were, compare
  the report's description with the document itself.

## Notice how people are described

Reports often avoid names, using titles or descriptions. This protects privacy and limits legal
exposure. It also makes responsibility harder to trace. Track each described person consistently as
you read, and note whether senior people are described more vaguely than junior ones.

Passive constructions deserve the same attention. "The gift was approved" leaves out who approved
it. Look for whether the report says so elsewhere.

## Map what is missing

Make a short list as you go.

- Periods before or after the scope.
- Units or affiliates not covered.
- People who were not interviewed, including those outside the institution.
- Records that were unavailable, deleted or never kept.
- Questions the report raises and does not answer.

A good report states its own limits. Where it does not, your list is the caveat to attach when you
cite it.

## Read the recommendations as findings

Recommendations reveal what the reviewers thought was deficient. A recommendation to create central
records of gifts implies that central records were lacking. Compare the recommendations with the
findings: if a recommendation addresses a problem the findings barely mention, the findings may have
been understated.

## What a fact-finding report can tell you

- Specific facts drawn from internal records: dates, amounts, approvals, communications.
- Which policies existed at the time and whether they were followed.
- The institution's formal account, which it can be held to.
- What the institution has been advised to change.

## What it cannot tell you

- Anything outside its scope or beyond its sources.
- Legal liability. A report is not a judgment, and its authors are not a court.
- What people privately knew or intended, beyond what they said and wrote.
- That the account is complete. Later disclosures can add to or contradict a report.

## How to cite it

- Cite facts to the page, and say "the report found" or "the report states."
- Keep the report's qualifiers when you paraphrase.
- Distinguish the report's facts from its characterizations in your own writing.
- State who commissioned and wrote it the first time you cite it.

## Common mistakes

- Reading only the summary.
- Repeating a characterization as if it were a fact.
- Treating "no evidence" as a finding of absence.
- Rejecting documented facts because the author was paid by the institution. Facts with sources can
  be checked.
- Assuming the report is the last word.
