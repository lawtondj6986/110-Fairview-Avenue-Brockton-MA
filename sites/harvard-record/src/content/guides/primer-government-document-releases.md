Large sets of government-held records reach the public in a handful of ways. A court orders filings
unsealed. An agency answers a records request with thousands of pages. A legislative committee
publishes what it gathered in an inquiry. A statute or order requires an archive to release a
collection. Each route has its own rules about what is included, what is withheld and how the
material is prepared. Knowing how a release was made is the first step in reading it correctly. This
primer explains the process and then the habits that keep work with a release honest.

## The main routes

- Records requests. Under the federal [Freedom of Information Act](/law/freedom-of-information-act/)
  and state laws such as the [Massachusetts public records
  law](/law/massachusetts-public-records-law/), anyone may ask an agency for records. The agency
  searches, reviews and releases what the law requires, withholding material under stated
  exemptions. Large requests are often answered in rolling productions over months.
- Court unsealing. Material filed under seal in a lawsuit or criminal case may be unsealed by a
  judge, often after a motion by a news organization or another party. The judge may order names
  redacted or release documents in stages.
- Legislative releases. Committees receive documents through requests and subpoenas and may publish
  some of them with a report or hearing. Committees are generally not bound by FOIA, and they decide
  what to publish.
- Mandated releases. A statute, executive order or court order may require a collection to be
  reviewed and released, sometimes on a schedule. Archival and declassification releases often work
  this way.

## How a release is prepared

The steps are broadly the same whatever the route.

1. Collection. Someone gathers the records: files from an agency's systems, a party's [document
   production](/glossary/document-production/) in litigation, or exhibits filed in court. What was
   gathered depends on the search terms, custodians and dates chosen. Material outside the search is
   absent, not nonexistent.
2. Processing. Files are converted to a common format, usually PDF or page images. Emails lose some
   of their [metadata](/glossary/metadata/) in conversion. Attachments may be separated from the
   messages that carried them.
3. Review. Reviewers decide what to release and what to withhold. In a FOIA release, withholdings
   are justified by citing exemptions, such as those for personal privacy or for internal
   deliberations. In litigation, parties withhold privileged material and list it in a [privilege
   log](/glossary/privilege-log/).
4. [Redaction](/glossary/redaction/). Withheld passages are blacked out or replaced with codes
   naming the exemption. Redaction is done by people and software, and both make mistakes:
   inconsistent redaction of the same name across copies, or redactions that can be lifted from a
   poorly prepared file.
5. Numbering. Pages are often stamped with sequential identifiers, known as [Bates
   numbers](/glossary/bates-number/), which let anyone cite a precise page.
6. Text recognition. Scanned pages are run through optical character recognition (OCR) so they can
   be searched. OCR misreads names, numbers and handwriting.
7. Publication. The material is posted, often with a cover letter, an index or a description of what
   was searched and withheld. It may be posted in parts, revised, or replaced with a better-redacted
   version.

A release is therefore a selection, shaped by the search, the review and the law that governs it. It
is a [public record](/glossary/public-record/) of what was released, not a full record of what
happened.

## Common traps

- Volume. A release of many thousands of pages often contains the same email many times, once in
  each person's mailbox and again in every reply. See [duplicates and
  threads](/glossary/duplicate-and-thread/).
- Search hits. A name appearing on a page is a [search hit, not a
  document](/glossary/search-hit-vs-document/) about that person. Names appear in contact lists,
  newsletters, forwarded articles and OCR errors.
- Unverified content. Investigative files contain tips, rumors and allegations that the agency
  recorded but never confirmed. A record of a claim is not confirmation of it.
- Missing context. A message without the reply, or a draft without the final version, can mislead.
- Copies. Once a release is public, others repost it, sometimes edited or incomplete. A [mirror
  site](/glossary/mirror-site/) is a lead to the original, not a substitute. Official links also
  break; see [link rot](/glossary/link-rot/).
- Private people. Releases often include names, addresses and personal details of people who are not
  the subject of any inquiry, including victims and bystanders. A redaction failure does not make a
  detail fit to republish.

## Working responsibly

These habits apply whatever the subject.

- Get the official copy. Download from the releasing body's own site or docket, and save the cover
  letter or order that came with it.
- Record provenance. Note the source address, the date you downloaded it, and the release or batch
  it came from. Make an [archive snapshot](/glossary/archive-snapshot/) of the release page. A file
  checksum lets you show later that your copy is unchanged.
- Read the description of the release before searching. Learn what was searched, what was withheld
  and why.
- Cite by page identifier. Use Bates numbers or the releasing body's page numbers, not a search
  result.
- Check OCR against the image for any word you rely on.
- Deduplicate before counting. Count distinct documents, not pages or hits, and say how you counted.
- Separate what a document records from what it shows. A memo that repeats an allegation records the
  allegation.
- Protect people who are incidental to the record. Do not repeat personal details of private
  individuals unless they are essential, and never reconstruct redacted material about them.
- Watch for later versions. Releasing bodies sometimes reissue material with fewer or more
  redactions.

## A worked hypothetical

A state agency releases several thousand pages of correspondence about a contract dispute in
response to a records request, in three batches over four months. A researcher downloads each batch
from the agency's site and saves the cover letters, which list the search terms and the exemptions
applied. The researcher runs a search for a contractor's name and gets hundreds of hits. After
removing duplicate copies and quoted replies, they find a few dozen distinct messages, most of them
routine scheduling. One message appears with a paragraph redacted in the first batch and unredacted
in the third, after the agency reconsidered an exemption. The researcher cites both versions by page
identifier and notes the change.

A blog later posts a "complete" compilation. It turns out to omit the third batch. The researcher's
notes on provenance make the gap easy to show.

## What the documents look like

- The request or motion that started the release. FOIA requests are often obtainable; motions to
  unseal are on the court docket.
- Cover letters and determination letters. Sent to the requester; often posted with the release.
  They state what was searched and withheld.
- Orders. Unsealing orders and scheduling orders are public court records.
- The released documents. Public, usually as PDFs with page identifiers.
- Exemption codes and privilege logs. Exemption codes are printed on the pages. Privilege logs are
  exchanged between parties and are not always filed.
- Indexes. Sometimes provided. In FOIA litigation, an agency may be ordered to produce a detailed
  index justifying each withholding.
- Withheld material. Not public. It may be challenged through an administrative appeal or in court.

## Questions worth asking

- Which body made the release, under what authority, and what did it say was searched and withheld?
- Is this the official copy, and which batch or version is it?
- How many distinct documents mention the subject, after duplicates are removed?
- Does a document record an event, or record someone's claim about one?
- Were the same pages released elsewhere with different redactions?
- Does using a detail serve the reader, or only expose a private person?
