 This worked example applies the guide on [grading a source](/guides/method-grading-a-source/) to
the timeline on the [Program for Evolutionary Dynamics page](/program-for-evolutionary-dynamics/).
It adds no facts. Each statement restates a row in the [claim ledger](/ledger/), with its caveat.
The aim is to show why two kinds of source on one timeline carry different grades, and what a grade
does and does not say.

## Step 1: sort the sources by who produced them

The timeline draws on two kinds of source.

The first is official. The General Counsel's report on Epstein's connections to Harvard was
published on 1 May 2020, and Martin Nowak, the Program's director, was placed on paid administrative
leave (Ledger R-02) [@harvard-2020-report]. The President's message released the report the same
day. Both documents speak for the institution in its official role.

The second is reporting: accounts in The Harvard Crimson and Harvard Magazine of what the University
and its faculty did, and of what later documents contained. These outlets quote or follow records
and statements. They do not speak for the University.

## Step 2: grade the institutional report

The site's grade A1 means an official document speaking for itself, and an institutional report is
the first example the definition gives. The report is therefore A1 for what Harvard found and said.

Several timeline rows rest on it. [Ledger F-01](/ledger/#F-01) records that Epstein's gifts to
Harvard totalled about $9.1 million between 1998 and 2008, a University-wide total [@bacow-2020].
[Ledger A-01](/ledger/#A-01) records that he visited the Program's offices more than 40 times
between 2010 and 2018 [@harvard-2020-report]. Ledger A-02 records that he held a visitor key card,
the door passcode and a key to an office.

An A1 grade does not strip a row of its limits. The caveat on A-01 says the figure is an estimate,
and that the report says the records do not allow a precise count. The caveat on Ledger A-03 adds
the response of the Program's director, who told reviewers that other visitors also used the room
called "Jeffrey's Office." The grade describes the kind of document. The caveats describe what the
document itself says about its reach.

The method adds one more check: an institution's report about itself is still its own account. A
reader should note who commissioned it and what it was asked to examine. The grade records the
source's standing as a record of the institution's findings. It does not settle questions the report
did not address.

## Step 3: grade the news accounts

The site's grade A2 means reporting by an established outlet that quotes or closely follows primary
documents, or records an on-the-record statement. That fits the Crimson and Harvard Magazine items
on the timeline.

[Ledger R-03](/ledger/#R-03) records that in March 2021 the Faculty of Arts and Sciences found
policy violations, imposed two-year restrictions and closed the Program [@crimson-2021-nowak]. The
finding belongs to a Faculty of the University, but the ledger cites it through two news accounts,
so the row carries A2. The ledger grade is the grade of the strongest source that directly supports
the claim, not the standing of the body whose decision is described. If the Faculty's own
announcement were added to the register, the row could be regraded.

Ledger R-04 shows why the distinction matters. It records that the restrictions were lifted in
March 2023, reported by the student newspaper, with the caveat that no formal University
announcement was located [@crimson-2023-nowak]. The site has a single news account and nothing
official, so the row stays at A2 and its evidence state stays at documented activity.

Ledger R-06 records that Professor Nowak was placed on paid administrative leave a second time in
late February 2026 [@crimson-2026-nowak-leave]. Its caveat carries three limits: he is not accused
of a crime, sources differ by a day on the date, and the outcome has not been published. He has said
he had no knowledge of Epstein's crimes and regrets the connection. The grade does not alter any of
that.

## Step 4: keep grade and state apart

A grade describes the source. An evidence state describes the claim. The timeline has rows where the
two point in different directions.

[Ledger R-08](/ledger/#R-08) records that on 17 June 2026 the ranking member of the House Judiciary
Committee wrote to Harvard requesting records from 1998 onward [@raskin-2026]. The letter is a
primary record, so the row is A1. Its state is documented activity, because what the letter shows is
that a request was made. The caveat states that the letter's characterizations are assertions by
its authors. A high grade for the letter is not a high grade for what it asserts.

Ledger A-07 runs the other way in scale. It records that Professor Nowak's name returns more than
4,000 results in the Justice Department's released database [@ihe-2026-names]. The source is A2 and
the state is documented activity, but the caveat limits the claim sharply: it is a count of search
hits, including duplicates, and not a count of messages.

## Step 5: check the timeline entry against its row

A timeline entry can cite more sources than its ledger row lists. The test is the row. Where an
entry draws on both the report and a news account, the strongest source sets the grade, and the
weaker one is supporting. Where an entry rests only on reporting, it is A2 however official the
underlying event was. Where a source relays a weaker account inside a stronger article, the weaker
grade applies to that account.

## What the grades do not say

A grade is not a verdict on anyone named in a source. The A1 grade on the report says nothing about
whether its findings were right. A lower grade does not mean an account is wrong. Both say only what
kind of record the reader is holding. For how to cite each kind, see [citing
sources](/guides/method-citing-sources/).
