After a crisis, an institution usually makes commitments: a review, a new policy, a new office,
training, a donation of tainted funds, a report back to the community. Attention then moves on.
Whether the commitments were kept is rarely announced. Checking is straightforward work that anyone
can do with public sources.

## Step 1: Capture the commitments at the time

Collect every statement in which the institution said what it would do.

- Messages from the president, provost or deans.
- The recommendations section of any review report, and the institution's response to it.
- Press releases and official news articles.
- Any agreement with a regulator, which will list required actions and deadlines.
- Testimony or letters to legislators.

Save a copy of each with its date and web address. Web pages are revised and removed. A public web
archive can preserve a page as it stood on a given day.

## Step 2: Turn statements into a list

Write each commitment as one line with five fields.

1. What was promised, in the institution's exact words.
2. Who made the promise.
3. Who was to carry it out.
4. By when.
5. What evidence would show it was done.

Sort the list into two groups. Specific commitments have a verifiable outcome: "we will publish a
revised policy by the end of the academic year." General commitments do not: "we will reflect on our
values." Only the first group can be checked. Note the second group, and note that it could not be
checked.

Pay attention to verbs. "Will" is a commitment. "Will consider," "will explore" and "intends to" are
not. A promise to form a committee is kept when the committee is formed, whatever the committee
later does.

## Step 3: Look for evidence

For each specific commitment, search for the following.

- The document itself. If a policy was promised, find it, and check its adoption date and whether it
  does what was described.
- A named person. If an office or role was promised, see whether someone holds it and whether it
  still exists.
- A public report. If the institution promised to report back, look for the report.
- Budget or staffing evidence, where the commitment involved resources.
- For donated or redirected funds, an announcement naming the amount and the recipient, or the
  recipient's own acknowledgment.
- Coverage in the student newspaper, which often follows up when national media do not.
- For regulator agreements, any monitoring letters or a closure letter from the agency.

Record what you found and where. Record searches that found nothing, with the date you looked.

## Step 4: Classify each commitment

Use plain categories.

- Done, with evidence.
- Partly done. State which part.
- Done late.
- No public evidence either way.
- Withdrawn or replaced. State by what.

"No public evidence" is the most common result and the most easily misread. It means the public
record is silent. The work may have been done without announcement.

## Step 5: Ask

For items with no public evidence, ask the institution. A short, specific request works best: quote
the commitment, give its date and source, and ask what was done and where it is documented. Address
it to the office that made the commitment or to the communications office. See the guide on
transparency requests for how to frame it.

Record the reply, or the absence of one, with dates.

## What to look for beyond the list

- Durability. A new office that was quietly merged or left unfilled two years later was not a
  lasting change.
- Reach. A policy adopted by the central administration may not bind every school. See the guide on
  decentralized universities.
- Substance. Compare the adopted policy with the recommendation that prompted it. Recommendations
  are sometimes accepted "in principle" and adopted in weaker form.
- Measurement. Check whether the institution set any way to tell if the change worked, such as a
  repeat survey or an audit.

## What this method can and cannot tell you

It can show which public commitments have public evidence of completion, and which do not. It
produces a dated, sourced record that others can verify and extend.

It cannot show what happened inside the institution without disclosure. It cannot judge whether a
completed action was effective. It does not measure sincerity. It also covers only what was
promised: an institution that promised little will score well.

## Common mistakes

- Paraphrasing the promise. Use the original words, because later disputes turn on them.
- Counting an announcement of intent as completion.
- Treating silence as failure. State it as silence.
- Checking once. Some commitments have deadlines years out, and some completed changes are later
  reversed.
- Ignoring the passage of leadership. A new president may not feel bound by a predecessor's
  statement, which is itself worth documenting.

## A simple table to keep

Keep one table with these columns: commitment (quoted), source and date, responsible office,
deadline, evidence found, evidence source and date, status, date last checked. Update it once or
twice a year.
