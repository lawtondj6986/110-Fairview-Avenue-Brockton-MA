A settlement is an agreement that ends a legal dispute without a decision on the merits. The parties
trade the uncertainty of a ruling for agreed terms. The announcement of a settlement, whether a
joint statement, a press release or a court filing, is usually brief and carefully negotiated.
Knowing what a settlement is makes it easier to see what an announcement leaves out.

## What kind of settlement is it

- Private settlement of a lawsuit. A contract between the parties. The terms are often confidential,
  and the court record may show only that the case was dismissed.
- Class action settlement. Resolves the claims of a group. A court must approve it as fair, so the
  agreement and the amounts are filed publicly, with notice to class members.
- Settlement with a government agency. May take the form of a consent decree (entered and
  enforceable as a court order), a settlement agreement or a resolution agreement. These are usually
  public.
- Pre-suit settlement. Reached before any case is filed. There may be no public record at all.

Identify the type first. It determines how much you can expect to learn.

## Reading the amount

A headline figure needs unpacking.

1. Total versus net. The total often includes the plaintiffs' attorneys' fees and costs, and in
   class actions the cost of administering the settlement. What claimants receive is less.
2. Number of claimants. A large sum divided among many people may be modest for each. A figure with
   no claimant count cannot be assessed.
3. Who pays. An insurer may pay some or all. The announcement may not say.
4. Cash versus other value. Some settlements count the estimated value of services, programs or
   policy changes toward the total.
5. Timing. Payment may be in installments.
6. Caps and reversion. In some class settlements, money not claimed returns to the defendant.

A settlement amount reflects many things: the strength of the claims, the cost and risk of trial,
the defendant's wish to avoid publicity, insurance limits and the parties' resources. It is not a
measure of what happened.

## The no-admission clause

Nearly every settlement states that the defendant does not admit liability or wrongdoing. This is
standard and tells you little either way.

- It does not mean the claims were unfounded.
- It does not mean they were true.
- It means the question was not decided.

Under general rules of evidence, settlements and settlement offers usually cannot be used in court
to prove that a claim was valid. The law treats a settlement as a compromise, and readers should
too.

## Non-monetary terms

These are often more informative than the sum.

- Policy changes, training, new staff roles, audits or independent monitoring. These show what the
  complaining side considered deficient and what the defendant was willing to change.
- Reporting obligations and the length of any monitoring period.
- Confidentiality. Parties may agree not to disclose terms. Public institutions may be limited by
  public records law in what they can keep confidential, and rules vary by state.
- Non-disparagement. Parties may agree not to criticize each other.
- Release. The claims the plaintiff gives up, which may be broader than those in the lawsuit.
- Agreed statements. A joint statement is negotiated text. Each side's separate statement is
  advocacy.

## Reading the statements

- Compare the parties' statements. The plaintiff's side tends to describe vindication. The
  defendant's side tends to cite the cost and distraction of litigation. Both are positioning.
- Look for facts: date, amount, parties, what will change.
- Note what a statement declines to say and whether confidentiality is given as the reason.
- Watch for language suggesting the settlement confirms the allegations, or clears the defendant. It
  does neither.

## Where to find the actual terms

1. The court docket. Look for a motion for approval (in class actions), the agreement attached as an
   exhibit, a consent decree, or a stipulation of dismissal. See [the guide to reading a court
   docket](/guides/read-a-court-docket/).
2. Dismissal terms. "With prejudice" means the claims cannot be filed again. "Without prejudice"
   means they can.
3. The agency's website, for government settlements.
4. Public records requests, for settlements paid by public institutions, subject to state law.
5. Financial statements and governing board minutes, which occasionally disclose large settlements.

When the agreement itself is available, read it in preference to any announcement.

## What a settlement announcement can tell you

- That a dispute ended by agreement, and when.
- Usually the parties, and sometimes the amount.
- Sometimes the changes the defendant agreed to make.
- That neither side obtained a ruling.

## What it cannot tell you

- Whether the allegations were true.
- Why each side settled.
- What individual claimants received, in most cases.
- Terms kept confidential.
- Whether agreed changes were carried out. That requires follow-up. See [the guide on following up
  on promises](/guides/follow-up-on-promises-after-a-scandal/).

## Common mistakes

- Treating payment as an admission.
- Treating the no-admission clause as exoneration.
- Reporting the gross figure as what the plaintiffs received.
- Comparing amounts across cases with different numbers of claimants and different claims.
- Describing a case as "won" or "lost." A settled case was neither.
- Stopping at the announcement when the agreement is on the public docket.

## How to describe a settlement accurately

State who settled with whom, when, and for how much if known. Say what the claims alleged, with
attribution. Say that the defendant did not admit wrongdoing, if that is the case, and that no court
ruled on the merits. Describe any agreed changes. Note what is not public.

Definitions of consent decree, release and related terms are in the [glossary](/glossary/).
