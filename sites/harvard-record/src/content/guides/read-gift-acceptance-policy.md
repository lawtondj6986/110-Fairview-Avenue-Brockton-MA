A gift acceptance policy sets out which donations a university will take, on what terms, and who
decides. Some are public and detailed. Others are short, internal, or split across several
documents. Reading one well means looking past the principles to the procedures.

## What the document is

A gift acceptance policy is an internal rule adopted by the governing board or senior
administration. It is not a law, and its details are largely the institution's own choice. It
usually sits alongside related policies: a naming policy, a conflict of interest policy, a policy on
sponsored research, and sometimes a separate policy on anonymous gifts.

Check the date and the approving body first. A policy revised recently, with a stated review cycle,
is a living document. An undated policy is hard to hold anyone to.

## What it usually covers

- Types of assets accepted, such as cash, securities, real estate and bequests.
- The difference between a gift and other funding. A gift is a voluntary transfer for which the
  donor receives nothing of substantial value in return. Sponsored research and contracts involve
  deliverables and are handled under different rules.
- Restrictions a donor may place on the use of a gift, and restrictions the university will refuse.
- Who may accept gifts on the institution's behalf, often with different approval levels by size.
- Naming of buildings, professorships and programs.
- Anonymous gifts.
- Circumstances in which a gift may be declined or returned.

## Clauses to read closely

### Who decides

Look for a named decision-maker for difficult cases. A standing committee with members outside the
fundraising office is a real control. A policy that leaves hard calls to the fundraisers who are
responsible for meeting targets has a built-in conflict.

Note whether the policy applies to the whole university or only to the central development office.
In a decentralized university, individual schools, centers and faculty members also receive money.
If the policy does not clearly bind them, it has a gap. See the guide on who decides what in a
decentralized university.

### Due diligence

Look for a description of what is checked before a gift is accepted: the source of the funds, the
donor's public record, and any legal restrictions. Useful details include the size of gift that
triggers a review, who performs it, and whether the review is repeated for later gifts from the same
donor.

Check whether review covers the original source of funds. Gifts can arrive through a foundation, a
company, a donor-advised fund or an intermediary. A policy that reviews only the entity that writes
the check can miss who is actually giving.

### Anonymity

Anonymous to the public is different from anonymous to the institution. A sound policy requires that
someone at the university knows the donor's identity and has vetted the gift, even when the name is
withheld from publication. Look for who holds that knowledge and whether the decision to grant
anonymity is recorded.

### Donor involvement

Look for language on academic independence. Common provisions state that donors may not select
faculty, choose students, direct research conclusions or control curriculum. Check whether the
policy also addresses softer benefits: visiting appointments, office space, use of the university's
name, access to events and introductions to researchers.

### Declining and returning gifts

Look for stated grounds on which a gift will be refused, and whether there is a process for
reconsidering a gift already accepted. Policies differ on whether money can be returned once spent
or added to an endowment. Naming policies often include a clause allowing a name to be removed.

### Records

Look for a requirement that gifts be recorded centrally, with donor identity, purpose and approval.
Central records are what make any later review possible.

## Questions the policy should let you answer

1. If a donor with a serious public controversy offered a gift, who would decide, and using what
   criteria?
2. If the same donor offered money directly to a single professor's lab, would the answer be the
   same?
3. Could a gift be accepted anonymously without senior approval?
4. What can a donor receive in return, and what is ruled out?
5. Who would know if the policy were not followed?

If the document cannot answer most of these, it is a statement of values more than a control.

## What a policy can and cannot tell you

A policy tells you what the institution has committed to on paper and who is accountable. It tells
you how seriously the drafting was taken.

It does not tell you whether the policy is followed. It does not list the gifts received or
declined. It does not cover conduct before its adoption date. A strong policy written after a
problem came to light describes the intended future, not the past.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming that a published policy applies to every unit of the university.
- Reading "may" as "must." A committee that may be consulted is not a required step.
- Confusing a gift policy with rules on research funding, which follow different law and different
  offices.
- Treating the absence of a public policy as proof that none exists. Many are internal. Asking for
  it is reasonable.
