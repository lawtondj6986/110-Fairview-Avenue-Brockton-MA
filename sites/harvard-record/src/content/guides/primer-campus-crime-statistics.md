Every college or university that takes part in federal student aid programs must publish crime
statistics under the [Clery Act](/law/clery-act/). The figures appear in the [annual security
report](/glossary/annual-security-report/) and are submitted to the U.S. Department of Education,
which makes them available online. Surveys of students, such as a [campus climate
survey](/glossary/campus-climate-survey/), often report much higher rates of some offenses. Both can
be accurate. They measure different things. This primer explains how the official numbers are built
and why they diverge from surveys.

## What the Clery count is

A Clery statistic is a count of crimes reported to certain people at the institution, in certain
places, in certain categories, during a calendar year. Each part of that sentence limits what is
counted.

- Reported. The count reflects reports, not convictions, arrests or findings. A crime is counted
  when it is reported to the right people, whether or not anyone is charged.
- To certain people. Reports to campus police or security, to local police, and to [campus security
  authorities](/glossary/campus-security-authority/) count. A campus security authority is an
  official with significant responsibility for student and campus activities, such as a dean of
  students or a coach. Professional and pastoral counselors acting in that role are not campus
  security authorities.
- In certain places. Only crimes within [Clery geography](/glossary/clery-geography/) count. That
  means on-campus property (with residence halls reported as a subset), certain noncampus buildings
  owned or controlled by the institution or by recognized student organizations, and public property
  immediately adjacent to campus. A crime in an off-campus apartment a few blocks away is usually
  outside the count.
- In certain categories. The law lists the offenses: criminal homicide, sex offenses, robbery,
  aggravated assault, burglary, motor vehicle theft and arson; hate crimes; dating violence,
  domestic violence and stalking; and arrests and disciplinary referrals for liquor, drug and
  weapons violations. Definitions are drawn from the FBI's crime reporting programs, not from state
  criminal codes.
- During a calendar year. Crimes are counted in the year they were reported, which may differ from
  the year they happened.

## How the numbers are gathered

The institution's Clery compliance staff, often within campus police, assemble the figures. They
pull incidents from police records. They collect reports from campus security authorities, usually
through a form that may omit names. They ask local law enforcement agencies for crimes reported to
them within the institution's geography. Local agencies are asked, but cooperation and data quality
vary.

Each incident is then classified. Staff decide which Clery category fits, apply rules for incidents
involving more than one offense, and check whether the location falls inside the geography. A report
can be removed from the count as "unfounded" only under narrow conditions, when sworn or
commissioned law enforcement personnel determine it was false or baseless; unfounded crimes are
themselves disclosed. The report covers the three most recent calendar years.

The Department of Education has issued detailed guidance on these rules and has revised it,
including by replacing a long handbook with shorter guidance in recent years. The categories and
procedures for a given year depend on the guidance in force then. The Department can fine
institutions for Clery violations after a program review.

## What a survey measures

A climate survey asks a sample of students, anonymously, about their own experiences. It usually
describes behaviors ("did someone do the following without your consent") rather than using legal
labels, because many people do not use legal terms for what happened to them. It asks about a period
such as the current academic year or time since enrolling. It covers experiences wherever they
happened, including off campus and before enrollment if the questions allow. Crucially, it captures
incidents that were never reported to anyone.

A survey therefore estimates prevalence: how many people experienced something. A Clery count
records reported incidents. The difference is explained in [prevalence versus reported
incidents](/glossary/prevalence-vs-reported-incidents/).

Surveys have their own limits. Results depend on the [response rate](/glossary/response-rate/) and
on who chose to respond. Question wording and time frames differ between surveys, so figures from
two instruments rarely line up. A survey gives an estimate with uncertainty, not a count.

## Why the two differ

- Reporting. Many incidents, especially sexual assault, are never reported to police or campus
  officials. They can appear in a survey and never in Clery statistics.
- Geography. A survey covers off-campus incidents that Clery excludes.
- Definitions. Survey behavior questions and FBI-derived Clery definitions do not map one to one.
- Units. Clery counts incidents. Surveys usually count people, as a percentage of respondents.
- Time. Clery uses the calendar year of the report. Surveys use the period named in the question.

A rise in Clery numbers can mean more crime, or better reporting, or a change in how incidents were
classified. A low number can mean a safe campus, or a campus where few people report. The guide on
[figures that differ](/guides/method-figures-that-differ/) covers reasoning about such gaps.

## A worked hypothetical

A mid-sized university's report lists a handful of rapes on campus for the most recent year, most of
them in residence halls. The same year, its climate survey estimates that a meaningful share of
undergraduate respondents experienced nonconsensual sexual contact during the academic year.

Both are consistent. Several incidents described by survey respondents happened at off-campus
parties. Many were never reported. Some reported incidents were classified as fondling rather than
rape under the Clery definitions. One reported in January described an event the previous autumn, so
it falls in the new year's count. The two numbers answer different questions.

## Related records that are not statistics

Clery also requires a [daily crime log](/glossary/daily-crime-log/) maintained by institutions with
a campus police or security department, which is open to public inspection and lists incidents by
date, location and nature. It requires [timely warnings](/glossary/timely-warning/) about certain
crimes that pose a continuing threat and [emergency
notifications](/glossary/emergency-notification/) for immediate threats. These records show
individual events and can be checked against the annual totals.

## What the documents look like

- Incident reports and police records. Created by campus police. Access depends on state law and on
  whether the department is a public agency; many are not public.
- Campus security authority reports. Internal forms. Not public.
- Requests to and replies from local police. Internal correspondence. Not usually public.
- Daily crime log. Public. Available for inspection, and many institutions post it online.
- Annual security report. Public. It includes the statistics and statements of policy, such as how
  to report and how disciplinary proceedings work.
- Federal submission. Public through the Department of Education's online data tool, which allows
  comparison across years and institutions.
- Program review reports and fine letters. When the Department finds violations, its reports and
  letters are often public or obtainable by records request.
- Climate survey reports. Published by the institution or a survey consortium when the institution
  chooses or state law requires. Raw data are rarely public.

## Questions worth asking

- Which offenses and which geography do the statistics cover, and is a nearby area where students
  live inside or outside it?
- Did the numbers change because of reporting, classification or guidance rather than events?
- Has the Department of Education reviewed the institution's Clery compliance, and with what result?
- Does the institution run a climate survey, how many students responded, and what period did the
  questions cover?
- How do the daily crime log entries for a period compare with the annual totals?
- Who are the campus security authorities, and how are they trained to report?
