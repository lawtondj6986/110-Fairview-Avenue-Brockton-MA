When an institution faces a serious question about its own conduct, it often responds by
commissioning a review. The review may be done by staff, by a committee of faculty or trustees, or
by an outside law firm. Its findings may be released in full, summarized, or kept private. The
choices made at the start (who commissions the review, what it may examine, and who controls the
result) shape what the review can find. This primer explains how such reviews are usually set up and
conducted. Practice varies, and the vocabulary is not standardized.

## Why a review is commissioned

Common triggers include press reports, lawsuits, complaints from faculty or students, government
inquiries and pressure from donors or alumni. A review can serve several purposes at once: finding
out what happened, deciding whether to discipline anyone, preparing for litigation or regulators,
repairing reputation and recommending changes. These purposes can pull in different directions. A
review designed to establish facts for the public is different from one designed to give legal
advice to the institution.

## Who does the work

Reviews fall into a few broad types, discussed in the glossary entry on [internal and external
review](/glossary/internal-and-external-review/).

- Internal offices. The [general counsel's](/glossary/general-counsel/) office, internal audit, a
  compliance office or an HR investigator handles the matter. This is common for routine issues.
- Faculty or administrative committees. A committee of faculty and staff is appointed to examine a
  question and make recommendations.
- Board committees. For matters involving senior leaders, the governing board may form a [special
  committee](/glossary/special-committee/) of members without involvement in the events, which then
  hires outside help and reports to the full board.
- Outside counsel or consultants. A law firm or consulting firm is retained to investigate and
  report. This is the form most often described as an [independent
  investigation](/glossary/independent-investigation/).
- Outside bodies. Regulators, courts, agencies or legislative committees conduct their own
  inquiries. These are not commissioned by the institution and are outside its control.

## The engagement and the scope

An outside review begins with an engagement letter, a contract between the institution and the firm.
It identifies the client, defines the scope, sets reporting lines and addresses confidentiality and
privilege. The client is usually the institution or a committee of its board. The engagement letter
is rarely published, though its key terms are sometimes summarized in the announcement or the final
report.

Scope is the most consequential choice. A review can be limited to a single person, a period of
time, a set of gifts or a particular question. Matters outside the scope may not be examined even if
the reviewers come across them. Scope can also change during a review, with or without public
notice.

## Privilege and confidentiality

When a review is conducted by lawyers acting as legal counsel to the institution, the communications
and work product may be protected by attorney-client privilege and the work-product doctrine.
Privilege belongs to the client. The institution decides what to waive, and therefore what to
release. Witnesses interviewed by the institution's lawyers are typically told that the lawyers
represent the institution, not the witness, and that the institution controls whether what they say
is disclosed.

A review set up as a fact-finding exercise for public release may be structured differently, but
privilege questions often remain. The [privilege log](/glossary/privilege-log/) entry explains how
withheld material is listed in litigation, which is one of the few settings where its existence
becomes visible.

## Access to evidence

A review is only as good as what it can see. Reviewers typically collect documents (email,
calendars, financial records, gift records, database entries) and interview witnesses. Questions
that determine the quality of the evidence include:

- Did reviewers have direct access to email and records systems, or did they receive only what
  institutional staff chose to provide?
- Were current and former employees required, or merely invited, to cooperate? An institution can
  require cooperation from current employees as a condition of employment. It usually cannot compel
  former employees, donors or outsiders.
- Were records preserved from the moment the review was contemplated? The institution's [records
  retention policy](/glossary/records-retention-policy/) may have allowed deletion of older
  material.
- Did any key witnesses decline to be interviewed?

A credible report says what it could and could not examine.

## Findings and the report

At the end, reviewers deliver findings, often in a written report and an oral briefing. Findings are
statements of fact the reviewers consider established, usually by a stated standard such as more
likely than not (see [preponderance of the evidence](/glossary/preponderance-of-the-evidence/)).
Some reports also reach conclusions about whether policies were violated and make recommendations.

A [fact-finding report](/glossary/fact-finding-report/) may be released in full, released with
[redactions](/glossary/redaction/), summarized in a public statement, or kept confidential. Reports
often omit names of witnesses and of people who were not found to have done anything wrong. They
rarely publish the underlying evidence. The guide on [reading a fact-finding
report](/guides/read-a-fact-finding-report/) covers how to read one.

What happens next is a separate process. Discipline of individuals follows its own procedures,
described in the [primer on faculty discipline](/guides/primer-faculty-discipline/). Policy changes
may be announced and then implemented, partly implemented or quietly dropped; see the guide on
[following up on promises](/guides/follow-up-on-promises-after-a-scandal/).

## What makes a review credible

No single feature guarantees credibility, but several are widely regarded as important. The guide on
[whether a review was independent](/guides/was-an-internal-review-independent/) offers a checklist.

- The reviewer is chosen by people with no involvement in the events, often a board committee rather
  than the administration under scrutiny.
- The scope is published at the start and is broad enough to answer the question that prompted the
  review.
- The reviewer has full and direct access to records and can interview anyone the institution can
  require to cooperate.
- The reviewer has no ongoing business with the institution that would create pressure, or any such
  relationship is disclosed.
- The institution commits in advance to publishing the findings, and the report states its methods,
  sources and limits.
- People criticized in the report are given a chance to respond before publication.
- The institution responds publicly to each recommendation.

## A worked hypothetical

After press reports that a donor had unusual access to a lab, a university announces that a law firm
will review the matter. The firm is retained by the general counsel. The announcement says the
review covers the donor's gifts. Over several months the firm collects development office records
and interviews staff. Two former employees and the donor decline interviews. The firm's report finds
that gifts were accepted according to policy but that some contacts were not logged. It makes
recommendations. The university releases a summary of the findings, not the full report.

A reader can see from this account that the scope covered gifts but not access, that the client was
the general counsel rather than a board committee, that key witnesses were missing, and that the
full report was not released. None of these points establishes that the findings are wrong. They
define what the review could have found.

## What the documents look like

- Announcement: a press release or message to the community stating that a review has begun,
  sometimes with scope and reviewer. Public.
- Engagement letter: the contract setting client, scope and privilege terms. Internal.
- Document requests and preservation notices: instructions to staff to preserve and produce records.
  Internal.
- Interview memos: lawyers' notes of interviews, usually treated as privileged. Internal.
- Interim briefings: oral or written updates to the client. Internal.
- Final report: public, summarized or confidential, at the institution's choice.
- Response and implementation reports: statements on which recommendations were adopted. Sometimes
  public.
- Related outside records: government inquiries, court filings and congressional letters, which may
  cite or contradict the review.

## Questions worth asking

1. Who commissioned the review, and to whom did the reviewers report?
2. What was the written scope, and did it change?
3. Was the reviewer acting as legal counsel to the institution?
4. What records did the reviewers have, and who declined to cooperate?
5. What standard did the reviewers use to make findings?
6. Was the full report released, and if not, what was withheld and why?
7. Which recommendations were adopted, and who is checking that they were implemented?
