## What it does

Chapter 151B of the Massachusetts General Laws is the state's main anti-discrimination statute. It
prohibits discrimination in employment, and also in housing, credit and some other areas. This
explainer deals with employment.

In employment, the statute makes it unlawful to refuse to hire, to discharge, or to discriminate in
pay or in the terms, conditions or privileges of employment because of a protected characteristic.
The list of protected characteristics is longer than under federal law. It includes race, color,
religious creed, national origin, ancestry, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, age,
disability, genetic information, pregnancy and related conditions, and military or veteran status.
The list has been extended over time, so read the current text.

The statute addresses sexual harassment by name. It defines sexual harassment as sexual advances,
requests for sexual favors, and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature in two
situations. The first is when submission to or rejection of the conduct is made, explicitly or
implicitly, a term or condition of employment or a basis for employment decisions. The second is
when the conduct has the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with a person's work
performance by creating an intimidating, hostile, humiliating or sexually offensive work
environment.

A separate section requires employers to adopt a written policy against sexual harassment. The
policy must include a statement that sexual harassment is unlawful, a description and examples, the
consequences for harassers, how to file an internal complaint, and how to contact the state and
federal agencies. Employers must give the policy to new employees and to all employees each year.
The statute encourages, but does not require, training.

The statute also prohibits retaliation against a person who opposes a forbidden practice or takes
part in a proceeding, and it prohibits coercion, intimidation, threats or interference with rights
the chapter protects. It reaches any person who aids or abets a forbidden act.

## Who it covers

Chapter 151B applies to employers with six or more employees, which is a lower threshold than the 15
employees required under the federal Title VII. It also covers the Commonwealth and its
subdivisions, employment agencies and labor organizations. Certain small nonprofit social or
fraternal clubs are excluded, and there are limited provisions for religious organizations.

Universities, public and private, are covered as employers. Faculty, staff and student employees are
protected in their employment.

Students as students are not covered by the employment provisions. Massachusetts has a separate
statute on fair educational practices, Chapter 151C, which among other things addresses sexual
harassment of students by educational institutions.

## What it does not do

Like federal law, Chapter 151B is not a general code of workplace civility. Conduct must meet the
statutory definition and be connected to a protected characteristic.

It does not protect people who are not employees or applicants, such as true independent
contractors, although other Massachusetts laws may apply to them.

It does not displace federal law. A person may have claims under both, and the two are often filed
together. But the laws are not identical, and Massachusetts courts do not always follow federal
interpretations. Three differences are often noted. Massachusetts courts have held employers
strictly liable for sexual harassment by supervisors, without the affirmative defense that federal
law allows. Individuals, not only employers, can be liable under the provisions on aiding, abetting
and interference. And Chapter 151B has no statutory cap on compensatory damages of the kind found in
Title VII.

It does not allow a person to go directly to court. A complaint must first be filed with the state
commission.

## How it is enforced

The Massachusetts Commission Against Discrimination enforces the chapter. A complaint must be filed
with the Commission within 300 days of the alleged act. Where the conduct is a continuing pattern, a
complaint may be timely if at least one act in the pattern falls within the period, under rules
developed by the Commission and the courts.

The Commission investigates and decides whether there is probable cause. If it finds probable cause,
it attempts conciliation and, if that fails, holds a public hearing before a commissioner or hearing
officer, who may order remedies including back pay, emotional distress damages, reinstatement,
training and civil penalties. Decisions can be appealed to the full Commission and then to the
courts.

A complainant may instead take the case to court. After 90 days from filing with the Commission, or
sooner with a commissioner's consent, the complainant may bring a civil action in the Superior
Court, and must do so within three years of the alleged act. In court a jury trial is available, and
the court may award actual damages, punitive damages and attorney's fees.

Because of a work-sharing arrangement, a complaint filed with the state Commission is generally also
treated as filed with the federal Equal Employment Opportunity Commission where federal law applies.

## Common misreadings

It is often assumed that the federal and state standards are the same. The differences on supervisor
liability, individual liability and damages can change the outcome.

It is often assumed that a policy on paper satisfies the law. The policy requirement is one duty.
Liability for harassment that actually occurs is a separate matter.

It is sometimes assumed that complaints to the Commission are public from the start. Investigative
files are generally not open during the investigation, and many matters settle without a public
decision. Public hearing decisions are published.

It is sometimes assumed that the 300-day period runs from when the person left the job. It runs from
the discriminatory act.

## Where to read it

Read Chapter 151B on the Legislature's website. The definitions, including that of sexual
harassment, are in section 1. The main prohibitions are in section 4, the policy requirement is in
section 3A, the Commission's procedure is in section 5, and the court action is in section 9. The
Commission's regulations are in title 804 of the Code of Massachusetts Regulations, and the
Commission publishes guidelines on sexual harassment in the workplace. See the Title VII explainer
in `/law/`.
