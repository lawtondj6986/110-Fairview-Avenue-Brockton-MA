## What it does

The Sex Offender Registration and Notification Act (SORNA) is Title I of the Adam Walsh Child Protection and Safety Act of 2006. It replaced earlier federal registration laws with a more detailed set of national minimum standards.

SORNA does not create a single federal registry. Registration in the United States is run by states, territories, the District of Columbia and many tribal governments. SORNA tells these jurisdictions what their registries should contain, and links them through a national public website that searches all the public registries at once.

Its main elements are these.

- Who must register. A person convicted of a sex offense, as the Act defines that term, must register. The definition covers offenses under federal, state, tribal, military and some foreign law.
- Where. A registrant must register, and keep the registration current, in each jurisdiction where they live, where they are an employee, and where they are a student.
- Tiers. The Act sorts offenders into three tiers by the seriousness of the offense. The tier sets the length of the registration period (15 years, 25 years or life) and how often the person must appear in person to verify their information (once, twice or four times a year). The shortest period can be reduced for a person who keeps a clean record.
- Information. The registry must hold specified information, such as name, addresses, employer, school, vehicle details, a photograph and the offense.
- Updates. A registrant must appear in person within three business days after a change of name, residence, employment or student status.
- Public notification. Jurisdictions must post most registrant information on a public website, with certain items withheld.

Later amendments added a requirement that registrants report planned international travel in advance.

## Who it covers

The Act imposes duties on two groups. The first is jurisdictions, which are expected to conform their registry laws to the federal standards. The second is individuals convicted of covered offenses, who have a federal duty to register that exists alongside their duties under state law.

Certain juveniles adjudicated delinquent for serious offenses are included. Some consensual conduct between people close in age is excluded from the definition.

Colleges are affected indirectly. An earlier federal law, carried forward in the Clery Act, requires each institution to tell the campus community where information about registered sex offenders can be obtained. State registries are in turn expected to collect information about a registrant's enrollment or employment at an institution of higher education.

## What it does not do

SORNA does not make state law uniform. Many jurisdictions have not fully implemented its standards. State laws differ on which offenses require registration, how long registration lasts, which registrants appear on the public website, and what restrictions apply. A person's duties in practice depend heavily on the state.

It does not, itself, impose restrictions on where a registrant may live or work or go. Residency and presence restrictions, where they exist, come from state or local law or from conditions of supervision.

It does not bar a registrant from being on a campus, donating to an institution, or associating with anyone. Whether an institution permits a registered person to enroll, work, visit or give is a matter of the institution's policies and of any applicable state law.

It does not require registration for an arrest, a charge or a civil finding. Registration follows a conviction, or a qualifying juvenile adjudication.

It does not ensure that every public registry entry is current or complete. Registries depend on information registrants provide and on agency resources.

## How it is enforced

Against jurisdictions, the tool is financial. A jurisdiction that the Department of Justice finds has not substantially implemented the Act loses a percentage of a federal criminal justice grant each year. An office within the Department of Justice, known as the SMART Office, reviews implementation and issues guidelines.

Against individuals, there are two layers. Each jurisdiction must make failure to register a crime under its own law. In addition, a federal statute, 18 U.S.C. § 2250, makes it a crime to knowingly fail to register or update a registration when the person was convicted of a federal sex offense, or travels in interstate or foreign commerce, or enters, leaves or resides in Indian country. The United States Marshals Service is the lead federal agency for locating noncompliant registrants.

## Common misreadings

It is often assumed that there is one national registry with one set of rules. There are many registries with different rules, and a national search tool.

It is often assumed that tier labels mean the same thing everywhere. SORNA's tiers are based on the offense of conviction. Some states use the same words for classifications based on an individual risk assessment. Massachusetts, for example, classifies by level through a state board. A tier or level in one system cannot be translated directly into another.

It is often assumed that the public website shows every registrant. Jurisdictions may withhold some categories from public view.

A registry entry shows that a person was convicted of a registrable offense and is, or was, subject to registration. It does not describe the facts of the offense in detail, and it says nothing about conduct for which there was no conviction.
