## What it does

United States citizens are ordinarily taxed by the federal government on their income from anywhere
in the world. Moving to another state does not change that. The territories are different. Puerto
Rico, the United States Virgin Islands, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands and American Samoa each
have their own tax systems, and the Internal Revenue Code contains special rules that coordinate
federal tax with territorial tax.

Those coordination rules, combined with laws passed by territorial legislatures, make possible
economic development programs that offer very low tax rates to people and businesses that move to a
territory. Two models are best known.

In the first model, the territory has its own income tax law. A bona fide resident of that territory
may exclude from federal gross income the income derived from sources within the territory. The
territory is then free to tax that income lightly or not at all. Puerto Rico works this way, and its
legislature has enacted incentive statutes for exporters of services and for individual investors
who become residents.

In the second model, the territory uses the Internal Revenue Code itself as its local tax law, a
so-called mirror system, and a bona fide resident satisfies their United States income tax
obligation by filing and paying in the territory. Federal law generally forbids such a territory
from reducing the tax it collects, but it allows reductions for income from sources within the
territory or effectively connected with a trade or business there. The United States Virgin Islands
works this way, and its economic development program grants approved businesses a large reduction of
income tax on qualifying income, together with reductions in other local taxes.

In both models the beneficiary applies to a territorial agency, receives a decree, certificate or
grant for a term of years, and takes on commitments. Commitments typically include maintaining an
office, employing a minimum number of local residents, making a capital investment and making
charitable contributions in the territory.

This overview is general. Rates, eligibility rules and required commitments are set by territorial
law and have been revised repeatedly. Read the current statutes and agency rules for specifics.

## Who it covers

The federal rules apply to individuals who are bona fide residents of a territory and to businesses
operating there. The territorial programs are open to those who apply and are approved under the
program's criteria, which usually favor designated industries such as financial services,
consulting, technology, manufacturing or tourism.

Under federal law, bona fide residence is not a matter of declaration. Since a 2004 change in the
law, a person must generally meet three tests for the tax year: a presence test based on days
physically spent in the territory, a requirement of no tax home outside the territory, and a
requirement of no closer connection to the United States or a foreign country than to the territory.

## What it does not do

The incentives do not cover income from outside the territory. This is the most important limit. The
same 2004 legislation tightened the sourcing rules so that, in general, income that would be treated
as United States source income, or as effectively connected with a business in the United States,
under ordinary principles is not treated as territorial income. Compensation for services is
generally sourced where the services are performed. A person who lives in a territory but earns fees
for work performed on the mainland does not convert those fees into low-taxed territorial income.

They do not, in themselves, indicate wrongdoing. These are lawful programs created by statute and
intended to attract investment and jobs to economies that have struggled. Taking part in one is not
evidence of impropriety. The legal questions are whether the participant really resides in the
territory, whether the income really arises there, and whether the commitments in the grant are
kept.

## How it is enforced

Two governments have roles. The territorial agency that grants the benefits can audit compliance
with the grant's conditions and can revoke or decline to renew a grant.

The Internal Revenue Service enforces the federal residence and sourcing rules. A person who begins
or ends bona fide residence in a territory must generally file an information statement with the
Service. The Service has examined participants in these programs and has identified them as a
compliance priority. Where it concludes that a taxpayer was not a bona fide resident, or that income
was United States source, it can assess federal tax, interest and penalties.

## Common misreadings

It is often said that moving to a territory makes a person's income tax-free. The benefit is limited
to qualifying income from sources within the territory, earned by a genuine resident.

## Where to read it

The federal provisions are 26 U.S.C. § 932 (Virgin Islands coordination), § 933 (Puerto Rico source
income), § 934 (limits on reduction of Virgin Islands tax) and § 937 (residence and source rules),
with regulations under each. The Internal Revenue Service publishes a tax guide for individuals with
income from United States territories. The incentive programs themselves are in territorial statutes
and agency rules. See `/donor-pathways/` and `/glossary/` on this site.
