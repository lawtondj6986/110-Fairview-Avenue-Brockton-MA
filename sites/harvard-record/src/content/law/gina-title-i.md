## What it does

The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 (GINA) has two main parts. Title I deals with
health insurance. Title II deals with employment and has its own explainer in `/law/`.

Title I is not a single section of the U.S. Code. It works by amending older laws that already
regulate health coverage: the Employee Retirement Income Security Act, the Public Health Service
Act, the Internal Revenue Code, and the part of the Social Security Act that governs Medicare
supplemental policies.

Its rules, in outline:

- A group health plan or health insurer may not adjust premiums or contribution amounts for a group
  on the basis of genetic information.
- An insurer in the individual market may not use genetic information to decide eligibility or to
  set premiums, and may not treat genetic information as a preexisting condition.
- Plans and insurers may not request or require an individual or a family member to undergo a
  genetic test.
- Plans and insurers may not request, require or purchase genetic information for underwriting
  purposes, or about an individual before enrollment.

Genetic information is defined broadly. It includes a person's genetic test results, the genetic
test results of family members, and the manifestation of a disease or disorder in family members,
which is to say family medical history. It also includes requests for or receipt of genetic
services, and participation in clinical research that includes genetic services. Information about a
person's sex or age is not genetic information.

GINA also directed the Department of Health and Human Services to revise the HIPAA Privacy Rule so
that genetic information is treated as health information and so that most health plans may not use
or disclose it for underwriting. That change was made by regulation in 2013.

## Who it covers

Title I covers group health plans (including self-insured employer plans), health insurance issuers
in the group and individual markets, and issuers of Medicare supplemental policies. The people
protected are applicants, enrollees and their dependents, along with the family members whose
information counts as genetic information about them.

## What it does not do

Title I applies only to health coverage. It does not apply to life insurance, disability insurance
or long-term care insurance. Whether an insurer in those lines may ask about or use genetic test
results depends on state law, which varies. See the explainer on state genetic privacy laws in
`/law/`.

It does not protect against the use of a manifested condition. If a person has been diagnosed with a
disease, GINA does not stop a health plan from taking the disease itself into account. Other federal
law, mainly the Affordable Care Act, separately limits the use of health status in most health
coverage.

It does not stop a treating clinician from ordering or recommending a genetic test, and it allows a
plan to ask for the minimum genetic information needed to decide whether a claim should be paid. A
plan may also ask, but not require, a person to take a genetic test as part of research, under
stated conditions, so long as the result is not used for underwriting.

It does not regulate what researchers, direct-to-consumer testing companies, data brokers or law
enforcement do with genetic data. Those questions fall under other laws or, in places, under none.

## How it is enforced

Because Title I amends several statutes, enforcement is split. The Department of Labor enforces the
provisions for employer-sponsored plans under its authority over employee benefit plans. The
Department of Health and Human Services, with state insurance regulators, covers insurers. The
Treasury Department, through the Internal Revenue Service, can impose excise taxes on noncompliant
group health plans. The statute sets out per-day penalties with minimums and caps. Read the current
text for amounts.

A participant in an employer plan may also be able to use the general enforcement provisions of the
employee benefits law. Complaints about an insurer usually start with the state insurance
department.

## Common misreadings

GINA is often taken to mean that genetic information cannot be used against a person in any
insurance. That overstates it. The gap for life, disability and long-term care insurance is the
best-known limit of the law.

It is sometimes thought to protect only test results. Family medical history is covered too, and for
most people that is the more common kind of genetic information a plan might ask about.

It is sometimes thought to guarantee confidentiality of research data. GINA limits what health plans
and employers may do with genetic information. It does not control who else may obtain data from a
study or a testing company. Research protections come from the Common Rule, certificates of
confidentiality and the terms of consent.

A final point: GINA sets a federal minimum. States may give greater protection, and many do.

## Where to read it

The Act is Public Law 110-233. Title I's provisions are spread across Title 29, Title 42 and Title
26 of the U.S. Code, which makes them hard to read in one place. The joint regulations of the three
departments, and the summaries those departments publish, are easier starting points. For how
genetic topics appear on this site, see `/dna-genetics/`.
