## What it does

The Uniform Prudent Management of Institutional Funds Act (UPMIFA) is a model statute approved by
the Uniform Law Commission in 2006. It replaced an earlier uniform act from 1972. A uniform act has
no force until a state legislature enacts it. Nearly every state and the District of Columbia have
done so, some with changes. Massachusetts has adopted it. The law that applies to a given
institution is its own state's version.

UPMIFA does three things.

First, it sets the standard of conduct for managing and investing institutional funds. Those
responsible must act in good faith and with the care an ordinarily prudent person in a like position
would exercise under similar circumstances. They must consider the charitable purposes of the
institution and of the fund. Investment decisions are judged in the context of the whole portfolio,
costs must be reasonable, and the institution may delegate investment management to outside agents
if it selects and monitors them prudently.

Second, it governs spending from endowment funds. An endowment fund, in the Act's terms, is a fund
that under the terms of a gift instrument is not wholly expendable by the institution on a current
basis. Subject to the donor's intent as expressed in the gift instrument, the institution may spend
or accumulate as much of an endowment fund as it determines is prudent for the uses, benefits,
purposes and duration for which the fund was established. The Act lists factors to be considered.

This replaced an older rule that barred spending below a fund's historic dollar value, meaning the
amount originally given. Under UPMIFA an institution may prudently spend from a fund whose market
value has fallen below the original gift. The uniform text offers states an optional provision under
which spending more than seven percent of a fund's value in a year, averaged over a period, is
presumed imprudent. Only some states adopted it.

Third, it sets out how a restriction in a gift instrument can be released or modified:

- With the donor's consent in a record, the institution may release or modify a restriction, so long
  as the fund still serves a charitable purpose of the institution.
- A court may modify a restriction on management or investment if it has become impracticable or
  wasteful, impairs management of the fund, or if unanticipated circumstances mean a change would
  further the fund's purposes. This reflects the doctrine of equitable deviation.
- A court may modify a restriction on the purpose or use of a fund if it has become unlawful,
  impracticable, impossible to achieve or wasteful, in a manner consistent with the charitable
  purposes expressed in the gift instrument. This reflects the doctrine of cy pres.
- For a fund that is both small and old, under thresholds each state sets, the institution may
  itself release or modify a restriction that has become unlawful, impracticable, impossible or
  wasteful, after giving notice to the attorney general and waiting a set period.

In court proceedings the attorney general must be notified and given an opportunity to be heard.

## Who it covers

The Act applies to institutions organized and operated exclusively for charitable purposes,
including nonprofit colleges and universities, and to government bodies to the extent they hold
funds for charitable purposes. It applies to funds the institution holds for its own charitable
purposes.

## What it does not do

UPMIFA does not tell an institution whether to accept a gift or what terms to agree to. It applies
once a fund exists.

It does not override a donor's specific written instructions on spending. A gift instrument can set
its own rule. General words such as "spend only the income" or "preserve the principal" are treated
as creating an endowment of permanent duration, not as a different spending rule, unless the
instrument says more.

It does not cover funds that the governing board, and not a donor, has chosen to set aside as
endowment. Those board-designated or quasi-endowment funds can be spent at the board's discretion.

It does not, in its uniform text, give donors a right to sue to enforce their restrictions.
Enforcement has traditionally belonged to the state attorney general. Whether a donor or a donor's
heirs have standing depends on state law and on the gift agreement, and states differ.

## How it is enforced

The state attorney general, as the official responsible for protecting charitable assets, may
investigate and sue for imprudent management or for failure to honor restrictions. Courts supervise
modifications.

## Common misreadings

An endowment is often imagined as one pool the institution may use as it likes. It is usually
thousands of separate funds, many restricted to stated purposes.

"Restricted" is often taken to mean permanently fixed. Restrictions can be changed by the routes
above, but not by the institution acting alone, except for small, old funds.

## Where to read it

The uniform text and official comments are published by the Uniform Law Commission. The binding text
is the version in your state's code. An institution's audited financial statements describe its
endowment and spending policy in the notes. See `/donor-pathways/` and `/glossary/` on this site.
