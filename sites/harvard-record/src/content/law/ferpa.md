## What it does

The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act of 1974 (FERPA) does two things. It gives a right of
access to education records, and it restricts disclosure of those records.

At a college or university the rights belong to the student, whatever the student's age. The law
calls such a person an eligible student. (In elementary and secondary school the rights belong to
parents until the student turns 18.)

An eligible student may:

- inspect and review their education records, which the school must allow within 45 days of a
  request;
- ask the school to amend a record they believe is inaccurate or misleading, and have a hearing if
  the school refuses;
- control disclosure of personally identifiable information from the records, subject to the
  exceptions below.

Education records are records that are directly related to a student and maintained by the
institution or by someone acting for it. The term is broad. It covers transcripts, disciplinary
files, financial aid records, emails about a student kept by the institution, and much else.

## Who it covers

FERPA applies to educational agencies and institutions that receive funds under programs run by the
Department of Education. In practice that is nearly every college and university, public or private,
and all public school districts.

The law protects current and former students. It does not protect applicants who were never in
attendance.

## What it does not do

FERPA does not forbid all disclosure without consent. The exceptions are many. A school may disclose
without consent, among other cases:

- to school officials with a legitimate educational interest, as the school defines that in its
  annual notice;
- directory information, such as name, field of study and dates of attendance, if the school has
  given public notice and the student has not opted out;
- to another school where the student seeks to enroll;
- in connection with financial aid;
- to comply with a court order or lawfully issued subpoena, usually after trying to notify the
  student;
- to appropriate parties in a health or safety emergency;
- to the victim of an alleged crime of violence or non-forcible sex offense, the final results of
  the disciplinary proceeding about that offense;
- to anyone, the final results of such a proceeding if the institution found that the student
  violated its rules. Final results here means the student's name, the violation and the sanction.

Several kinds of record are outside the definition of education record. These include notes kept in
the sole possession of the person who made them, records of a campus law enforcement unit created by
that unit for a law enforcement purpose, employment records of employees who are not employed
because they are students, treatment records made by health professionals and used only for a
student's treatment, and records created after a person is no longer a student that do not relate to
their time as a student, such as most alumni fundraising records.

FERPA does not give a right to sue. The Supreme Court has held that the statute creates no private
right of action.

## How it is enforced

A student may file a complaint with the Department of Education's Student Privacy Policy Office,
generally within 180 days of the alleged violation or of learning of it. The office investigates
and, if it finds a violation, tells the institution what steps to take. The law aims at voluntary
compliance. The ultimate sanction is the loss of federal education funding, which applies only where
an institution has a policy or practice of violating the law and will not comply. In practice that
sanction is not used, and matters end with corrective steps.

## Common misreadings

Institutions sometimes cite FERPA as a reason they cannot tell a complainant how a disciplinary case
ended. For cases of sexual assault, dating violence, domestic violence and stalking, the Clery Act
requires that both parties be told the result, and the regulations state that doing so does not
violate FERPA.

FERPA is often cited as a reason campus police reports cannot be released. Records that a campus law
enforcement unit creates for law enforcement purposes are not education records. Whether they are
public depends on other law, such as the Clery Act's crime log requirement and state public records
law.

FERPA is sometimes thought to bar a school from releasing anything about any student. Directory
information and the other exceptions show it does not. The exceptions permit disclosure. Most do not
require it, so an institution may lawfully decline.

For a reader of institutional records, FERPA explains why published reports are often redacted or
give only totals. It limits what an institution may release. It does not show that anything is being
hidden.
