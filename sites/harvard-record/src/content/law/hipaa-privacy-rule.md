## What it does

The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) told the Department of
Health and Human Services to write national standards for the privacy of health information. The
Privacy Rule is the result. It is a regulation, not a section of the statute.

The rule protects "protected health information," which is information that identifies a person and
relates to that person's health, health care or payment for health care, when held by an
organization the rule covers.

The basic structure is simple. A covered organization may use or disclose protected health
information only as the rule permits or requires. Uses for treatment, payment and health care
operations are permitted without the patient's written authorization. A list of public-interest
disclosures is also permitted, for example those required by law, for public health reporting, or to
law enforcement under stated conditions. Most other uses, including most marketing, need the
patient's written authorization. Outside treatment, the organization must generally limit what it
uses or shares to the minimum necessary.

The rule also gives individuals rights. A person may see and get a copy of their records, ask for
corrections, receive a notice of privacy practices, and ask for an accounting of certain
disclosures.

For research, the rule offers several routes. A researcher may obtain health information with the
person's authorization, with a waiver approved by an institutional review board or privacy board, as
a limited data set under a data use agreement, or in de-identified form. Data are de-identified
either when an expert determines that the risk of identifying anyone is very small, or when 18
listed types of identifier are removed and the holder has no actual knowledge that the rest could
identify a person.

## Who it covers

The rule applies to covered entities and business associates.

Covered entities are health plans, health care clearinghouses, and health care providers that send
health information electronically in connection with standard transactions such as billing an
insurer. Business associates are outside persons or firms that handle protected health information
on a covered entity's behalf, such as billing services or cloud vendors.

A university is often a hybrid entity. It may designate its clinics, hospitals or health plan as
covered components, leaving the rest of the institution outside the rule. A research laboratory that
is not part of a covered component, and does not provide billed health care, is usually not covered
even when it holds sensitive health or genetic data. Its obligations come from research regulations
and from agreements with the sources of its data.

## What it does not do

The Privacy Rule does not cover health information in general. It covers certain holders.
Information a person gives to a fitness app, a direct-to-consumer genetic testing company, an
employer or a journalist is usually outside the rule.

It excludes education records covered by the student privacy law, FERPA, and the treatment records
of students at a campus health service that FERPA addresses. So a student's campus clinic records
are often governed by FERPA and state law, not HIPAA.

It excludes employment records that a covered entity holds in its role as employer.

It does not protect de-identified information. And it stops protecting a person's information 50
years after death.

It does not override state laws that are more protective of privacy. Many states have stricter rules
for genetic, mental health or HIV-related information.

## How it is enforced

The Office for Civil Rights in the Department of Health and Human Services investigates complaints
and conducts compliance reviews. It may require corrective action and may impose civil money
penalties, which are tiered by culpability and adjusted for inflation. State attorneys general may
also bring civil actions on behalf of state residents. The Department of Justice handles criminal
violations, which involve knowingly obtaining or disclosing identifiable health information in
violation of the law.

An individual cannot sue under HIPAA. There is no private right of action. A person may complain to
the Office for Civil Rights, and may have claims under state law.

## Common misreadings

HIPAA is often invoked as a general ban on discussing anyone's health. It binds covered entities and
their business associates. It does not bind a friend, a reporter or, in most cases, an employer.

It is often assumed that all medical research is under HIPAA. Only research that uses information
from a covered entity is, and even then the research-specific routes above apply.

It is sometimes assumed that de-identified means anonymous in every sense. De-identification under
the rule is a legal standard. Genetic data in particular can sometimes be linked back to a person by
comparison with other data sets, which is one reason data use agreements commonly forbid attempts to
re-identify.

The acronym is HIPAA, with one P.
