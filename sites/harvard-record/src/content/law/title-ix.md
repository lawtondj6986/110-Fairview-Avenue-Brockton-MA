## What it does

Title IX is a short statute. Its core sentence says that no person in the United States shall, on
the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to
discrimination under any education program or activity receiving federal financial assistance.

The statute works as a condition on funding. A school that accepts federal money promises not to
discriminate on the basis of sex. Federal financial assistance includes research grants and,
importantly, federal student aid that students bring with them. Because nearly every college and
university enrolls students who use federal aid, nearly every one is covered.

Courts and agencies have long read sex discrimination to include sexual harassment and sexual
violence, because harassment can deny a person equal access to education. The statute itself does
not use those words. The detailed duties come from regulations and court decisions. See the separate
explainer on the Title IX regulations in `/law/`.

## Who it covers

Title IX covers recipients of federal financial assistance that operate education programs or
activities. That includes public and private colleges and universities, school districts, and some
other bodies such as museums or training programs that run educational activities with federal
funds.

Under a 1988 amendment, coverage is institution-wide. If any part of a college or university
receives federal funds, all of its operations are covered, not only the department that received the
money.

The law protects students, employees, applicants and other people who take part in, or try to take
part in, the institution's programs. It protects people of any sex.

The statute has exceptions. Among them: an institution controlled by a religious organization is
exempt to the extent that applying the law would conflict with the organization's religious tenets.
The admissions provisions apply to vocational, professional and graduate schools and to public
undergraduate institutions, so the admissions policies of private undergraduate colleges are outside
the statute. Membership practices of social fraternities and sororities are also exempt.

## What it does not do

Title IX does not apply to individuals as such. The legal duty rests on the institution that
receives funds. A person accused of harassment is not a defendant under Title IX, although the
institution's own disciplinary process may apply to that person.

It is not a criminal law. It does not define crimes, and a Title IX process does not decide criminal
guilt. A campus finding and a criminal verdict use different standards and can come out differently.

It does not reach conduct with no connection to the institution's education program or activity.
Where that line falls is set by regulations and case law and has shifted over time.

It does not, by itself, require public reporting of crime statistics. That duty comes from the Clery
Act, a separate law.

## How it is enforced

There are two main routes.

The first is administrative. Each federal agency that gives money to schools can enforce Title IX
against its recipients. For most schools the lead agency is the Office for Civil Rights in the
Department of Education. Anyone may file a complaint with that office, generally within 180 days of
the alleged discrimination. The office can investigate and can also open reviews on its own. Most
cases end in a negotiated resolution agreement in which the school promises specific changes. The
formal penalty, ending federal funding, requires notice, an opportunity for a hearing and other
steps, and is very rarely used.

The second route is a private lawsuit. The statute does not mention one, but the Supreme Court has
held that individuals may sue. To win money damages for harassment, a plaintiff generally must show
that an official with authority to take corrective action had actual knowledge of the harassment and
responded with deliberate indifference. For harassment by another student, the Court has also
required that the harassment be severe, pervasive and objectively offensive. This is a demanding
standard, and it is stricter than the standard an agency may apply in its own enforcement.

Retaliation against a person who complains of sex discrimination is itself a form of discrimination
that Title IX prohibits.

## Common misreadings

Title IX is often described as a sports law. Athletics is one application. The statute covers all
aspects of an education program, including admissions where covered, housing, employment, discipline
and the response to harassment.

It is sometimes assumed that a Title IX violation means a specific person was found responsible for
misconduct. A Title IX violation is a finding about the institution's conduct, for example that it
failed to respond adequately to a report.

A resolution agreement is not necessarily an admission. Many agreements state that the institution
does not admit a violation. Read the document itself.

Private institutions are not exempt because they are private. Coverage follows federal funds.
