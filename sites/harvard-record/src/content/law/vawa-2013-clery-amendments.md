## What it does

In 2013 Congress reauthorized the Violence Against Women Act (VAWA). Section 304 of that law amended the Clery Act. The amendment is often called the Campus Sexual Violence Elimination Act, or Campus SaVE, after a bill whose provisions were folded into it. The Department of Education published implementing regulations in October 2014, and they took effect on July 1, 2015.

The amendments made three kinds of change.

First, they expanded what must be counted. Institutions must report statistics for dating violence, domestic violence and stalking, in addition to the sex offenses already on the Clery list. The amendments also added national origin and gender identity to the categories of bias used for counting hate crimes.

Second, they required prevention and education. An institution's annual security report must describe its primary prevention and awareness programs for all incoming students and new employees, and its ongoing prevention and awareness campaigns. The programs must cover, among other things, the definition of consent in the applicable jurisdiction, options for bystander intervention, and information on reducing risk.

Third, they set minimum procedural standards for institutional disciplinary proceedings in cases of dating violence, domestic violence, sexual assault and stalking. The annual security report must state that such proceedings will:

- provide a prompt, fair and impartial process from the initial investigation to the final result;
- be conducted by officials who receive annual training on these offenses and on how to conduct an investigation and hearing that protects safety and promotes accountability;
- give the accuser and the accused the same opportunity to have others present, including an advisor of their choice;
- give both parties simultaneous written notice of the result, of any appeal procedures, of any change to the result, and of when the result becomes final.

The report must also state the standard of evidence the institution uses. And a student or employee who reports one of these offenses must receive a written explanation of rights and options, whether the offense occurred on or off campus. That explanation covers preserving evidence, how and to whom to report, the choice whether to involve the police, protective orders, and available counseling, health, legal and other services, as well as options for changes to academic, living, transportation and working situations.

## Who it covers

The amendments apply to the same institutions as the Clery Act: all postsecondary institutions that take part in federal student aid programs under Title IV of the Higher Education Act, public and private. The procedural protections apply to students and employees alike, whether as accuser or accused.

## What it does not do

The amendments do not dictate the standard of evidence. They require the institution to disclose the one it uses. Other rules, such as the Title IX regulations, may limit the choice.

They do not define the offenses by reference to the institution's own code. For counting purposes, the regulations supply definitions drawn from federal law and from the FBI's crime reporting programs. The institution's policy definitions, and the criminal law of the state, may differ.

They do not require an institution to investigate every off-campus incident. The written explanation of rights must be given regardless of location, but the crime statistics still follow Clery geography.

The advisor provision does not guarantee that an advisor can speak. An institution may restrict how far an advisor participates in a proceeding, so long as the limits apply equally to both parties. It may not limit the choice of advisor.

Like the rest of the Clery Act, these provisions do not give an individual a right to sue.

## How it is enforced

Enforcement is the same as for the Clery Act generally. The Department of Education reviews compliance, issues findings and may impose a civil fine for each violation, with the maximum amount adjusted yearly for inflation. Missing or inadequate policy statements are among the findings that program reviews often report, because each required statement that is absent can be treated as a separate violation.

## Common misreadings

Campus SaVE is sometimes described as a separate statute. It is a set of amendments inside the Clery Act. A reader looking for it in the U.S. Code will find it in 20 U.S.C. § 1092(f), mainly in paragraph (8).

It is also confused with the Title IX regulations. The two overlap, and the 2020 Title IX rule borrows the Clery and VAWA definitions of sexual assault, dating violence, domestic violence and stalking. But they come from different statutes, are enforced through different offices, and impose different duties. A proceeding can satisfy one and fall short of the other.

A third confusion concerns "result." Under these provisions, the result that both parties must receive includes any sanction and the rationale for the result. The regulations state that giving this notice does not violate the federal student privacy law, FERPA.

## Where to read it

The statutory text is in 20 U.S.C. § 1092(f). The regulations are in 34 C.F.R. § 668.46, especially paragraphs (j) and (k) on prevention programs and disciplinary procedures. Paragraph lettering can change when rules are revised, so check the current text. An institution's own statements appear in its annual security report.
