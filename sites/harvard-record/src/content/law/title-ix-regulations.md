## What it does

Title IX is one sentence of principle plus exceptions. The regulations in 34 C.F.R. Part 106 say what a school must actually do. They require each recipient of federal education funds to name a Title IX coordinator, publish a nondiscrimination notice, adopt grievance procedures, and avoid sex discrimination in admissions (where covered), programs, athletics and employment.

The part that draws the most attention is the response to sexual harassment. The version of the rule that took effect in August 2020 was the first to set out that response in binding regulatory text, not only in guidance. In outline, the 2020 rule works as follows.

It defines sexual harassment as conduct on the basis of sex in one of three categories. The first is an employee conditioning an educational benefit on unwelcome sexual conduct (quid pro quo). The second is unwelcome conduct that a reasonable person would find so severe, pervasive and objectively offensive that it effectively denies a person equal access to the education program. The third is sexual assault, dating violence, domestic violence or stalking, as those terms are defined in the Clery Act and the Violence Against Women Act.

A school must respond when it has actual knowledge of such conduct in its education program or activity, against a person in the United States. For colleges, actual knowledge means notice to the Title IX coordinator or to an official with authority to institute corrective measures. The response must not be deliberately indifferent, meaning clearly unreasonable in light of the known circumstances.

The coordinator must offer the complainant supportive measures (such as schedule or housing changes) whether or not a formal complaint is filed. A formal complaint triggers a grievance process with written notice of the allegations, a presumption that the respondent is not responsible until the process ends, an investigation, an opportunity for both parties to review evidence, and a written decision. At colleges and universities the rule requires a live hearing at which each party's advisor may question the other party and witnesses. The school chooses either the preponderance of the evidence standard or the clear and convincing evidence standard and must apply the same one to complaints against students and employees. Both parties may appeal on specified grounds. Informal resolution is allowed with voluntary written consent, but not for allegations that an employee harassed a student.

## Who it covers

The regulations bind recipients of Department of Education funds: school districts, colleges and universities, and other recipients that run education programs. They apply to complaints by and against students and employees.

Under the 2020 rule, an education program or activity includes locations, events and circumstances over which the school exercised substantial control over both the respondent and the context. It also includes buildings owned or controlled by a student organization that a college officially recognizes.

## What it does not do

The 2020 rule does not reach conduct outside the United States, and it does not reach conduct outside the school's education program or activity as defined above. A school may still address such conduct under its own code of conduct. The rule sets a federal floor for one category of misconduct. It does not stop a school from prohibiting other behavior.

The rule does not require a school to report to the police, and it does not create a criminal process. It does not set a deadline in days for finishing a case. It requires reasonably prompt time frames that the school sets for itself.

## How it is enforced

The Office for Civil Rights investigates complaints and may open its own reviews. Outcomes are usually resolution agreements. Withdrawal of federal funds is the formal sanction and is rare. Courts hearing private lawsuits apply the standards set by the Supreme Court, and they may treat the regulations as relevant but not controlling.

## Common misreadings

The most important point for a reader is that the text has changed more than once. In 2024 the Department issued a revised rule that changed the definition of sex-based harassment, the scope of coverage and the required procedures. Courts blocked it in many states, and in January 2025 a federal district court vacated the 2024 rule nationwide. The Department then stated that it would enforce the 2020 rule. Separately, in 2021 a federal court vacated one provision of the 2020 rule, which had barred decision-makers from relying on statements of a person who did not submit to cross-examination.

Because of this history, a campus policy written in a given year may reflect whichever rule was in force then. A document from before August 2020 reflects earlier guidance, which worked differently. Do not assume that a procedure described in an older record matches the present rule, or the reverse.

A second misreading is that the regulation's definition of harassment limits what a school may punish. It limits what must go through the federal grievance process.

## Where to read it

Read the current text on the Electronic Code of Federal Regulations, which shows the regulation as in force today and lets you compare past versions. Key sections of the 2020 rule are § 106.30 (definitions), § 106.44 (response) and § 106.45 (grievance process). Check the Department of Education's website for current statements on which version it enforces. Further rulemaking or litigation is possible, so confirm the text before relying on any summary, including this one.
