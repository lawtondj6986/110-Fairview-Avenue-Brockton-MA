## What it does

Title IV of the Higher Education Act of 1965 authorizes the main federal student aid programs,
including Pell Grants, federal student loans and federal work-study. A college cannot receive these
funds for its students simply by being accredited. It must also sign a program participation
agreement with the Secretary of Education.

The agreement is a written contract. In it the institution promises to comply with the statute, the
regulations and a long list of specific conditions. The Department of Education in turn certifies
the institution to participate, usually for a period of years, after which the institution must
apply for recertification.

The agreement matters for a reader of institutional records because it is the legal hinge for many
duties that seem unrelated to financial aid. An institution's obligation to follow the Clery Act,
for example, is a condition of the agreement. The Department's power to fine an institution for a
campus safety failure flows from the institution's status as a Title IV participant.

Among the commitments set out in the statute and regulation are that the institution will:

- use federal aid funds only for their intended purposes and act as a fiduciary in handling them;
- maintain the administrative capability and financial responsibility the regulations require;
- have a fair and equitable refund policy and return unearned aid when a student withdraws;
- not pay commissions, bonuses or other incentive payments based on success in securing enrollments
  or financial aid to people engaged in recruiting or admissions, subject to the terms of the rule;
- comply with the campus security and crime reporting requirements of the Clery Act;
- have a drug and alcohol abuse prevention program;
- make consumer information available, such as completion and graduation rates;
- on written request, disclose to the alleged victim of a crime of violence or a non-forcible sex
  offense the results of any disciplinary proceeding the institution conducted against the student
  alleged to have committed it;

The list has grown with each reauthorization and with later rulemaking, so the current regulation
should be read for the full set.

## Who it covers

Every institution that participates in a Title IV program has signed an agreement. That includes
public, private nonprofit and for-profit institutions, from community colleges to research
universities, and it includes foreign institutions that enroll American students with federal loans.

## What it does not do

The agreement does not set academic standards. Academic quality is left to accrediting agencies, and
basic authorization to operate is left to the states. Together the Department, accreditors and
states are sometimes called the triad.

It does not give students, employees or the public the right to sue an institution for breaching it.
Courts have generally held that the Higher Education Act creates no private right of action. A
separate statute, the False Claims Act, has been used by the government and by whistleblowers where
an institution is alleged to have knowingly made false promises of compliance in order to receive
federal funds. Whether a particular promise can support such a claim is a contested and technical
question.

## How it is enforced

The Department of Education oversees compliance through required annual audits by independent
accountants, through program reviews carried out by its own staff, and through the recertification
process.

When it finds a violation, the Department has a range of responses. It can require repayment of
misspent funds. It can impose a civil fine for each violation, with the maximum adjusted annually
for inflation. It can place the institution on provisional certification or on a more restrictive
method of receiving funds, or require a letter of credit. And it can limit, suspend or terminate the
institution's participation in the Title IV programs. An institution has a right to contest a fine
or a limitation, suspension or termination before a hearing official, with an appeal to the
Secretary.

Loss of eligibility would be financially devastating for most institutions. For that reason the
threat alone is a strong incentive, and actual termination is rare outside cases of financial
collapse or fraud.

## Common misreadings

It is often said that a university could "lose its federal funding" over a single compliance
failure. The legal authority exists, but the usual outcomes are findings, corrective requirements
and fines.

The agreement is sometimes confused with the conditions on federal research grants. Research funding
comes with its own separate terms from each funding agency. An institution can be in good standing
under one and face problems under the other.

It is sometimes assumed that civil rights laws such as Title IX are enforced through this agreement.
They apply because the institution receives federal financial assistance, but they are separate
statutes with their own enforcement office and procedures.

## Where to read it

The statutory requirements are in 20 U.S.C. § 1094. The regulation is 34 C.F.R. § 668.14. The fine,
limitation, suspension and termination procedures are in Subpart G of 34 C.F.R. Part 668. The
Federal Student Aid office publishes final program review determinations and fine actions for many
institutions. See the [Clery Act explainer](/law/clery-act/) and the
[campus safety page](/campus-safety/) on this site.
