## What it does

Most organizations exempt from federal income tax must file an annual information return with the
Internal Revenue Service. For larger organizations the return is Form 990. Smaller ones may file a
shorter version, and private foundations file Form 990-PF. It is called an information return
because its main purpose is to report, not to compute tax.

A second statute makes the return public. An exempt organization must make its three most recent
annual returns, and its original application for exemption, available for public inspection, and
must provide copies on request. The Internal Revenue Service also releases the returns, and they can
be found through its online search tool and through independent websites that republish the data.

The core form covers the organization's mission and programs, revenue and expenses, assets and
liabilities, the members of its governing body, and the compensation of officers, trustees, key
employees and the highest paid employees and independent contractors. A governance section asks
whether the organization has policies on conflicts of interest, whistleblowers and document
retention.

Schedules add detail when certain conditions are met. Among those most useful for reading a
university's return:

- Schedule A: the basis for public charity status.
- Schedule B: contributors (see below for its limits).
- Schedule D: supplemental financial statements, including endowment funds, with beginning and
  ending balances, contributions, investment return and spending.
- Schedule F: activities outside the United States.
- Schedule J: detailed compensation for the highest paid people.
- Schedule L: transactions with interested persons, such as loans to or business dealings with
  officers, trustees and their families.
- Schedule R: related organizations and unrelated partnerships.

## Who it covers

The filing duty applies to most organizations exempt under section 501(a), including private
universities and their affiliated foundations, hospitals and institutes.

Some organizations do not file. Churches and certain church-related bodies are excepted.
Governmental units, which include many state universities, generally do not file, though foundations
affiliated with them do. A university system may also file through several separate legal entities,
so a reader may need more than one return to see the whole.

## What it does not do

For public charities, Form 990 does not disclose who the donors are. Schedule B lists substantial
contributors and amounts, but the law protects the names and addresses of contributors to
organizations other than private foundations and political organizations. The public copy shows
amounts with the names removed. A reader can see that a gift of a given size was received in a year.
The return does not say from whom.

It does not show gift agreements, the purposes or conditions attached to particular gifts, naming
arrangements, or pledges from named persons.

It does not list visitors, advisors, affiliates or honorary titles. It names people only in defined
roles: governing body members, officers, key and highly paid employees, top contractors and, on
Schedule L, interested persons in reportable transactions.

It does not present information by department, laboratory or program within a university. Figures
are for the legal entity as a whole.

It is not audited by the tax authority before release. It is signed under penalty of perjury, and
many organizations have it prepared or reviewed by outside accountants, but it is self-reported. It
also uses tax accounting categories and a fiscal year that may differ from the institution's audited
financial statements.

## How it is enforced

The Internal Revenue Service can impose a daily penalty on an organization that files late or files
an incomplete return without reasonable cause, and a separate penalty on responsible individuals who
fail to comply after a demand. An organization that does not file for three consecutive years loses
its exemption automatically. Penalties also apply for refusing public inspection or copies.
Substantive problems that a return reveals, such as an excess benefit transaction, are pursued under
the rules that govern those matters.

## Common misreadings

A common assumption is that a determined reader can find a university's donor list in its tax
filings. For a public charity, that is not so. The place where a donor's gift may be visible is the
donor's side. If the donor gave through a private foundation, the foundation's Form 990-PF lists its
grants and recipients, and that return is public in full. See the explainer on private foundation
rules in `/law/`.

A second assumption is that compensation figures are simple salaries. Schedule J reports several
components, including deferred compensation and benefits, and some amounts can be reported in more
than one year. Read the schedule's columns and notes.

A third is that the absence of a Schedule L entry shows that no insider dealings took place. The
schedule has thresholds and definitions. It captures specific kinds of transactions with specific
categories of people.

## Where to read it

The filing requirement is in 26 U.S.C. § 6033 and the public inspection rule is in § 6104. The form,
its schedules and their instructions are on the Internal Revenue Service website, and the
instructions are the best guide to what each line means. Returns can be found through the Service's
Tax Exempt Organization Search. See `/guides/` for a walk through a return and `/sources/` for how
this site cites filings.
