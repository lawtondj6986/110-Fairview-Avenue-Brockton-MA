## What it does

The Common Rule is the name for the Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects. It is
called common because many federal departments and agencies have adopted the same text in their own
regulations. The version used by the Department of Health and Human Services is Subpart A of 45
C.F.R. Part 46. Other subparts of Part 46 add protections for pregnant women, fetuses and newborns
(Subpart B), prisoners (Subpart C) and children (Subpart D).

The rule rests on three requirements.

First, an institution that conducts covered research must give the government a written assurance
that it will comply. For research supported by the Department of Health and Human Services this is
called a Federalwide Assurance.

Second, the research must be reviewed and approved by an institutional review board (IRB) before it
starts, and must stay under the board's oversight. An IRB has at least five members, including at
least one whose main concerns are scientific, one whose main concerns are nonscientific, and one who
is not affiliated with the institution. The board may approve research only if risks are minimized
and are reasonable in relation to expected benefits, the selection of subjects is equitable, privacy
and confidentiality are adequately protected, and consent is properly sought and documented.

Third, the investigator must obtain the informed consent of each subject, or of a legally authorized
representative, unless the IRB approves a waiver or alteration under conditions set out in the rule.
Consent must describe the purpose, procedures, foreseeable risks, benefits and alternatives, and
must make clear that taking part is voluntary.

The rule was substantially revised, with a general compliance date in January 2019. The revision
added new categories of exempt research, a concise key-information section at the start of consent
forms, an option called broad consent for storing and reusing identifiable data and biospecimens,
and a requirement that most cooperative research in the United States use a single IRB.

## Who it covers

The rule applies to research involving human subjects that is conducted or supported by a federal
department or agency that has adopted it.

Two definitions do most of the work. Research is a systematic investigation designed to develop or
contribute to generalizable knowledge. A human subject is a living individual about whom an
investigator obtains information or biospecimens through intervention or interaction, or whose
identifiable private information or identifiable biospecimens the investigator obtains, uses,
studies, analyzes or generates.

Some research involving human subjects is exempt from most of the rule's requirements. The exempt
categories include many educational studies, surveys and interviews under stated conditions, and
secondary use of existing data or specimens under stated conditions. Institutions usually decide for
themselves, through an office and not the investigator alone, whether a study is exempt.

## What it does not do

The rule does not apply by its own force to research that has no federal funding and is not
otherwise regulated. Many institutions choose, as a matter of their own policy, to hold all their
research to equivalent standards, but that is a choice, and the details differ.

It does not cover research on people who have died, or work with information or specimens that are
not identifiable to the investigator. Both follow from the definition of human subject.

It does not cover activities that are not research as defined, such as some journalism, oral
history, quality improvement and public health surveillance.

It does not govern who may fund a study, who may visit a laboratory, or how a gift to an institution
is accepted. Those are matters of institutional policy and other law.

It does not give research subjects a right to sue for violations.

## How it is enforced

The Office for Human Research Protections oversees compliance for research supported by the
Department of Health and Human Services. It can open an evaluation after a complaint or report, ask
the institution to investigate and respond, and issue a determination letter. It can require
corrective action and, in serious cases, restrict or suspend an institution's assurance, which stops
the affected federally supported research. Funding agencies can also act under the terms of a grant.

Day-to-day enforcement is local. The IRB can suspend or end approval of a study, and institutions
must report serious or continuing noncompliance and unanticipated problems to the federal office.

## Common misreadings

IRB approval is sometimes read as an endorsement of a study's scientific merit or of its funder. The
board's task is to protect subjects.

It is sometimes assumed that any use of human DNA is human-subjects research. If specimens or data
are not identifiable to the investigator and were not collected for the study through interaction,
the activity may fall outside the definition. Whether genomic data should be treated as identifiable
is a question regulators have said they will keep under review.

Exempt does not mean unregulated. Exempt studies are still subject to institutional policy, and some
exemptions require a limited IRB review of privacy protections.

The Common Rule and the Food and Drug Administration's rules are separate, and a study can be under
one, both or neither.
