## What it does

The Massachusetts Public Records Law gives any person the right to inspect and obtain copies of
public records held by state and local government bodies. The right of access is in Chapter 66 of
the General Laws. The definition of public records, and the list of exemptions, is in clause 26 of
section 7 of Chapter 4.

The law starts from a presumption that government records are public. A record may be withheld only
if a specific exemption applies, and the custodian of the record carries the burden of showing that
it does.

Each agency and municipality must name a records access officer. A request may be made in person, in
writing or by email, and the requester need not give a reason. After a major revision that took
effect in 2017, the custodian must respond within 10 business days. The response must either provide
the records or explain in writing what will be withheld and why, what fees apply, and when the
records will be produced. The law sets outer time limits for production and allows a custodian to
ask the Supervisor of Records for more time. It also limits fees.

The exemptions are listed by letter. They include records specifically exempted by another statute,
certain internal personnel rules, personnel and medical files and other material whose disclosure
would be an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy, policy deliberations still in progress, and
investigatory materials whose disclosure would prejudice effective law enforcement. Other statutes
add further protections. For example, state law restricts release of police reports of rape, sexual
assault and domestic violence, and protects the identity of victims of those crimes.

## Who it covers

The law covers records made or received by officers and employees of state agencies, executive
offices, boards, commissions and authorities, and of cities, towns, counties and districts. That
includes public colleges and universities and municipal police departments.

The Legislature and the judiciary are largely outside the law, by statutory text and court
decisions. The scope of coverage of the Governor's office has also been limited by case law and has
been a recurring subject of reform proposals.

## What it does not do

The law does not apply to private organizations. A private college or university is not a government
body, and its records are not public records under this statute. This is so even though the
institution may receive public funds, hold a state charter, or exercise powers granted by the state.
Massachusetts' highest court has addressed the point in the context of a private university's police
department and concluded that the department's records were not public records under the law as
written.

There is a narrow exception created by a different statute. Massachusetts law on police daily logs
requires each police department to keep a daily log, written in plain language, recording responses
to valid complaints, crimes reported, and the names, addresses and charges of people arrested. That
requirement extends to colleges and universities whose officers hold special state police powers
under state appointment. Those logs are to be open to the public, with exceptions written into the
statute for certain entries, such as those that would identify victims of sexual offenses or
domestic violence. This gives the public access to a campus police log at a private institution. It
does not open the department's incident reports, investigative files or internal records.

Bills to bring private campus police more fully under the public records law have been filed
repeatedly. Check whether the law has changed.

The federal Clery Act independently requires a campus police or security department to keep a daily
crime log open for public inspection. The federal and state log requirements overlap but are not the
same.

The public records law also does not require a custodian to create a record or answer questions, and
it does not apply to federal agencies.

## How it is enforced

A requester who is denied, or who receives no timely response, may petition the Supervisor of
Records in the office of the Secretary of the Commonwealth. The Supervisor issues a written
determination and may order the custodian to comply. If the custodian does not, the Supervisor may
refer the matter to the Attorney General, who may sue to enforce the order.

A requester may also go directly to Superior Court. The court may order production and, since the
2017 revision, may award attorney's fees and costs to a requester who prevails, subject to
exceptions, and may assess punitive damages in a limited range against a custodian who acted in bad
faith.

## Common misreadings

It is often assumed that a large private university in Massachusetts must answer public records
requests. It need not. Information about such an institution reaches the public through other
channels. These include filings the institution makes with government bodies (which are public
records in the hands of the government body, subject to exemptions), federal disclosures, court
records, and what the institution chooses to publish.

A related point is often missed. A record that a private university sends to a state agency or a
city, such as a filing with the Attorney General's charities division or correspondence with a
municipal licensing board, can be requested from that agency or city.

The police log exception is sometimes overstated as making campus police fully subject to the law.
It concerns the log.

The 10 business day deadline is sometimes read as a deadline for producing records. It is a deadline
for a response.
