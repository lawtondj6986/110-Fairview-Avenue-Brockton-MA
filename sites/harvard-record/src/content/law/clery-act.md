## What it does

The Clery Act is a disclosure law. It does not tell a college how safe it must be. It tells a
college what it must count, publish and announce, so that students, employees and applicants can
judge for themselves.

Its main requirements are these.

- An annual security report, published by October 1 each year. The report gives crime statistics for
  the three most recent calendar years and a set of policy statements, for example on how to report
  crimes, how the campus is secured, and how the institution handles reports of sexual assault,
  dating violence, domestic violence and stalking.
- Crime statistics for a defined list of offenses. The list includes criminal homicide, sex
  offenses, robbery, aggravated assault, burglary, motor vehicle theft and arson, plus hate crimes,
  plus arrests and disciplinary referrals for liquor, drug and weapons violations. Amendments in
  2013 added dating violence, domestic violence and stalking.
- A daily crime log, if the institution has a campus police or security department. The log must be
  open to public inspection.
- Timely warnings about listed crimes that pose a serious or continuing threat to students and
  employees.
- Emergency notification when there is an immediate threat to health or safety on campus.
- For institutions with on-campus student housing, an annual fire safety report and a procedure for
  missing student notification.

Statistics are also sent to the Department of Education, which posts them in a public online
database.

## Who it covers

The Act covers every postsecondary institution, public or private, that takes part in federal
student aid programs under Title IV of the Higher Education Act. That is nearly all accredited
colleges and universities in the United States.

Within an institution, the duty to pass along crime reports falls on campus security authorities.
This term is broader than police. It includes campus security staff and officials with significant
responsibility for student and campus activities, such as residence life staff and coaches. Pastoral
and professional counselors acting in those roles are not included.

## What it does not do

The Act counts only crimes reported to campus security authorities or local police, and only those
that occurred within Clery geography. That means on campus, in on-campus student housing (a subset
of on campus), on public property within or immediately next to campus, and in certain noncampus
buildings or property that the institution or a recognized student organization owns or controls. A
crime against a student in a private off-campus apartment is generally not counted.

The statistics do not measure how much crime occurred. They measure reports. A low number can
reflect little crime or little reporting. A rising number can reflect better reporting.

The Act does not require a conviction or even an arrest before an incident is counted. It also does
not identify victims or accused people. The annual report gives totals.

The Act does not create a right for an individual to sue. The statute says so directly.

## How it is enforced

The Department of Education enforces the Act. It conducts program reviews, sometimes after a
complaint, a news report or an audit finding, and sometimes at random. A review can end in a final
determination that lists findings. The Department may then impose a civil fine for each violation.
The maximum fine per violation is adjusted for inflation each year, so check the current figure. In
principle the Department may also limit or end an institution's participation in federal student aid
programs.

Program review reports and fine letters are public documents and are often the most detailed outside
account of how an institution handled its obligations.

## Common misreadings

A zero in a category is often read as proof that nothing happened. It means nothing in that category
was reported to the right people, in the covered places, in that year.

Clery numbers are often compared with local police statistics. The two use different geography and
different counting rules, so they rarely match.

The Clery Act and Title IX are frequently confused. Title IX is a civil rights law about an
institution's response to sex discrimination. The Clery Act is a consumer information law about
crime reporting and safety policies. One incident can raise questions under both, and each has its
own definitions.

Finally, the daily crime log and the annual statistics are different records. The log lists
incidents reported to campus police or security, including some that fall outside the Clery crime
list or outside Clery geography within the department's patrol area.

## Where to read it

The statute is 20 U.S.C. § 1092(f). The regulations are at 34 C.F.R. § 668.46, with fire safety
rules at § 668.49. Congress has amended the Act several times, most recently in late 2024 to add
requirements on hazing, so read the current text. Each institution's annual security report is on
its website. The Department of Education's campus safety data tool has the statistics for all
institutions. See the [campus safety page](/campus-safety/) for how this site uses these records, and
the [guide to reading a campus security report](/guides/read-a-campus-security-report/) for help.
