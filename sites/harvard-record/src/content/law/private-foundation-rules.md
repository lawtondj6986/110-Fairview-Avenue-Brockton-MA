## What it does

Every 501(c)(3) organization is either a public charity or a private foundation. The Code presumes
an organization is a private foundation unless it fits a public charity category, for example by
being a school, a hospital or a church, or by drawing broad public support. A typical private
foundation is funded by one person, family or company, is run by people the founder chose, and makes
grants to other charities.

Because such an organization is closely held, Congress in 1969 imposed a set of rules backed by
excise taxes. The main ones are:

- Self-dealing (section 4941). Most financial transactions between a foundation and its disqualified
  persons are prohibited.
- Minimum distributions (section 4942). A grantmaking foundation must pay out, each year, charitable
  distributions equal to roughly five percent of the value of its investment assets.
- Excess business holdings (section 4943). Limits on how much of a business the foundation and its
  insiders may own together.
- Jeopardizing investments (section 4944). A bar on investments that put the charitable purpose at
  risk.
- Taxable expenditures (section 4945). Restrictions on spending for lobbying, political campaigns,
  grants to individuals without approved procedures, and grants to organizations that are not public
  charities unless the foundation exercises expenditure responsibility.

A foundation also pays an excise tax on its net investment income (section 4940).

The self-dealing rule is the strictest. Disqualified persons include substantial contributors,
foundation managers such as officers and trustees, their family members, and entities in which these
people hold large interests. Prohibited acts include sales, leases and loans between the foundation
and a disqualified person, furnishing goods, services or facilities, paying compensation, and
transferring or using the foundation's income or assets for a disqualified person's benefit. It does
not matter that the terms were fair or even favorable to the foundation. The statute has exceptions,
the most used being reasonable compensation for personal services that are necessary to carry out
the foundation's purposes.

Private foundations file Form 990-PF each year. Unlike a public charity's return, it is public in
full. It lists the foundation's officers and trustees, its assets and investments, its substantial
contributors by name, and every grant paid during the year with the recipient and amount.

## Who it covers

The rules apply to private foundations and to their disqualified persons and managers. They do not
apply to public charities such as universities. A university is affected as a recipient of
foundation grants.

Donor-advised funds are not private foundations. They are accounts held by a sponsoring public
charity, and they are subject to a different and lighter set of rules. A sponsor's return lists the
grants the sponsor made to organizations, but it does not link a grant to the account or donor that
recommended it.

## What it does not do

The rules do not restrict which public charities a foundation may support, beyond requiring that
grants serve charitable purposes. A foundation may give to any qualifying university for any
charitable purpose it chooses.

They do not regulate what the recipient does. A university's duties in accepting and using a grant
come from its own policies, the grant terms and state law.

They do not bar a donor from receiving recognition for a foundation grant. They do bar the
foundation from buying the donor a tangible benefit. A foundation grant that pays for something of
value to a disqualified person, such as event tickets or benefits that come with a payment, can be
self-dealing. Regulations also treat a foundation's payment of a disqualified person's own legally
binding pledge as self-dealing, because it relieves that person of a debt.

## How it is enforced

Enforcement is by excise tax. For self-dealing, an initial tax is imposed on the disqualified person
who took part, as a percentage of the amount involved for each year. A smaller tax applies to a
foundation manager who knowingly participated. If the act is not corrected, meaning undone as far as
possible, a much larger second-tier tax follows. The foundation itself is not taxed for
self-dealing, since it is treated as the injured party. The other rules have similar two-tier
structures. Flagrant or repeated violations can lead to termination of the foundation's status with
a further tax. Read the current statute for rates.

State attorneys general also oversee foundations as charities under state law, and the Code requires
foundations to send their returns to the relevant state officials.

## Common misreadings

Self-dealing is often understood as any conflict of interest. In this setting it is a defined tax
term covering listed transactions with listed persons.

Form 990-PF is often overlooked as a source. For a reader tracing a gift, it is frequently the only
public document that names both the giver and the recipient. It shows the amount paid in the year
and a short purpose description. It does not show the terms.

## Where to read it

The definition is in 26 U.S.C. § 509 and the excise tax rules are in §§ 4940 to 4946. The Internal
Revenue Service publishes Form 990-PF with instructions, and returns are available through its Tax
