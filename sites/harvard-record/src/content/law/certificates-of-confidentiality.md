## What it does

A Certificate of Confidentiality is a legal protection for the privacy of people who take part in
research. It comes from a provision of the Public Health Service Act. Its purpose is to let
participants give sensitive information, including genetic information, without fear that
researchers will be forced to hand it over.

The protection was strengthened by the 21st Century Cures Act of 2016. Before that law, a
certificate allowed a researcher to refuse to disclose identifying information. Since then, a person
to whom a certificate is issued is prohibited from disclosing it, subject to listed exceptions.

The core rules are these. A person covered by a certificate may not disclose or provide the name of
a research participant, or any information, document or biospecimen containing identifiable,
sensitive information about the participant that was created or compiled for the research:

- in any federal, state or local civil, criminal, administrative, legislative or other proceeding,
  unless the participant consents; or
- to any other person not connected with the research, except in the permitted cases below.

The statute also says that the protected information is immune from the legal process and may not be
admitted as evidence or used for any purpose in any action, suit or other judicial, legislative or
administrative proceeding without the consent of the person to whom the information pertains.

The definition of identifiable, sensitive information is broad. It covers information about an
individual that is gathered or used in the course of research where the individual is identified, or
where there is at least a very small risk that some combination of the information, a request for
it, and other available data sources could be used to deduce the individual's identity. Genomic data
can fall within this definition even when names have been removed.

The protection lasts indefinitely for information collected while the certificate was in effect.

## Who it covers

For research funded by the National Institutes of Health that collects or uses identifiable,
sensitive information, a certificate is issued automatically as a term of the award. Investigators
do not apply. This has been the case for covered research since late 2016. Other agencies within the
Department of Health and Human Services have their own procedures for the research they fund.

Researchers whose studies are not federally funded may apply for a certificate, which the agency may
issue at its discretion for health-related research.

The duty binds the recipient institution and its investigators. It also follows the data. Under
agency policy, a recipient that shares protected information with another researcher or organization
must make sure the receiving party understands that it too is bound.

## What it does not do

A certificate does not prevent all disclosure. The statute permits disclosure:

- when required by federal, state or local laws, such as laws requiring reports of child abuse or
  communicable disease, but not in the proceedings described above;
- when necessary for the medical treatment of the participant, with the participant's consent;
- with the consent of the participant;
- for other scientific research that complies with applicable federal regulations on the protection
  of human subjects.

A certificate does not stop a participant from sharing their own information, and it does not
prevent a participant from consenting to release, for example to an insurer or employer. Consent
forms are expected to explain this.

It does not protect information that was not collected for the research. Records that exist
independently, such as a person's ordinary medical records held by a clinic, are not shielded merely
because the person joined a study.

It does not prevent the funding agency from auditing or evaluating the project.

It does not substitute for data security. A certificate is a legal bar on disclosure by the people
it binds. It does nothing against a breach or against re-identification by someone who obtains the
data from another source.

It does not cover research that has no certificate. Privately funded studies whose investigators did
not apply for one are outside the protection.

It is not a guarantee tested in every setting. Court decisions applying certificates are relatively
few. They have generally respected the protection, but the reach of some exceptions has had limited
judicial examination.

## How it is enforced

There are two sides to enforcement.

Against outside demands, the certificate is asserted by the researcher or institution. When a
subpoena or court order seeks protected information, the institution is expected to invoke the
certificate and resist, typically through its lawyers and with notice to the funding agency.

Against the researchers themselves, compliance is a term and condition of the federal award. An
institution that discloses protected information in violation of the statute is out of compliance
with its award, and the funding agency can take the enforcement actions available under grant rules.
The statute does not set out a specific penalty schedule or a private right of action for
participants.

## Common misreadings

A certificate is sometimes thought to make research data secret from everyone. It permits sharing
for other compliant research, which is how large genomic data repositories operate.

It is sometimes thought to be the same as HIPAA. HIPAA governs health care organizations and allows
a number of disclosures, including some to law enforcement and in litigation. A certificate, where
it applies, is stricter on those points and binds researchers whether or not they are covered by
HIPAA.

It is sometimes thought to block mandatory reporting. Reports required by law, such as those for
suspected child abuse, are permitted.

It is sometimes assumed that every study has one. Coverage depends on the funder and on whether the
investigators applied.

## Where to read it

The statute is subsection (d) of 42 U.S.C. § 241, which is section 301(d) of the Public Health
Service Act. The National Institutes of Health publishes its policy and answers to common questions.
A study's consent form should say whether a certificate applies and describe its limits. See the
Common Rule explainer in `/law/` and `/dna-genetics/` on this site.
