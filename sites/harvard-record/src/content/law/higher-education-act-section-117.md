## What it does

Section 117 of the Higher Education Act of 1965 is a disclosure law about foreign money. It requires
a covered institution to file a report with the Secretary of Education when it receives a gift from,
or enters into a contract with, a foreign source, and the value is $250,000 or more. The threshold
is met by a single gift or contract, or by several from the same source that together reach that
amount within a calendar year.

Reports are due twice a year, on January 31 and July 31, covering the preceding period.

The report must give the total dollar amount attributable to each country. For a foreign government,
it must give the amount and the name of the country. Where a gift or contract is restricted or
conditional, more detail is required, including the amount, the date and a description of the
conditions or restrictions. A restricted or conditional gift or contract, in the statute's terms, is
one that includes provisions about matters such as the employment or selection of faculty, the
establishment of departments or programs, the admission of students, or awards based on nationality.

A separate provision requires an institution that is owned or controlled by a foreign source to
report that fact.

The statute says the reports are public records, open to inspection. The Department of Education
publishes the reported data online.

## Who it covers

The law applies to institutions of higher education that receive federal financial assistance,
including institutions whose students receive federal student aid. Both public and private
institutions are covered.

A foreign source includes a foreign government and its agencies, a legal entity created solely under
the laws of a foreign country, an individual who is not a citizen or national of the United States,
and an agent acting on behalf of any of these.

How far reporting extends to affiliated foundations and other related entities that receive money
for the benefit of an institution has been a subject of agency interpretation. Read the current
instructions for the reporting system.

## What it does not do

Section 117 does not prohibit foreign gifts or contracts. It requires that they be reported.

It does not apply to domestic donors. A gift from a United States citizen or a domestic entity, of
any size, is not reportable under this section. That is a significant limit for anyone trying to
trace donor influence generally. A gift routed through a domestic foundation or company may not
appear, depending on the facts and on whether the domestic body is acting as an agent of a foreign
source.

It does not cover gifts below the threshold from a single source in a year.

It does not, in the statute's basic terms, require the public naming of every individual donor. The
statute keys the required content to country and, for restricted gifts, to the conditions. What the
Department's reporting system asks for, and what it publishes, has changed over time, so check the
current instructions and the current public data set before drawing conclusions from an absence.

It does not regulate how a gift is used once received. Other laws, including export controls,
research security requirements attached to federal grants, and sanctions rules, may bear on
particular relationships with foreign sources. Some federal research agencies also impose their own
foreign support disclosure rules on grant applicants. Those are separate from Section 117.

## How it is enforced

The Department of Education administers the reporting system and may open a compliance investigation
of an institution. If an institution fails to comply, the statute allows a civil action to be
brought in federal court, at the request of the Secretary of Education, by the Attorney General to
compel compliance. Where the failure is knowing or willful, the institution must pay the
government's full costs of obtaining compliance, including the costs of investigation and
enforcement.

For many years the provision drew little enforcement attention. Beginning in 2019 the Department
opened a series of investigations, and reported totals rose sharply as institutions filed late or
amended reports. Enforcement priorities have shifted with changes of administration.

## Common misreadings

The public data are sometimes read as a complete ledger of foreign money at a university. They are a
record of what institutions reported, under the rules and systems in place at the time. Gaps can
reflect the threshold, the definition of foreign source, late filing, or changes in the reporting
form.

A reported contract is sometimes read as a gift. The data include contracts, such as tuition
agreements with foreign governments for sponsored students, research agreements and licensing deals.
These are exchanges, not donations.

A report is sometimes read as evidence of wrongdoing. Reporting is what the law requires. A failure
to report is the potential violation.
