## What it does

A trademark is a word, name, symbol or device that identifies the source of goods or services and distinguishes them from those of others. Universities own trademarks in their names, seals, logos and often team names and colors. The main federal trademark statute is the Lanham Act. States also protect marks under their own statutes and common law.

Trademark law has a specific purpose. It protects the public from being misled about who makes or stands behind something, and it protects the owner's investment in its reputation. It is not a general right to control every use of a word.

The central claim is infringement. An owner must show that it has a valid mark and that the defendant's use of the same or a similar mark, in connection with goods or services, is likely to cause confusion. The confusion may be about the source of the goods or services, or about whether the owner sponsors, endorses or is affiliated with the defendant. Courts weigh a list of factors that varies somewhat among the federal circuits. Common factors are the strength of the mark, the similarity of the marks, how closely related the goods or services are, evidence of actual confusion, the defendant's intent, and how careful the relevant audience is likely to be.

A second claim, dilution, is available only to owners of famous marks. It addresses uses that blur a mark's distinctiveness or tarnish its reputation even without confusion. The dilution statute expressly excludes several kinds of use. These are fair use, including use in identifying, criticizing, commenting on or parodying the famous mark's owner or its goods or services. They also include all forms of news reporting and news commentary, and any noncommercial use of a mark.

Nominative fair use is the doctrine most relevant to someone writing about an institution. It applies when a person uses another's mark to refer to the mark owner or its products themselves, because there is no other practical way to do so. A critic cannot review a book without naming it, and a reporter cannot cover an organization without using its name. Courts that apply the doctrine commonly ask three questions:

1. Is the owner or its product not readily identifiable without using the mark?
2. Did the user take only as much of the mark as is reasonably necessary to identify the owner or product?
3. Did the user do nothing that would, in conjunction with the mark, suggest sponsorship or endorsement by the owner?

## Who it covers

Trademark law applies to anyone who uses a mark in commerce in connection with goods or services. Whether a noncommercial website, publication or research project uses a mark in the sense the statute requires is itself sometimes disputed, and courts have not been uniform.

Independent publications, commentators, competitors, resellers and repair services all rely on nominative use. Mark owners include companies, universities, charities and individuals.

## What it does not do

Trademark law does not prevent people from using an institution's name in text to discuss, report on, criticize or praise it. Plain-word reference of this kind is the core of nominative use, and the First Amendment also protects it.

It does not give an owner the power to stop unflattering commentary. A statement's truth or falsity is a matter for defamation law, not trademark law.

It does not protect a user who goes beyond what is needed. Using an institution's seal, logo, distinctive typeface or color scheme is harder to justify than using its name in plain text, because a logo is rarely necessary to identify the institution. Design choices that make a site look official weigh against the user.

It does not make a disclaimer a complete answer. A clear and prominent statement that a site is independent and not affiliated with the institution helps to dispel confusion, and courts take it into account. A disclaimer will not rescue a site whose overall presentation still suggests affiliation.

Domain names raise separate questions. A federal statute on cybersquatting provides a claim against a person who registers or uses a domain name that is identical or confusingly similar to a distinctive mark with a bad faith intent to profit from the mark. The statute lists factors bearing on bad faith, including whether the use is a bona fide noncommercial or fair use. Domain disputes can also be brought under a private arbitration policy that domain registrars apply. Outcomes for sites that use an institution's name in a domain for commentary or criticism have varied with the facts and the forum.

## How it is enforced

There is no agency that polices trademark use in the marketplace. The Patent and Trademark Office registers marks but does not enforce them. Owners enforce their rights themselves, usually first through a demand letter and then, if they choose, a civil suit. Remedies include injunctions, the defendant's profits, damages and, in exceptional cases, attorney's fees.

How nominative fair use fits into a case depends on the circuit. Some courts use the three-part test in place of the usual confusion factors. Some treat it as an affirmative defense to be raised after confusion is shown. Others fold its considerations into the ordinary likelihood of confusion analysis. The practical questions are much the same everywhere, but the procedure and burden of proof differ.

## Common misreadings

The registration symbol next to a name does not mean others may not write the name. It means the mark is registered for particular goods or services.

Trademark and copyright are different. A name is not protected by copyright. A logo may be protected by both, which is a further reason an independent site avoids reproducing logos.

"Fair use" in trademark law is not the same as fair use in copyright law. The tests and purposes differ.

A demand letter is not a ruling. It states the sender's position.

Classic, or descriptive, fair use is a different doctrine. It protects using a descriptive word in its ordinary meaning, not as a reference to the mark owner.

## Where to read it

The Lanham Act is at 15 U.S.C. § 1051 and following. Infringement of registered marks is in § 1114. Unregistered marks, dilution and cybersquatting are in § 1125. Statutory defenses are in § 1115. Nominative fair use is found mainly in court decisions, so the law of the relevant federal circuit must be consulted.
