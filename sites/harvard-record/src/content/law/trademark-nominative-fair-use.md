## What it does

A trademark is a word, name, symbol or device that identifies the source of goods or services and
distinguishes them from those of others. Universities own trademarks in their names, seals, logos
and often team names and colors. The main federal trademark statute is the Lanham Act. States also
protect marks under their own statutes and common law.

Trademark law has a specific purpose. It protects the public from being misled about who makes or
stands behind something, and it protects the owner's investment in its reputation. It is not a
general right to control every use of a word.

The central claim is infringement. An owner must show that it has a valid mark and that the
defendant's use of the same or a similar mark, in connection with goods or services, is likely to
cause confusion. The confusion may be about the source of the goods or services, or about whether
the owner sponsors, endorses or is affiliated with the defendant. Courts weigh a list of factors
that varies somewhat among the federal circuits. Common factors are the strength of the mark, the
similarity of the marks, how closely related the goods or services are, evidence of actual
confusion, the defendant's intent, and how careful the relevant audience is likely to be.

Nominative fair use is the doctrine most relevant to someone writing about an institution. It
applies when a person uses another's mark to refer to the mark owner or its products themselves,
because there is no other practical way to do so. A critic cannot review a book without naming it,
and a reporter cannot cover an organization without using its name. Courts that apply the doctrine
commonly look at three things:

1. Whether the owner or its product is readily identifiable without using the mark.
2. Whether the user took only as much of the mark as is reasonably necessary to identify the owner
   or product.
3. Whether the user did anything that would, in conjunction with the mark, suggest sponsorship or
   endorsement by the owner.

## Who it covers

Trademark law applies to anyone who uses a mark in commerce in connection with goods or services.
Whether a noncommercial website, publication or research project uses a mark in the sense the
statute requires is itself sometimes disputed, and courts have not been uniform.

## What it does not do

Trademark law does not prevent people from using an institution's name in text to discuss, report
on, criticize or praise it. Plain-word reference of this kind is the core of nominative use, and the
First Amendment also protects it.

It does not give an owner the power to stop unflattering commentary. A statement's truth or falsity
is a matter for defamation law, not trademark law.

It does not protect a user who goes beyond what is needed. Using an institution's seal, logo,
distinctive typeface or color scheme is harder to justify than using its name in plain text, because
a logo is rarely necessary to identify the institution. Design choices that make a site look
official weigh against the user.

It does not make a disclaimer a complete answer. A clear and prominent statement that a site is
independent and not affiliated with the institution helps to dispel confusion, and courts take it
into account. A disclaimer will not rescue a site whose overall presentation still suggests
affiliation.

Domain names raise separate questions. A federal statute on cybersquatting provides a claim against
a person who registers or uses a domain name that is identical or confusingly similar to a
distinctive mark with a bad faith intent to profit from the mark. The statute lists factors bearing
on bad faith, including whether the use is a bona fide noncommercial or fair use. Domain disputes
can also be brought under a private arbitration policy that domain registrars apply. Outcomes for
sites that use an institution's name in a domain for commentary or criticism have varied with the
facts and the forum.

## How it is enforced

There is no agency that polices trademark use in the marketplace. The Patent and Trademark Office
registers marks but does not enforce them. Owners enforce their rights themselves, usually first
through a demand letter and then, if they choose, a civil suit. Remedies include injunctions, the
defendant's profits, damages and, in exceptional cases, attorney's fees. Federal circuits differ on
how nominative fair use fits into a case and on who bears the burden of proof.

## Common misreadings

The registration symbol next to a name does not mean others may not write the name. It means the
mark is registered for particular goods or services.

## Where to read it

The Lanham Act is at 15 U.S.C. § 1051 and following. Infringement of registered marks is in § 1114.
Unregistered marks and cybersquatting are addressed in § 1125, which also covers dilution of famous
marks. Statutory defenses are in § 1115. Nominative fair use is found mainly in court decisions, so
the law of the relevant federal circuit must be consulted. This explainer describes general
principles and is not advice on any particular use. See `/methodology/` for this site's statement of
independence.
