## What it does

A charity has no owners. Its assets are held for public purposes and for the benefit of an
indefinite class of people, none of whom is in a position to hold its managers to account. To fill
that gap, the law of nearly every state gives a public official the power to represent the public's
interest in charitable assets. In most states that official is the attorney general. The power comes
from long-standing common law, and most states have added statutes.

The oversight has several parts.

Registration and reporting. Many states require charities that hold assets in the state or solicit
donations from its residents to register and to file annual reports. The report commonly includes a
copy of the federal Form 990 and, above a revenue threshold, audited or reviewed financial
statements. These filings are generally open to the public. In Massachusetts, for example, public
charities file an annual report with a division of the Attorney General's office.

Solicitation regulation. State laws govern fundraising appeals. They prohibit deceptive solicitation
and often require professional fundraisers and fundraising counsel to register, to file their
contracts and to report the results of their campaigns.

Fiduciary oversight. The attorney general may investigate whether directors and officers have
breached their duties of care and loyalty, for example through self-dealing, waste or excessive
compensation. The attorney general may sue to recover assets, remove fiduciaries or obtain other
relief.

Restricted gifts and changes of purpose. The attorney general is the traditional enforcer of the
terms of charitable gifts. When a charity asks a court to modify a restriction, under the doctrines
of cy pres or deviation or under the state's version of the Uniform Prudent Management of
Institutional Funds Act, the attorney general is a necessary party or must be notified.

Fundamental transactions. Many states require notice to, or approval by, the attorney general or a
court before a charity sells substantially all its assets, merges, converts to a for-profit entity
or dissolves. Some states have specific rules for hospitals and, more recently, for colleges in
financial distress.

## Who it covers

The authority extends to charitable corporations, charitable trusts and unincorporated charitable
associations that are organized in the state, hold assets there or solicit there. Private nonprofit
colleges and universities are within it. So are private foundations.

Many states exempt some classes of organization from registration and reporting. Religious
organizations are commonly exempt. Some states exempt educational institutions, or hospitals, or
very small charities. An exemption from filing does not remove the attorney general's underlying
authority over charitable assets.

## What it does not do

The attorney general does not run charities and does not review their ordinary decisions. Under the
business judgment approach that most states apply, a board's good faith, informed decision is not
second-guessed merely because it turned out badly or is unpopular.

The attorney general does not approve or screen gifts. Whether to accept a donation, from whom, and
what recognition to give, are board and management decisions. Oversight comes into play if a
decision involves a breach of fiduciary duty, a misuse of restricted funds or deception of donors.

Its authority does not create standing for the public. In most states members of the public, and
often donors themselves, cannot sue to enforce a charity's obligations. Courts recognize exceptions,
for instance for persons with a special interest, and a few states have expanded donor standing by
statute or decision. This varies considerably.

## How it is enforced

The tools are civil. An attorney general may issue investigative demands, negotiate a settlement or
assurance of compliance, or sue in state court. Courts can order restitution, impose a constructive
trust on misapplied funds, remove or bar directors and officers, appoint a receiver, enjoin
deceptive solicitation, and in extreme cases dissolve the organization. Failure to register or file
can lead to penalties and loss of the right to solicit. Conduct such as embezzlement may be
prosecuted under criminal law.

State oversight runs alongside federal tax oversight by the Internal Revenue Service. The two are
independent. The tax authority asks whether the organization meets the conditions for exemption. The
state asks whether charitable assets are being used for their purposes and whether fiduciaries are
doing their duty. Federal law permits the tax authority to share certain information with state
charity officials.

## Common misreadings

It is often assumed that the Internal Revenue Service is the only regulator of charities. For
questions about gift restrictions, fiduciary duty and fundraising practices, the state attorney
general is usually the more relevant authority.

It is often assumed that a donor who is unhappy with how a gift was used can simply sue. In many
states the donor's remedy is to bring the matter to the attorney general, unless the gift agreement
or state law provides otherwise.

## Where to read it

Each state attorney general's website has a charities section with the registration rules, forms and
usually a searchable database of filings. The state's nonprofit corporation act, charitable trust
statutes and solicitation act contain the governing text. For Massachusetts, see the General Laws
provisions on public charities in Chapter 12 and on solicitation in Chapter 68, and confirm current
text. See also the [UPMIFA](/law/upmifa/) and [Form 990](/law/form-990/)
explainers.
