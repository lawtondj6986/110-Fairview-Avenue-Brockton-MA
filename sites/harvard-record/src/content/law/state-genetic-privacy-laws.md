## What it does

Federal law protects genetic information in three main settings. The Genetic Information
Nondiscrimination Act covers health insurance and employment. The HIPAA Privacy Rule covers health
plans, most health care providers and their contractors. Research rules cover federally funded and
regulated studies. Much is left outside. State laws address some of what remains, and they do so
unevenly.

State genetic laws fall into several broad types. A given state may have several, one or none.

- Insurance laws beyond health coverage. Some states limit how life, disability income or long-term
  care insurers may use genetic information. The limits range from a requirement of informed consent
  for testing, through a rule that any use must be actuarially justified, to a prohibition on using
  genetic test results in underwriting at all. A small number of states have broad prohibitions.
  Many have narrow rules or none.
- Employment laws. Many states have their own bans on genetic discrimination at work. Some cover
  employers too small to fall under federal law.
- General genetic privacy statutes. Some states require consent before a person's genetic test is
  performed, or before results are disclosed to third parties, with listed exceptions such as law
  enforcement, paternity proceedings and newborn screening. A few declare genetic information or DNA
  samples to be the property of the individual.
- Consumer genetic testing laws. Beginning around 2021, a growing number of states passed laws aimed
  at direct-to-consumer genetic testing companies. These laws share a common outline, described
  below.
- Comprehensive consumer privacy laws. A number of states have general data privacy statutes that
  classify genetic data as sensitive and require consent or an opt-out for its processing.

The consumer genetic testing laws typically require a company to publish a clear privacy policy, to
obtain express consent before collecting and using genetic data, and to obtain separate express
consent before transferring the data to a third party, using it for research, or using it for
marketing. They typically give the consumer rights to access and delete their data and to have their
biological sample destroyed. They typically bar the company from disclosing a consumer's genetic
data to insurers or employers without consent. And they typically require reasonable security.

## Who it covers

Coverage depends on the type of law. Insurance provisions bind insurers licensed in the state.
Employment provisions bind employers in the state. Consumer genetic testing laws bind companies that
offer genetic testing products or services directly to consumers in the state, or that collect
genetic data from such products.

The consumer laws commonly carve out entities and data already regulated elsewhere. Typical
exclusions cover protected health information held by organizations subject to HIPAA, and data
collected in research conducted under the federal human-subjects rules. A university laboratory
doing regulated research is therefore often outside these particular statutes, while still bound by
research rules and by any broader state law on genetic testing consent.

## What it does not do

State law does not provide uniform protection. A person's rights can change when they move, and a
company or study operating nationally faces different rules in different states.

Most states do not bar life, disability or long-term care insurers from asking applicants about
genetic test results they already have, or from considering family medical history. This is the gap
most often noted by genetic counselors. A person considering predictive testing may want to
understand it beforehand. What a specific state allows must be checked in that state's insurance
code.

Most state laws do not reach data that have been de-identified, and definitions of de-identified
differ.

## How it is enforced

Enforcement varies as much as the substance. Insurance provisions are enforced by the state
insurance regulator. Employment provisions are usually enforced through the state civil rights
agency and the courts. Consumer genetic testing laws are most often enforced by the state attorney
general, with civil penalties for each violation. A few state genetic privacy statutes give
individuals a right to sue and to recover a set amount of damages for each violation, which has led
to significant litigation in those states. Most do not.

## Common misreadings

It is often said that genetic discrimination is illegal in the United States. Federal law prohibits
it in health insurance and employment. Elsewhere the answer depends on the state and the setting.

It is often assumed that a testing company's promises are the same as legal rights. A privacy policy
is a company's statement and can change. A statute is binding. Where both exist, read both.

It is often assumed that these laws regulate university research. Most consumer genetic privacy
statutes exclude research conducted under federal human-subjects rules.

## Where to read it

There is no single text. Start with your state legislature's website and search the insurance code,
the labor or civil rights code, and the consumer protection or health code for "genetic." State
attorneys general and insurance departments often publish summaries. The National Human Genome
Research Institute has maintained a public database of state genome statutes. Because this area
changes every legislative session, treat any summary, including this one, as a starting point and
confirm against current law. See `/dna-genetics/` on this site.
