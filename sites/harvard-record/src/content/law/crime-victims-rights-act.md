## What it does

The Crime Victims' Rights Act, passed in 2004, lists rights that a victim of a federal offense holds in the criminal justice process. Earlier federal law had described similar interests but gave victims no effective way to assert them. This Act lets victims go to court.

The rights listed in the statute are:

- to be reasonably protected from the accused;
- to reasonable, accurate and timely notice of any public court proceeding, or any parole proceeding, involving the crime, and of any release or escape of the accused;
- not to be excluded from any such public court proceeding, unless the court finds by clear and convincing evidence that the victim's testimony would be materially altered by hearing other testimony;
- to be reasonably heard at any public proceeding in the district court involving release, plea, sentencing or parole;
- the reasonable right to confer with the attorney for the government in the case;
- to full and timely restitution as provided in law;
- to proceedings free from unreasonable delay;
- to be treated with fairness and with respect for the victim's dignity and privacy;
- to be informed in a timely manner of any plea bargain or deferred prosecution agreement;
- to be informed of these rights and of related services.

The last two were added by amendment in 2015.

The Act puts duties on two sets of actors. Courts must ensure that victims are afforded these rights. Officers and employees of the Department of Justice and other federal agencies engaged in detecting, investigating or prosecuting crime must make their best efforts to see that victims are notified of, and accorded, the rights.

## Who it covers

A crime victim under the Act is a person directly and proximately harmed as a result of the commission of a federal offense, or an offense in the District of Columbia. If the victim is under 18, incompetent, incapacitated or deceased, a guardian, family member or other representative may assert the rights. A person accused of the crime may not act as such a representative.

The Act applies in federal criminal cases. It also applies, in modified form, to certain federal habeas corpus proceedings arising from state convictions.

## What it does not do

The Act does not apply to state prosecutions. Most crimes are prosecuted by states, and victims in those cases look to state constitutions and statutes. Every state has some form of victims' rights law, and they differ.

It does not make the victim a party to the criminal case. The prosecution is still brought by the government against the defendant.

It does not give victims control over charging or plea decisions. The statute says that nothing in it shall be construed to impair the prosecutorial discretion of the Attorney General or officers under the Attorney General's direction. The right is to confer and to be informed and heard, not to veto.

It does not create a cause of action for damages. A victim cannot sue the United States or its officers for money for a breach of these rights. The statute says so expressly.

It does not allow a new trial on the ground that a right was denied. A victim may move to reopen a plea or sentence only under narrow conditions set out in the statute, including that the right was asserted before or during the proceeding and that the defendant did not plead to the highest offense charged.

One question has been heavily contested: whether the rights, particularly the right to confer and the right to be treated with fairness, apply before any charges have been filed, for example while prosecutors negotiate an agreement not to prosecute. Courts have reached different conclusions, and the 2015 amendment on deferred prosecution agreements addressed part of the problem. Whether a victim can start a freestanding court action to enforce the Act when no criminal case was ever filed has also been disputed. Do not treat this point as settled.

## How it is enforced

A victim, the victim's lawful representative, or the government's attorney may assert the rights by motion in the district court where the defendant is being prosecuted or, if no prosecution is under way, in the district court where the crime occurred. The court must take up and decide the motion promptly.

If the district court denies relief, the victim may petition the court of appeals for a writ of mandamus, which is an order directing the lower court to act. The court of appeals must decide the petition within 72 hours, subject to limited extension, and since 2015 must apply ordinary standards of appellate review.

The Act also required the Department of Justice to set up an administrative process. A victim may file a complaint with the Department against an employee who failed to respect these rights, and the Department may require training or impose discipline. The Department's decision on such a complaint is not subject to judicial review.

## Common misreadings

The Act is sometimes read as a guarantee of a particular outcome. It guarantees participation and information.

It is sometimes thought to apply to campus disciplinary proceedings. It applies to the federal criminal process. Rights in campus proceedings come from Title IX rules, the Clery Act and institutional policy.

It is sometimes thought to entitle a victim to see the prosecution's file. It contains no general right of access to investigative materials.

It is sometimes confused with victim compensation programs. Those are funded and administered under other laws.
