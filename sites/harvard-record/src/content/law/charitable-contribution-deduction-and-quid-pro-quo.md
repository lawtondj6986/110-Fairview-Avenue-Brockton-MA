## What it does

Section 170 of the Internal Revenue Code allows a taxpayer who itemizes to deduct contributions made
to qualifying organizations, which include universities and other 501(c)(3) charities. The deduction
is subject to percentage limits tied to the donor's income, with a carryover of the excess to later
years. The limits differ for cash and for appreciated property, and for gifts to public charities
and to private foundations. They have been changed by Congress several times, so read the current
text for the figures.

The central idea is that a contribution is a gift: a transfer made without receiving full value in
return. From that idea come three practical rules.

The first is the return benefit rule. If a donor receives goods or services in exchange for a
payment, the deduction is limited to the amount by which the payment exceeds the fair market value
of what was received. A payment of this mixed kind is called a quid pro quo contribution. Token
items of small value, and certain intangible religious benefits, are disregarded.

The second is substantiation. For any single contribution of $250 or more, the donor may not take a
deduction without a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the organization. The acknowledgment
must state the amount of cash or describe the property given, and must say whether the organization
provided any goods or services in return and, if so, give a description and good faith estimate of
their value. Gifts of property carry further requirements. A donor claiming more than $500 in
noncash gifts files a separate form with the return, and a claimed value over $5,000 for most kinds
of property requires a qualified appraisal.

The third is the organization's own disclosure duty under section 6115. When a charity receives a
quid pro quo contribution of more than $75, it must give the donor a written statement. The
statement must say that the deductible amount is limited to the excess of the payment over the value
of the goods or services provided, and must give a good faith estimate of that value.

## Who it covers

Section 170 applies to donors: individuals, corporations, and trusts and estates under related
provisions. Section 6115 applies to the recipient organization.

Donors who give through an intermediary, such as a private foundation or a donor-advised fund,
usually take the deduction when they fund the intermediary, not when the intermediary later makes a
grant to a university. Separate rules restrict the benefits a donor may receive in connection with
grants from those vehicles.

## What it does not do

These rules do not decide whether a charity should accept a gift, or on what terms. They concern the
donor's tax return and the paperwork between donor and charity.

They do not make gift terms public. Acknowledgment letters and quid pro quo statements go to the
donor, not to the public or, routinely, to the tax authority.

They do not treat every form of recognition as a return benefit. Public acknowledgment of a gift,
including putting a donor's name on a building or a professorship, has generally not been treated as
a benefit that reduces the deduction. Tangible benefits are different. Meals, event tickets, use of
facilities and similar items have a market value that must be subtracted.

They do not allow a deduction for a payment earmarked for a particular individual. A gift must be to
the organization, which must have control over the funds. A payment to a university that is really
tuition for a named student, or support for a named person chosen by the donor, is not a deductible
gift.

## How it is enforced

The Internal Revenue Service enforces section 170 on the donor's side by examining returns and
disallowing deductions that are overstated or not properly substantiated. Courts have applied the
substantiation rules strictly. A deduction can be lost entirely for want of a proper acknowledgment,
even where the gift was real. Penalties apply to substantial overvaluations of donated property.

On the organization's side, a charity that fails to give the required quid pro quo statement is
subject to a penalty for each contribution, with a cap for each fundraising event or mailing, unless
the failure was due to reasonable cause. An organization that knowingly gives a false acknowledgment
can face penalties for aiding an understatement of tax.

## Common misreadings

It is often assumed that a charity "approves" a donor's deduction. The charity states what it
received and what it gave back. The donor claims the deduction and bears the burden of supporting
it.

It is often assumed that a donor who receives access, introductions or influence has received a
return benefit that the tax law measures. The tax rules look for goods or services with an
ascertainable market value. Informal access is rarely valued or disclosed, which is one reason tax
records say little about it.
