## What it does

Before 1980, the question of who owned an invention made with federal research money was answered
differently by different agencies. Often the government kept title. The Bayh-Dole Act, named for its
Senate sponsors, replaced that patchwork with one policy for nonprofit organizations and small
businesses. The policy was later extended to larger contractors by executive action.

Under the Act, a university that makes an invention in the course of federally funded work (a
"subject invention") may elect to retain title to it. The university can then patent the invention
and license it to companies. The stated purpose is to promote the use of inventions arising from
federal support and to encourage collaboration between research institutions and industry.

In exchange, the university takes on duties, which are written into every funding agreement through
standard clauses. The main ones are:

- to disclose each subject invention to the funding agency within a set time after the inventor
  reports it;
- to elect in writing whether to retain title, within a set period;
- to file a patent application within a set period, and to state in the patent that the invention
  was made with government support and that the government has certain rights;
- to grant the government a nonexclusive, nontransferable, irrevocable, paid-up license to practice
  the invention, or have it practiced, for or on behalf of the United States;
- to report on efforts to put the invention to use;
- to give preference to small businesses in licensing, where feasible;
- in exclusive licenses to use or sell in the United States, to require that products be
  manufactured substantially in the United States, unless the agency grants a waiver.

Nonprofit organizations have further obligations. They must share royalties with the inventor. They
must use the remaining income, after expenses of administering inventions, to support scientific
research or education. And they may not assign their rights to a subject invention without agency
approval, except to an organization whose main function is managing inventions.

## Who it covers

The Act covers nonprofit organizations, including universities, and small business firms that are
parties to funding agreements with federal agencies. A funding agreement is a contract, grant or
cooperative agreement for experimental, developmental or research work funded in whole or in part by
the federal government.

It reaches inventions conceived or first actually reduced to practice in the performance of work
under such an agreement. Partial federal funding is enough.

## What it does not do

The Act does not, by its own force, take ownership from the inventor and give it to the university.
The Supreme Court has held that the Act does not automatically vest title in the federal contractor.
Under ordinary patent law, rights begin with the inventor. A university owns an employee's invention
because the employee has assigned it, usually under an employment agreement or institutional patent
policy. The Act then lets the university keep, as against the government, what it has obtained from
its inventors.

It does not apply to inventions made without federal funds. Ownership of inventions arising from
industry-sponsored or donor-funded research depends on institutional policy and on the terms of the
sponsorship or gift.

It does not cover copyrights, data, or biological materials as such, though funding agreements and
agency policies address those separately.

It does not regulate the price of products based on federally funded inventions. Whether the
government's reserved rights could be used in response to high prices has been debated for decades.
Agencies have, up to now, declined petitions to use the rights in that way. Check current agency
guidance.

It does not require a university to patent anything. If the university does not elect title, the
agency may take it, or may allow the inventor to keep rights.

## How it is enforced

Enforcement is through the funding agency under the terms of the funding agreement.

If a university fails to disclose an invention, elect title, or file for a patent within the
required times, the agency may demand that title be transferred to the government.

The agency also holds "march-in" rights. In limited circumstances listed in the statute, it may
require the university or its exclusive licensee to grant a license to another applicant, or may
grant the license itself. The circumstances include a failure to take effective steps to achieve
practical application of the invention, and a need to alleviate health or safety needs that are not
being reasonably satisfied. March-in involves a formal procedure with a right of appeal. It is
understood never to have been exercised.

Members of the public have no right to sue to enforce the Act's conditions.

## Common misreadings

A common statement is that taxpayers own what they fund. Under this Act the institution usually owns
the patent, and the government holds a license for its own purposes.

Another is that the government's license lets anyone use the invention. The license is for use by or
for the United States government.

A third is that every university patent is a product of federal funding. Institutions also own
inventions funded from other sources, under their own policies. The government-support statement in
a patent is the place to check.

A fourth is that royalty income goes into general funds without restriction. For subject inventions,
a nonprofit's use of net royalty income is limited by the Act.
