## What it does

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), first enacted in 1966, gives any person the right to request
records from a federal agency. The agency must release the records unless they fall within one of
nine exemptions or one of a few narrow exclusions.

The Act works in three ways. Agencies must publish certain material, such as rules, in the Federal
Register. They must proactively post other material, such as final opinions, policy statements and
records that have been requested repeatedly. And they must respond to individual requests for
records that reasonably describe what is sought.

A requester does not have to give a reason or show any special interest. The agency has 20 working
days to decide whether to comply, with extensions in unusual circumstances. In practice many
requests take much longer. Agencies may charge fees for search, review and copying, depending on the
type of requester, and fees may be reduced or waived when disclosure is in the public interest.

The nine exemptions cover, in summary:

1. properly classified national security information;
2. internal personnel rules and practices;
3. information that another statute specifically protects;
4. trade secrets and confidential commercial or financial information obtained from a person;
5. privileged inter-agency and intra-agency communications, such as deliberative drafts;
6. personnel, medical and similar files whose release would be a clearly unwarranted invasion of
   personal privacy;
7. law enforcement records, where release would cause one of several listed harms, including
   interference with proceedings or an unwarranted invasion of privacy;
8. records about the supervision of financial institutions;
9. geological information about wells.

Since 2016 the statute has also said that an agency may withhold only if it reasonably foresees that
disclosure would harm an interest an exemption protects, or if disclosure is prohibited by law. When
part of a record is exempt, the agency must release the rest.

## Who it covers

FOIA applies to agencies in the executive branch of the federal government, including departments,
independent regulatory agencies and government-controlled corporations.

It does not apply to Congress, the federal courts, or the President's immediate staff and advisors.
It does not apply to state or local governments, which have their own laws. It does not apply to
private organizations.

## What it does not do

FOIA does not reach a private university's files. Receiving federal grants does not make an
institution a federal agency, and records a grantee creates and keeps are generally not agency
records. A narrow provision in federal grant rules allows requests, through the funding agency, for
research data that were produced under a federal award and used by the government in developing an
agency action that has the force of law. It applies in limited circumstances.

What FOIA does reach is what federal agencies hold about a university. Examples include funded grant
applications and progress reports, correspondence with program officers, compliance and audit
records, civil rights complaint files and resolution documents, campus safety program review
reports, and foreign gift reports. Each is subject to the exemptions. Unfunded grant applications
are usually withheld. Personal details about individuals are commonly redacted under the privacy
exemptions.

FOIA does not require an agency to create a record, answer questions, or do research. It requires a
search for existing records.

It does not guarantee speed. Backlogs are common.

## How it is enforced

A requester who is denied, in whole or in part, may file an administrative appeal within the agency.
The denial letter must explain how. The requester may also ask the agency's FOIA public liaison for
help, or seek mediation from the Office of Government Information Services at the National Archives.

If the appeal fails, or if the agency misses its deadlines, the requester may sue in federal
district court. The court reviews the matter afresh, the agency bears the burden of justifying what
it withheld, and the court may examine the records privately. A requester who substantially prevails
may be awarded attorney's fees.

There is no general penalty against an agency for wrongly withholding, although the statute provides
for referral where personnel may have acted arbitrarily.

## Common misreadings

It is often assumed that a university that takes public money is subject to FOIA. The Act applies to
agencies. Public universities are subject to their states' records laws. Private universities are
generally subject to neither.

A response of "no records" is sometimes read as proof that something never happened. It means the
search, as conducted, found nothing responsive in that agency's systems. The scope of the search and
the wording of the request both matter.

Redactions are sometimes read as concealment of wrongdoing. Most redactions in records about
institutions protect the privacy of named individuals, including complainants, and are routine.

## Where to read it

The statute is 5 U.S.C. § 552. Each agency has its own FOIA regulations and an online reading room
of released records, which is worth searching before filing. The Department of Justice publishes a
detailed guide to the Act, and the government's central site has a request portal. See `/guides/`
for a request template and `/sources/` for how this site uses released records.
