## What it does

Title II of the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 applies to the workplace. It took
effect in November 2009. It has four main rules.

First, an employer may not discriminate on the basis of genetic information in hiring, firing, pay,
assignments, promotion or any other term or condition of employment. The same applies to employment
agencies, labor unions and joint apprenticeship and training programs.

Second, an employer may not request, require or purchase genetic information about an applicant or
employee, or about that person's family members. This rule applies whether or not the employer ever
uses the information.

Third, genetic information that an employer does hold must be kept confidential, in medical files
separate from personnel files, and may be disclosed only in limited circumstances listed in the
statute.

Fourth, an employer may not retaliate against a person who opposes a practice the law forbids or who
takes part in a proceeding under it.

Genetic information has the same broad meaning as in Title I of the Act. It includes a person's
genetic tests, the genetic tests of family members, and family medical history. It also includes the
fact that a person or family member sought or received genetic services, such as counseling or
testing, or took part in research that includes those services.

## Who it covers

Title II borrows its coverage from Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. It applies to private
employers and state and local government employers with 15 or more employees, to employment
agencies, to labor organizations and to joint labor-management training programs. Federal employees
are covered through parallel provisions. Applicants and former employees are protected as well as
current employees.

A university is covered as an employer. Faculty, staff, postdoctoral employees and student employees
are protected in their employment. Students as students are not within Title II.

## What it does not do

The ban on acquiring genetic information has exceptions. The main ones are:

- Inadvertent acquisition, sometimes called the water cooler exception, as when a manager overhears
  an employee discussing a relative's illness.
- Voluntary health or genetic services offered by the employer, including wellness programs, if the
  employee gives knowing, voluntary, written authorization and the employer receives only aggregate
  data.
- Family medical history received as part of the certification for family and medical leave.
- Information from publicly available sources, such as newspapers, though not from sources like
  court records or medical databases searched for that purpose.
- Genetic monitoring of the biological effects of toxic substances in the workplace, under strict
  conditions.
- DNA analysis by employers who run forensic laboratories, for quality control to detect sample
  contamination.

An exception to the ban on acquiring information is never an exception to the ban on using it. Even
lawfully obtained genetic information may not be the basis of an employment decision.

Title II does not cover discrimination based on a condition that has already manifested in the
employee. That is the territory of disability discrimination law. It does not apply to the uniformed
military. It does not regulate insurers (see Title I) or researchers.

The statute also does not allow claims of disparate impact, meaning claims that a neutral practice
falls more heavily on people with certain genetic information. Only intentional discrimination and
the other listed violations are covered.

## How it is enforced

The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission enforces Title II using the procedures of Title VII. A
person must first file a charge with the Commission, within 180 days of the violation, or within 300
days where a state or local agency enforces a similar law. The Commission may investigate, attempt
conciliation, sue, or issue a notice of the right to sue, after which the person may bring a
lawsuit. Remedies match those under Title VII: back pay, reinstatement, and compensatory and
punitive damages subject to caps that depend on the employer's size, plus attorney's fees.

## Common misreadings

A common assumption is that an employer breaks the law only if it acts on genetic information. In
fact, asking for it can be a violation by itself. This is why forms for employer medical
examinations often carry a notice telling the clinician not to provide family medical history. The
regulations offer suggested wording, and an employer that uses it is protected if genetic
information arrives anyway.

Another assumption is that genetic information means only DNA test results. For most employers the
practical risk is family medical history collected on a health questionnaire.

It is also sometimes assumed that the law applies to every employer. Employers with fewer than 15
employees are outside it, though state law may cover them. In Massachusetts, the state employment
discrimination statute lists genetic information as a protected category and reaches smaller
employers.

A workplace research study raises its own questions. If an employer that is also a research
institution invites its employees to join a genetic study, both this law and the human-subjects
rules bear on how the invitation and the data are handled.
