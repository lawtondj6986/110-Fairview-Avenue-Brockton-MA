## What it does

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has its own regulations to protect people who take part in
the studies it oversees. Part 50 of Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations covers informed
consent, with added safeguards for children. Part 56 covers institutional review boards (IRBs), the
committees that review research for the protection of participants.

In substance these rules resemble the Common Rule. A clinical investigation must be reviewed and
approved by an IRB and must remain under its continuing review. The IRB must find that risks are
minimized and reasonable in relation to expected benefits, that selection of subjects is equitable,
and that consent will be properly obtained and documented. Each subject must give informed consent,
with the required elements of information, unless a specific exception applies.

The rules sit alongside other FDA regulations that govern how studies are run. The main ones are the
rules for investigational new drugs (21 C.F.R. Part 312) and for investigational devices (21 C.F.R.
Part 812). Those rules put duties on sponsors and clinical investigators, such as record keeping,
monitoring and safety reporting.

The FDA rules differ from the Common Rule in several ways.

- Scope. The Common Rule follows federal funding. The FDA rules follow the regulated product.
- Exemptions. The Common Rule has a list of exempt research categories, such as certain surveys and
  secondary data studies. The FDA rules do not use that list. Their exemptions are few and specific.
- Consent waivers. For many years the FDA rules had no general provision letting an IRB waive
  consent for minimal-risk research, as the Common Rule does. FDA has since adopted a rule allowing
  such waivers under stated conditions. Check the current text of Part 50 for its terms.
- Emergency provisions. The FDA rules have their own exceptions for emergency use of a test article
  in a life-threatening situation and for planned emergency research, with detailed conditions
  including community consultation.
- Inspection. FDA inspects IRBs, investigators and sponsors directly and reviews their records,
  including records that identify subjects.
- The 2018 Common Rule revision. Several changes made to the Common Rule, such as broad consent,
  have not been carried into the FDA rules in the same form. FDA has proposed rules to bring the two
  closer together. Check whether those proposals have been finalized.

## Who it covers

The rules apply to clinical investigations that are regulated by FDA, or that support applications
for research or marketing permits for products FDA regulates. Those products include drugs,
biological products, medical devices, and some foods and other articles.

The trigger is the product and the purpose, not the source of money. A study paid for entirely by a
company or a private donor is covered if it is a clinical investigation of a regulated product. A
federally funded drug trial is covered by both these rules and the Common Rule.

The duties fall on IRBs, on clinical investigators and on sponsors. The definition of a human
subject also differs. Under the FDA rules a subject is a person who takes part in an investigation,
either as a recipient of the test article or as a control. For device studies it also includes a
person on whose specimen an investigational device is used. That can bring in studies of diagnostic
tests run on leftover specimens.

## What it does not do

The rules do not apply to research that involves no FDA-regulated product and supports no FDA
submission. A purely observational genetics study with no test article is generally outside them,
although it may be under the Common Rule or institutional policy.

They do not regulate ordinary medical practice, including a physician's decision to use an approved
product for an unapproved purpose in treating a patient.

They do not decide whether a product is safe or effective. That is the function of FDA's review of
marketing applications.

They do not give subjects a private right to sue.

## How it is enforced

FDA enforces through inspection under its bioresearch monitoring program. After an inspection it may
list observations, send a warning letter, or take administrative action. Against an IRB it can
restrict the board from approving new studies or enrolling new subjects, and can disqualify the
board or its parent institution. Against a clinical investigator who repeatedly or deliberately
fails to comply, it can begin disqualification proceedings, which bar the person from receiving test
articles. It can place a study on clinical hold, and it can refuse to rely on data from a
noncompliant study when reviewing a product. Serious cases can be referred for criminal prosecution
under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.

Warning letters and lists of disqualified or restricted investigators are published on FDA's
website.

## Common misreadings

It is often assumed that private funding puts a study outside federal human-subjects rules. For
studies of drugs and devices that is wrong.

It is also assumed that one IRB approval settles compliance under both regimes. An IRB reviewing a
study under both must apply both sets of criteria, and where they differ the stricter requirement
governs in practice.

A laboratory-developed genetic test used only in research is sometimes assumed to be outside FDA's
reach. Whether such a test is an investigational device depends on how it is used, especially
whether results are returned and used in care. That question is technical and has been the subject
of changing FDA policy and litigation, so check current sources.
