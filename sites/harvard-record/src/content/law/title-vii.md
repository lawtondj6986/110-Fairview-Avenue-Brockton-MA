## What it does

Title VII makes it unlawful for an employer to discriminate against a person with respect to hiring, firing, pay, or the terms, conditions or privileges of employment because of race, color, religion, sex or national origin. It also forbids retaliation against a person who opposes an unlawful practice or who files a charge or takes part in an investigation.

The statute does not use the word harassment. Courts have long held that harassment based on a protected characteristic is a form of discrimination in the terms and conditions of employment. Two patterns are recognized.

In the first, a supervisor ties a concrete job consequence to the employee's response to sexual demands. This is often called quid pro quo harassment. In the second, unwelcome conduct based on sex (or another protected trait) is severe or pervasive enough to create a work environment that a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive, and that the employee in fact finds so. This is a hostile work environment. A single very serious incident can be enough. So can a pattern of lesser incidents.

Whether the employer is legally responsible depends on who did the harassing. If a supervisor's harassment ends in a tangible employment action, such as firing or demotion, the employer is liable. If there is no tangible action, the employer can avoid liability by proving that it took reasonable care to prevent and promptly correct harassment, and that the employee unreasonably failed to use the complaint procedures or otherwise avoid harm. For harassment by coworkers or by non-employees, the employer is liable if it knew or should have known and failed to act.

The Supreme Court has held that discrimination because of sex includes discrimination because of sexual orientation or gender identity.

## Who it covers

Title VII covers employers with 15 or more employees, including private employers, state and local governments, employment agencies and labor organizations. Federal employees are covered by a separate section.

A university is an employer under Title VII. Faculty, staff and administrators are protected. Students who are also employees, such as teaching or research assistants, may be protected as to their employment, though whether a particular student role counts as employment can be contested. Religious institutions have limited exemptions relating to employment of members of their own religion.

## What it does not do

Title VII does not protect students as students. Sex discrimination against a student in an education program is addressed by Title IX. Where a person is both student and employee, both laws may be relevant, and courts have differed on how the two interact for employees.

It does not protect independent contractors, volunteers in most cases, or donors and visitors. An employer may, however, be responsible for a non-employee's harassment of its employees if it had control and failed to act.

It does not make every unpleasant act unlawful. The Supreme Court has said the statute is not a general civility code. Offhand comments and isolated minor incidents usually do not meet the standard.

In most courts it does not make the individual harasser personally liable. The claim is against the employer. State law may differ on this point.

## How it is enforced

A person must first file a charge with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. The deadline is 180 days from the unlawful act, extended to 300 days where a state or local agency enforces a law against the same practice, which is the case in most states. For a hostile environment claim, the charge is timely if at least one act that is part of the pattern falls within the period.

The Commission notifies the employer, may investigate, and may try to settle the matter through conciliation. It may sue in its own name, or the Department of Justice may sue where the employer is a state or local government. More often the Commission issues a notice of the right to sue, and the person then has 90 days to file a lawsuit in court.

Remedies include back pay, reinstatement, orders to change practices, attorney's fees, and compensatory and punitive damages for intentional discrimination. The combined compensatory and punitive damages are capped by statute at amounts that rise with the size of the employer.

## Common misreadings

Title VII and Title IX are often treated as the same thing on a campus. They have different triggers (employment versus education programs), different agencies, different deadlines and different liability standards. Notably, the Title VII standard for a hostile environment is "severe or pervasive," while the 2020 Title IX regulations use "severe, pervasive, and objectively offensive."

It is sometimes thought that an employer is liable only if it knew of harassment. For supervisor harassment, lack of knowledge is not by itself a defense.

It is sometimes thought that a settlement proves the claim. Settlements usually include no admission and often no public record at all. Charges filed with the Commission are confidential by law, so the absence of public charges tells a reader little.
