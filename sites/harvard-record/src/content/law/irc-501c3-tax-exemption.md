## What it does

Section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code describes a class of organizations that are exempt from federal income tax. To qualify, an organization must be organized and operated exclusively for one or more listed purposes, which include charitable, educational, scientific and religious purposes. In practice "exclusively" is read to mean primarily. An insubstantial amount of other activity does not defeat exemption.

The section then sets three limits.

- No part of the organization's net earnings may inure to the benefit of any private shareholder or individual. This is the bar on private inurement. It is aimed at insiders, such as officers, trustees and others with influence over the organization.
- No substantial part of its activities may be attempting to influence legislation. Some lobbying is allowed. The amount is limited.
- It may not participate or intervene in any political campaign for or against a candidate for public office. This ban is absolute.

A related doctrine, developed in regulations and case law, says the organization must serve public and not private interests. Benefit to private parties must be incidental to the exempt purpose.

Exemption has two main consequences. The organization generally pays no federal income tax on income related to its purposes. And, under a different section of the Code, donors may deduct their gifts. See the explainer on charitable contribution rules in `/law/`.

## Who it covers

Private nonprofit colleges and universities are 501(c)(3) organizations. Within that class they are public charities, not private foundations, because the Code treats educational organizations that have a regular faculty, curriculum and enrolled student body as public charities by their nature.

State universities are usually exempt on a different basis, as arms of government, although many also hold 501(c)(3) status or have affiliated 501(c)(3) foundations that raise money for them.

Hospitals, research institutes, museums and many other bodies affiliated with universities are commonly separate 501(c)(3) organizations.

## What it does not do

Section 501(c)(3) does not tell a university whose gifts it may accept. There is no federal tax rule that bars a charity from accepting a gift because of the donor's character, reputation or criminal record. Gift acceptance is a matter of the institution's own policies, its fiduciary duties under state law, and its judgment.

It does not require any particular governance structure, board size or conflict of interest policy, although the annual information return asks whether the organization has such policies.

It does not give donors or the public a right to sue the organization for departing from its mission.

It does not exempt all income. Income from a regularly carried on trade or business that is not substantially related to the exempt purpose is subject to the unrelated business income tax. Since 2017, certain private colleges and universities with large endowments per student have also been subject to an excise tax on net investment income. The terms of that tax have been amended, so check the current text.

It does not address state or local taxes. Property tax and sales tax exemptions come from state law.

## How it is enforced

The Internal Revenue Service enforces the section. Its tools are examination (audit) and, at the extreme, revocation of exempt status. Revocation is rare for large institutions.

For public charities, Congress added an intermediate tool. Under a separate section on excess benefit transactions, if an insider (a "disqualified person") receives an economic benefit from the organization that exceeds the value of what they provided in return, the insider owes an excise tax and must correct the transaction. Managers who knowingly approved it can also be taxed. This lets the Service penalize the individuals who benefited without ending the organization's exemption.

Courts have also upheld the denial of exemption to an educational institution whose practices violate fundamental public policy, in a decision concerning racial discrimination. The Service requires private schools to adopt and publicize a racially nondiscriminatory policy.

State attorneys general have separate authority over charitable assets under state law. See the explainer on state oversight of charities in `/law/`.

## Common misreadings

Tax exemption is often described as a public subsidy that makes a university answerable to the public in a general way. The actual legal conditions are the specific ones above. They concern purpose, private benefit, lobbying and campaign activity.

Private inurement is sometimes thought to cover any benefit to a donor. The doctrine is directed at insiders. A donor who receives something of value in return for a gift raises a different issue: the size of the donor's deduction, and possibly the private benefit doctrine if the benefit is more than incidental.

Nonprofit does not mean the institution cannot run a surplus, hold a large endowment or pay high salaries. It means there are no owners to whom profits are distributed. Compensation must be reasonable, and that is judged against comparable positions.

A loss of exemption is sometimes assumed to follow automatically from misconduct by a person connected to the institution. Exemption turns on how the organization itself is organized and operated.
