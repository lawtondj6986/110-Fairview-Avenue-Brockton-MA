## What it does

The Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments of 1988, known as CLIA, set federal quality standards for laboratory testing of human specimens. The aim is that a result reported for a patient is accurate, reliable and timely wherever the test is run.

Under CLIA, a facility needs a federal certificate before it examines materials derived from the human body to provide information for the diagnosis, prevention or treatment of disease, or for the assessment of health. This applies whether the facility is a hospital laboratory, a commercial laboratory, a physician's office or a university core facility, and whether or not it bills anyone.

The requirements depend on how complex the testing is. Tests are sorted into three levels.

- Waived tests are simple tests with a low risk of an incorrect result. A laboratory that runs only these needs a certificate of waiver and must follow the manufacturer's instructions.
- Moderate complexity tests carry requirements for personnel, quality control, proficiency testing and inspection.
- High complexity tests carry the strictest requirements, including specific qualifications for the laboratory director and testing staff. Most genetic sequencing done for clinical purposes is high complexity.

Laboratories doing moderate or high complexity testing must enroll in proficiency testing where it is available, keep quality systems, and undergo inspection roughly every two years. Inspection is done either by government surveyors, leading to a certificate of compliance, or by an approved private accrediting organization, leading to a certificate of accreditation.

Three agencies share the work. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services issues certificates, conducts inspections and takes enforcement action. The Food and Drug Administration assigns each test its complexity level. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention provides scientific and technical support.

## Who it covers

CLIA covers any laboratory in the United States that tests human specimens for the health purposes listed above. A laboratory in a state whose own licensing program has been approved as at least as strict may be exempt from the federal program and regulated by the state.

The regulations also list exceptions. The one that matters most for universities is the research exception. A research laboratory that tests human specimens but does not report patient-specific results for the diagnosis, prevention or treatment of any disease, or the assessment of the health of individual patients, is not subject to CLIA. Laboratories that do only forensic testing are also excepted.

## What it does not do

CLIA does not judge whether a test is clinically meaningful. It addresses analytical quality: whether the laboratory can measure what the test claims to measure, accurately and consistently. Whether a result predicts disease or should change treatment is a different question, sometimes called clinical validity and clinical utility.

It does not approve tests for marketing. That is the role of the Food and Drug Administration for test kits and other devices.

It does not apply to research testing that stays within the exception. The same instrument in the same building can be outside CLIA on Monday, when it is used for a de-identified research study, and inside it on Tuesday, if an individual's result is sent to that person or to a clinician for use in care.

It does not regulate privacy as its main subject, although the regulations say to whom results may be released. Privacy is mainly the business of HIPAA and state law.

## How it is enforced

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services can inspect, cite deficiencies and require a plan of correction. If problems are serious or not fixed, it can impose sanctions. These include directed plans of correction, on-site monitoring, civil money penalties, suspension or limitation of the certificate, and revocation. An owner or operator of a laboratory whose certificate is revoked can be barred from owning or operating another laboratory for a period. Laboratories that take part in Medicare can also lose approval to receive payments. Intentional violations can be prosecuted.

The agency publishes a registry each year listing laboratories that have been sanctioned.

## Common misreadings

The most common misreading in a research setting is that results from a research laboratory can be handed back to participants freely. Returning an individual result for use in health decisions is the very activity that brings CLIA into play. This is why studies often confirm a research finding in a certified laboratory before returning it. There has been long debate about how this rule fits with a participant's right under HIPAA to see their own data, and institutions handle the tension in different ways.

A second misreading is that a CLIA certificate means a test is approved by the government as medically valid. The certificate is about the laboratory's processes.

A third is that CLIA applies only to laboratories that bill Medicare. It applies to all laboratories that do covered testing.

A fourth is that direct-to-consumer genetic tests are outside CLIA. If the test gives health-related results on an individual's specimen, the laboratory that runs it generally needs a certificate.
