## What it does

Title VI says that no person in the United States shall, on the ground of race, color or national
origin, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to
discrimination under any program or activity receiving federal financial assistance.

Like Title IX, it is a condition on funding. An institution that takes federal money agrees not to
discriminate on the listed grounds. Each federal agency that provides assistance is directed to
issue rules and to enforce them.

This explainer is brief and is included mainly for contrast. Title IX, passed eight years later,
copied Title VI's wording and substituted sex for race, color and national origin. Much of the law
on how Title IX is enforced, including the private right to sue and the procedure for ending funds,
was developed first or in parallel under Title VI.

## Who it covers

Title VI covers recipients of federal financial assistance in every field, not only education.
Hospitals, state agencies, transit systems, and colleges and universities are all covered if they
receive federal funds. Under a 1988 amendment, coverage extends to all the operations of a covered
college or university, not only the part that received the money.

It protects any person who takes part in or seeks the benefits of the funded program. For a
university that mainly means students and applicants.

Federal agencies have long interpreted the law to protect students who face discrimination based on
shared ancestry or ethnic characteristics, including members of religious groups when the
discrimination is based on those characteristics and not on religious practice alone.

## What it does not do

Title VI does not list sex. Sex discrimination in education programs falls under Title IX. That is
the main contrast. Title IX is limited to education programs and activities, while Title VI applies
to any funded program.

It does not list religion. Discrimination based solely on religious belief or practice is outside
it, though other laws may apply.

It generally does not cover employment. The statute says it does not authorize agency action over
employment practices, except where a primary objective of the federal assistance is to provide
employment. Employment discrimination is mainly addressed by Title VII.

It does not apply to institutions that receive no federal financial assistance, or to the federal
government's own programs.

It does not, by its own terms, reach practices that are neutral on their face and unintentionally
fall more heavily on one group. The Supreme Court has read the statute itself to forbid only
intentional discrimination. Agencies have issued regulations that address practices with an
unjustified discriminatory effect, but individuals cannot sue to enforce those regulations. The
status of such regulations is a matter of agency policy that can change, so check current sources.

## How it is enforced

Enforcement follows the same two routes as Title IX.

The administrative route runs through the funding agency. For schools and colleges, that is usually
the Office for Civil Rights in the Department of Education. Anyone may file a complaint, generally
within 180 days of the alleged discrimination. The office investigates and, if it finds a violation,
seeks voluntary compliance, usually through a written resolution agreement. If that fails, the
agency may begin proceedings to end funding or may refer the matter to the Department of Justice.
Ending funds requires a hearing, an express finding on the record, and notice to congressional
committees, and it is limited to the particular program in which noncompliance was found. It is
rare.

The second route is a private lawsuit. Individuals may sue for intentional discrimination and may
recover damages. In harassment cases, courts generally apply a standard like the one used under
Title IX, asking whether the institution had actual knowledge of severe and pervasive harassment and
responded with deliberate indifference.

## Common misreadings

Title VI and Title VII are easily confused because both come from the Civil Rights Act of 1964.
Title VI concerns federally funded programs. Title VII concerns employment. Their coverage, agencies
and procedures differ.

It is sometimes assumed that Title VI applies only to public institutions. It applies to any
recipient of federal funds, and private universities are recipients.

It is sometimes assumed that a complaint to the Office for Civil Rights, or the opening of an
investigation, means a violation was found. Opening an investigation means only that the complaint
met the office's threshold for review.

Finally, because Title IX borrowed Title VI's language, readers sometimes assume the two have
identical exceptions. They do not. Title IX has exceptions, for example for certain admissions and
for religious institutions, that have no counterpart in Title VI.

## Where to read it

The statute is at 42 U.S.C. §§ 2000d to 2000d-7. The Department of Education's regulations are at 34
C.F.R. Part 100. Other agencies have their own. The Department of Justice coordinates enforcement
across agencies and publishes a legal manual on Title VI. See the Title IX and Title VII explainers
in `/law/`, and `/comparison/` for how this site sets different frameworks side by side.
