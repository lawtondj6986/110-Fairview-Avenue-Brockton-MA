## What it does

Defamation is the area of law that provides a remedy when a false statement of fact damages a
person's reputation. Libel is defamation in written or other fixed form. Slander is spoken
defamation. In the United States, defamation is mainly a matter of state law, made by courts and in
part by statutes. The First Amendment to the Constitution sets limits that apply in every state.

Although wording differs from state to state, a plaintiff generally must prove:

- a statement of fact that is false;
- that the statement was about the plaintiff (identifiable to readers, even if not named);
- that it was published, meaning communicated to at least one other person;
- that it was defamatory, meaning it would tend to harm the plaintiff's reputation;
- fault on the part of the defendant;
- in some cases, actual harm.

The fault requirement comes from constitutional law. A public official or public figure must prove
actual malice. That phrase has a special meaning: that the defendant knew the statement was false or
acted with reckless disregard of whether it was false. Reckless disregard means the defendant in
fact had serious doubts about the truth. It is not shown by carelessness alone, and it has nothing
to do with ill will. A private figure suing over a matter of public concern must show at least
negligence, and states set their own standards within that limit.

Several defenses and limits matter to anyone who writes about public records.

Truth. A statement that is true, or substantially true, is not actionable. Minor inaccuracies do not
defeat the defense if the gist is accurate.

Opinion. A statement that cannot reasonably be understood as asserting a provable fact is protected.
But calling something an opinion does not protect it if it implies undisclosed defamatory facts.
Stating the facts on which a view is based, accurately, and then giving the view, is the safer form.

The fair report privilege. This privilege protects a person who publishes a fair and accurate report
of an official proceeding or official record, even if a statement made in that proceeding or record
turns out to be false. The reason is that the public has an interest in knowing what happens in its
courts, legislatures and agencies. A report is fair and accurate if it conveys a substantially
correct account. It need not be complete, but it must not be edited in a way that misleads, for
example by reporting an accusation and leaving out that it was dismissed.

## Who it covers

Anyone who publishes a statement can be a defendant: a newspaper, a broadcaster, an institution, a
website operator or an individual. Any living person, and most organizations, can be a plaintiff. As
a general rule a claim cannot be brought on behalf of someone who has died.

The fair report privilege is available to anyone who reports on covered material in most states, not
only to professional news media, though state formulations differ.

## What it does not do

Defamation law does not reach true statements, however damaging, or pure opinion, however harsh.

The fair report privilege does not cover everything connected with a legal matter. Depending on the
state, it may not cover documents that have been filed but not yet acted on by a court, statements
made by lawyers or parties outside the proceeding, or unofficial remarks by police officers. It does
not protect a writer's own additions. If a writer goes beyond the record to assert, in the writer's
own voice, that an allegation is true, the privilege does not cover that assertion.

In many states the privilege depends on attribution. The reader must be able to tell that the
statement comes from an official record or proceeding. In some states the privilege can be lost
through malice in the sense of ill will. In others it cannot.

Repeating someone else's defamatory statement is generally treated as publishing it. Saying "it is
alleged" or "according to a source" does not by itself protect the repeater. This is why the fair
report privilege, which is an exception to that rule for official material, matters so much.

A federal statute, section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, generally protects operators of
websites from liability for content supplied by third-party users. It does not protect them for
content they write themselves.

## How it is enforced

Defamation is enforced by private lawsuits. The usual remedy is money damages. Courts are very
reluctant to order speech not to be published in advance.

Time limits are short. Each state sets its own limitation period, often between one and three years.
Most states follow a single publication rule, under which the period runs from first publication and
is not restarted merely because the material stays available online. Significant republication to a
new audience may be treated differently.

Many states have statutes aimed at meritless suits over speech on public issues, known as anti-SLAPP
laws. They let a defendant seek early dismissal and, often, attorney's fees. Their strength varies
greatly, and their use in federal court is unsettled. Some states also have retraction statutes that
limit damages if a correction is promptly published on request.

## Common misreadings

"Actual malice" does not mean spite.

"Alleged" is not a shield. The protection for reporting allegations comes from the fair report
privilege, and only for allegations in covered official sources, accurately described.

A lawsuit is not a finding. A complaint contains one side's claims. Reporting that a claim was made
is different from reporting that it is true, and a careful account also reports any denial,
dismissal or settlement.

A statement can be literally accurate and still give a false impression by what it leaves out or
places side by side. Some states recognize claims based on such implication.
