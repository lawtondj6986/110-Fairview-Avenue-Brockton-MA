## What it does

Part 93 of Title 42 of the Code of Federal Regulations sets out the Public Health Service policies on research misconduct. The Public Health Service includes the National Institutes of Health, which is the largest federal funder of biomedical research. The rule tells institutions that receive this funding how to handle allegations, and it sets out what the federal government may do when misconduct is found.

The rule's definition is narrow and specific. Research misconduct means fabrication, falsification or plagiarism in proposing, performing or reviewing research, or in reporting research results.

- Fabrication is making up data or results and recording or reporting them.
- Falsification is manipulating research materials, equipment or processes, or changing or omitting data or results, so that the research is not accurately represented in the research record.
- Plagiarism is the appropriation of another person's ideas, processes, results or words without giving appropriate credit.

Research misconduct does not include honest error or differences of opinion.

A finding requires three things. There must be a significant departure from accepted practices of the relevant research community. The misconduct must have been committed intentionally, knowingly or recklessly. And the allegation must be proven by a preponderance of the evidence, meaning it is more likely than not.

The rule assigns the first responsibility to the institution. Each funded institution must have written procedures and must file an assurance of compliance. The process has stages. An assessment decides whether an allegation falls within the definition and is specific enough to pursue. An inquiry is a preliminary review of whether an investigation is warranted. An investigation is the formal examination of the evidence, ending in a written report with findings. The institution must secure the research records, give the respondent notice and a chance to comment, protect the confidentiality of those involved as far as possible, and guard complainants, witnesses and committee members against retaliation.

The same definition, drawn from a government-wide policy issued in 2000, is used by other federal research agencies in their own rules.

## Who it covers

The rule applies to institutions that apply for or receive Public Health Service support for biomedical or behavioral research, research training, or related activities, and to people at those institutions who are involved in such work. It covers applications for funding as well as funded work, so misconduct in an unfunded grant application is included.

## What it does not do

The definition leaves out a great deal of conduct that people often call research misconduct in a loose sense. Part 93 does not cover:

- authorship or credit disputes, unless they amount to plagiarism;
- violations of human-subjects or animal welfare rules;
- undisclosed financial conflicts of interest;
- misuse of grant funds;
- harassment or other personal misconduct in a laboratory;
- poor record keeping or weak methods that fall short of a significant, culpable departure from accepted practice;
- questions about who funded research or how a gift was accepted.

Each of these may violate other rules or institutional policies. Institutions may also adopt broader definitions for their own internal purposes.

The rule does not apply to research with no Public Health Service connection, though other agencies' rules or institutional policy may.

It has a time limit. It generally applies only to misconduct occurring within six years of the date the allegation is received, with exceptions, for example where the respondent has continued to use or cite the questioned work.

It does not make investigation records public. Proceedings are confidential, and many institutional findings never become public.

## How it is enforced

The Office of Research Integrity in the Department of Health and Human Services oversees the system. Institutions must report to it when an inquiry finds that an investigation is warranted, and must send it the investigation report and the institution's decision. The Office reviews the record and may accept the findings, ask for more work, or make its own findings.

If it concludes that misconduct occurred, the Department may impose administrative actions. These include requiring correction or retraction of published work, requiring supervision or certification of the person's future funded research, barring the person from serving on advisory committees, and debarment or suspension from eligibility for federal funding for a period. Many cases end in a voluntary settlement agreement. A respondent may contest findings before an administrative law judge.

The Office publishes case summaries that name respondents when findings of misconduct are made and administrative actions are imposed. It can also take compliance action against an institution that fails to follow the rule.

Separately, false statements in grant applications or reports can lead to civil liability under the False Claims Act or to criminal prosecution. Those matters are handled by other offices.

## Common misreadings

A retraction is not a finding of misconduct. Journals retract papers for many reasons, including honest error.

The absence of a published federal finding is not a finding of innocence, and it is not evidence that no inquiry took place. Only cases that end in a federal finding are published.

The six-year limit is sometimes read as immunity for older work. Journals and institutions can still correct the literature regardless of the limit.

An institutional finding and a federal finding are separate. An institution may find misconduct under its own policy in a case where the federal office makes no finding, and the reverse is possible.

## Where to read it

The rule is at 42 C.F.R. Part 93. It was first issued in 2005. A revised version was published in 2024, with changes to procedures and definitions taking effect over the following period.
