A whistleblower policy tells employees and others how to raise concerns about illegal or improper
conduct and promises that they will not be punished for doing so honestly.

A typical policy identifies what should be reported (fraud, misuse of funds, legal violations,
serious breaches of policy), lists reporting channels (a supervisor, a compliance office, internal
audit, the general counsel, or a hotline run by an outside vendor that accepts anonymous reports),
describes how reports are reviewed, and forbids retaliation such as dismissal, demotion or
harassment. Reports of significant matters are usually summarized for the board's audit committee.
The annual federal information return filed by most tax-exempt organizations, Form 990, asks whether
the organization has a written whistleblower policy, which has encouraged adoption. Public
universities are generally also subject to state whistleblower statutes.

The policy is an internal commitment. Legal protection for whistleblowers is separate, comes from a
patchwork of federal and state laws, and depends on the type of employer, the subject of the report
and how it was made. The policy also covers reports and not outcomes. It does not guarantee that a
report will be substantiated or that the reporter will be told the result. People in dependent
positions, such as students, postdoctoral fellows and junior staff, may still hesitate to report
someone who controls their careers.
