A campus climate survey asks members of a campus community about their experiences of sexual
harassment, sexual assault, stalking and relationship violence, whether or not they told anyone. It
usually also asks whether respondents know where to get help, whether they would expect a report to
be taken seriously, and how they perceive the behavior of peers and faculty.

Surveys are run by institutions themselves, by multi-campus consortia using a shared questionnaire,
or by outside researchers. Some states require them, and federal agencies sometimes require them as
a term of a resolution agreement. A well-documented survey publishes its questionnaire, its
definitions, who was invited, the response rate, and any weighting applied to make respondents
resemble the whole population.

Climate surveys exist because official statistics capture only what is reported to authorities. They
are the main source of prevalence estimates. They are also estimates, with limitations. They depend
on who chooses to respond, on question wording, and on the time period asked about. Results from
different survey instruments, or from the same instrument at institutions with very different
response rates, are hard to compare directly. A survey describes experiences across a population. It
cannot identify or substantiate any individual incident.
