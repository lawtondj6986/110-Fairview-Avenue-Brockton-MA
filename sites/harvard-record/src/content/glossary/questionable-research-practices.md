Questionable research practices are ways of designing, analyzing or reporting research that are not
misconduct under the formal definition but that depart from good practice and can produce misleading
results. Some scientific bodies prefer the term detrimental research practices.

Commonly cited examples include reporting only the experiments or outcomes that "worked", trying
many analyses and presenting the one that reaches statistical significance (often called p-hacking),
presenting a hypothesis formed after seeing the results as if it had been predicted, stopping data
collection when the result looks favorable, excluding data points without a rule stated in advance,
keeping inadequate records, and granting authorship to people who did not contribute. Surveys of
researchers suggest such practices are much more common than outright fraud, and they are regarded
as one cause of published findings that later fail to replicate.

The category is defined by degree and context. Several of these choices can be legitimate when made
openly and for stated reasons. What makes them questionable is concealment and their effect on the
reliability of the result. Remedies are mostly preventive: registering study plans in advance,
sharing data and analysis code, and reporting all outcomes. Questionable practices do not usually
lead to formal misconduct findings, though they can lead to corrections or retractions.
