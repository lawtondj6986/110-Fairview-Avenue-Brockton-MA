Fundraising in the United States is guided by several professional codes written by associations of
practitioners. They are not laws. They express what the profession regards as proper conduct.

The best known are the Code of Ethical Principles and Standards of the Association of Fundraising
Professionals (AFP), the Donor Bill of Rights (a short statement of what donors are entitled to
expect, endorsed by several fundraising associations), the statement of ethics of the Council for
Advancement and Support of Education (CASE), and the ethics statement of Apra for prospect
researchers. Common themes include truthfulness in appeals, using gifts as the donor intended,
protecting confidential donor information, disclosing conflicts of interest, and not paying
fundraisers a commission or percentage of money raised.

These codes are oriented largely toward duties owed to donors and to the profession. They say less
about the opposite question, which is when an institution should decline a donor, although some
address the obligation to put the organization's mission ahead of a gift. Enforcement is limited. An
association can discipline or expel a member, but it has no power over an institution, and many
people who raise money, including presidents, deans and faculty, are not members. An institution's
own policies, and the charity and tax law of its state and of the United States, are what bind it.
