A saliva sample is a common way to collect DNA without a needle. Saliva itself is mostly water. The
human DNA in it comes from cells shed from the lining of the mouth and from white blood cells. A
collection tube usually contains a preserving liquid that stabilizes the DNA at room temperature so
the kit can be mailed. A cheek (buccal) swab works on the same principle.

Saliva yields enough DNA for genotyping and for sequencing. It also contains DNA from mouth bacteria
and sometimes from food, so the proportion of human DNA varies from sample to sample. Blood
generally gives a more consistent yield, which is why clinical laboratories often prefer it, but
saliva is widely accepted.

Two limits are worth knowing. First, a saliva kit provides DNA only. It does not provide living
cells that can be grown in a laboratory, which requires a different kind of sample such as blood or
a skin biopsy. Second, a mailed kit normally carries no proof of whose saliva is inside. Unless
collection was witnessed and documented, a result shows what the DNA in the tube contains, not who
supplied it.
