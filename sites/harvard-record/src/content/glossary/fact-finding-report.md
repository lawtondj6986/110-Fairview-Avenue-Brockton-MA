A fact-finding report records the results of an inquiry into what occurred. Its purpose is to
establish a factual account: who did what, when, and what others knew. Decisions about consequences
are left to the officials or body that commissioned it.

Reports of this type generally follow a common outline. They state the mandate and scope, describe
the method (how many people were interviewed, what records were searched, what could not be
obtained), present a chronology, give findings, and often add recommendations. Careful reports
separate what documents show from what witnesses recalled, and note where accounts conflict.
Individuals may be named, identified by role or anonymized, depending on the institution's choices
and privacy considerations.

A fact-finding report is not a judicial finding. Nobody testified under oath, there was no
cross-examination, and the people discussed may not have had a chance to respond. The standard of
proof is whatever the investigators adopted, and is sometimes not stated. A reader should check the
method section first, then note the scope limits, then distinguish findings from recommendations and
from characterizations in any covering letter or press release, which may be more favorable to the
institution than the report itself. Where a report is silent on a point, that silence should not be
read as a finding that nothing happened.
