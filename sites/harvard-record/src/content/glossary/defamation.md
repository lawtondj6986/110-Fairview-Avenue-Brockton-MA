Defamation is the area of law concerned with false statements that injure reputation. In the United
States it is mainly state law, shaped by constitutional limits that protect speech. The details vary
by state, but a claim generally requires a false statement of fact about an identifiable person,
communicated to at least one other person, made with the required degree of fault, and causing harm
to reputation.

Several features set the boundaries. Truth is a defense, so a substantially true statement is not
defamatory however damaging it is. Statements of pure opinion, which cannot be proven true or false,
are generally protected, although an opinion that implies undisclosed false facts may not be. The
level of fault depends on who is suing. Public officials and public figures must show that the
speaker knew the statement was false or acted with reckless disregard for whether it was, a standard
called actual malice. Private individuals usually face a lower bar, often negligence. Various
privileges protect certain kinds of statement, such as fair reports of official proceedings.

For readers, the practical points concern careful writing: separating fact from allegation,
attributing claims, avoiding implication by juxtaposition, and including responses. This entry is a
general description. It is not legal advice, and the law differs between states and countries.
