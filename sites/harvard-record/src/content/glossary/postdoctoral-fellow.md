A postdoctoral fellow, or postdoc, is a person with a doctoral degree who works for a limited period
in a senior researcher's group to gain further training and build a record of publications. The
position is common in the sciences and exists in other fields too.

Postdocs are appointed to a laboratory or center for terms of one or more years, usually renewable
up to a limit set by the institution. Their support comes either from the principal investigator's
grants or gifts, or from an individual fellowship awarded by a government agency or foundation.
Institutions often give the two groups different titles and classify them differently, sometimes
treating one as employees and the other as trainees or stipend recipients, with differing benefits.
The principal investigator acts as supervisor and mentor and typically writes the recommendation
letters on which the postdoc's next job depends.

Postdocs occupy a position between student and faculty. They do much of the hands-on research but
have little institutional power, short contracts and heavy dependence on one supervisor. Those
conditions can make it difficult for them to raise concerns about how a laboratory is run or who is
present in it, though the same policies and reporting channels that protect other members of the
university apply to them.
