---
term: "\"No evidence of\" versus \"evidence of no\""
short: "Saying no evidence was found for something is different from saying there is evidence it did not happen; the first reports a search, the second a finding."
category: "Records and sourcing"
aliases: ["no evidence of", "evidence of absence", "found no evidence"]
related: ["absence-of-evidence", "denial", "inference", "stated-intent-vs-documented-activity", "official-record"]
---

The two phrases sound alike and mean different things. "No evidence of X" reports the result of
looking: within the material examined, nothing was found that shows X. "Evidence of no X" is a
positive finding that X did not occur, for example a record placing a person somewhere else on the
date in question.

Official reports often use the first form. A sentence such as "the review found no evidence that
funds were used for that purpose" is bounded by what the reviewers examined. Its weight depends on
the scope of the review, the records and witnesses available, and whether the kind of event in
question would be expected to leave a trace. A thorough search of complete records that finds
nothing is fairly strong. A limited review with missing records and unavailable witnesses is much
weaker. Good reports state these limits.

Errors run in both directions. One is to read "found no evidence" as a declaration that the thing
never happened, which is to treat a bounded search as exoneration. The other is to read it as
evasion or cover-up, and to imply that the thing happened and was simply not found. Neither follows
from the words. The accurate course is to repeat the statement as made, with its source and scope,
and to add nothing.
