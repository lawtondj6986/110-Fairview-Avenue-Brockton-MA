A congressional hearing is how committees gather information on the record. Witnesses submit
written statements in advance, deliver shorter oral statements and answer questions from members,
typically in rounds alternating between the majority and minority parties. Committees can invite
witnesses or, under their own rules, compel attendance by subpoena.

Hearings leave several kinds of record. Committee websites usually post the witness statements and
a video soon after. The official printed hearing record, with a transcript and any materials
inserted into the record, is published later, sometimes many months afterward, and is available
through government publishing services. Members may also send written questions after the hearing,
and witnesses' answers can appear in the printed record.

Statements made at a hearing are testimony, and witnesses can face penalties for knowingly false
statements to Congress, but a hearing is not a trial. Members' questions and statements are not
findings. Committee reports, when issued, are the place where a committee states conclusions, and
those conclusions may reflect only the majority's view.
