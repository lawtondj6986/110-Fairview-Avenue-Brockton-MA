Eugenics is the name given in the 1880s to the idea that the hereditary quality of human populations
could and should be improved by influencing reproduction. Its advocates distinguished "positive"
eugenics, encouraging people judged desirable to have more children, from "negative" eugenics,
discouraging or preventing reproduction by those judged unfit.

In the early twentieth century eugenics was widely accepted among scientists, physicians and
policymakers in many countries. It led to laws permitting compulsory sterilization, to restrictions
on marriage and immigration, and to institutional confinement. In the United States, many states
adopted sterilization laws and tens of thousands of people were sterilized under them. In Nazi
Germany, eugenic doctrine was used to justify mass sterilization and murder.

Eugenics rested on scientific errors, including the assumption that complex social traits such as
poverty or criminality were simple inherited characteristics, and on prejudice about race, class and
disability. It was discredited scientifically and morally after the Second World War, and the term
is now used almost entirely in a critical sense. In current debate it is applied, with varying
precision, to proposals for selecting or altering the genetic traits of future children. Individual
reproductive choice and state coercion are distinct matters, and careful writing says which is
meant.
