An advisor of choice is someone a complainant or respondent chooses to help them in a campus
disciplinary proceeding. Under the Clery Act regulations added after the 2013 reauthorization of the
Violence Against Women Act, institutions must allow both parties in cases of dating violence,
domestic violence, sexual assault and stalking to be accompanied by an advisor of their choice at
any related meeting or proceeding. The institution may limit what advisors can do, as long as the
limits apply equally to both parties.

The 2020 Title IX regulations gave the advisor a larger role in postsecondary hearings: the advisor,
not the party, conducts cross-examination, and the institution must provide one at no cost to a
party who does not have one for the hearing. Many institutions keep a list of trained staff or
volunteers who can serve.

An advisor is not necessarily a lawyer, and is not the same as a confidential advocate, whose role is
support and whose communications may be privileged. Advisors usually may not speak for the party
except during questioning. The rules have changed several times and institutions' own policies vary,
so check the current versions.
