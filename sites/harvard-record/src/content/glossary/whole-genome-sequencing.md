Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) attempts to read nearly all of a person's DNA rather than a selected
portion. The DNA is broken into fragments, the fragments are read by a sequencing machine, and
software assembles the reads by aligning them to a reference genome. The output is a very large data
file and a list of several million places where the person differs from the reference.

WGS finds kinds of variation that narrower methods miss, including variants in regulatory regions
and many structural changes. Its cost has fallen dramatically since the first human genome was
completed in the early 2000s, which is why it moved from a major international project to a service
that laboratories and some companies offer to individuals.

"Whole" is an approximation. Standard short-read methods struggle with highly repetitive regions, so
some parts of the genome are read poorly or not at all. Producing the sequence is also different
from understanding it. The meaning of most variants is unknown, and interpretation depends on
databases that are still growing. Possessing a genome sequence does not by itself reveal a person's
medical future, and the quality of a sequence depends on depth and on the standards of the
laboratory.
