An induced pluripotent stem cell (iPS cell or iPSC) is an ordinary body cell that has been
reprogrammed in the laboratory into a state resembling an embryonic stem cell. The method, first
reported in mice in 2006 and in human cells in 2007, works by temporarily activating a small set of
genes that are normally active in early embryos. The resulting cells can multiply indefinitely and
can be coaxed into becoming many cell types, including nerve, heart and liver cells.

The usual starting material is a skin fibroblast or a blood cell. Because iPS cells carry the
donor's own genome, they let researchers study a person's cells in tissues that cannot safely be
sampled, and they are widely used to model inherited diseases and to test drugs. Therapies based on
iPS-derived cells are in clinical trials for a number of conditions. Their use in routine care
remains limited.

An iPS cell line is not a clone of a person and cannot simply be returned to the body to rejuvenate
it. Reprogramming can introduce genetic and other abnormalities, and safety is an active area of
study. Like any human cell line, iPS cells are a renewable source of the donor's DNA, which raises
the same consent and privacy questions.
