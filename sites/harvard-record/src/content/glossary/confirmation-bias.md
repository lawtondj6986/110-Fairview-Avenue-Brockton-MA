---
term: "Confirmation bias"
short: "The tendency to look for, notice, interpret and remember information in ways that support what one already believes."
category: "Records and sourcing"

Confirmation bias is a well-documented pattern in human reasoning. People tend to seek evidence that
fits their existing view, to accept such evidence with little scrutiny, to examine contrary evidence
harshly or overlook it, and to read ambiguous material as supportive. It operates without any
intention to deceive and affects experts as well as casual readers.

Large document collections are fertile ground for it. A researcher who begins with a theory can
search for a name, find the handful of messages that fit, and never read the hundreds that do not.
Ambiguous phrases get read in the most suspicious or the most innocent sense, according to what the
reader expects. The same bias leads institutions reviewing themselves to accept reassuring
explanations.

Recognized countermeasures are procedural. State in advance what evidence would count against the
hypothesis, and look for it. Read complete threads and files, not search results alone. Consider at
least one alternative explanation for each key fact. Apply the same standard of proof to welcome and
unwelcome claims. Have someone who does not share the hypothesis review the work, and record what
was searched and what was not found. Awareness alone does little. A reader can ask of any account
whether it reports evidence that cuts against its own conclusion.
