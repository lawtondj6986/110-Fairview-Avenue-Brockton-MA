Universities classify outside funding as either a gift or a sponsored award, and the classification
decides which office handles it and which rules apply.

A gift is given without expectation of anything in return. The donor may name a purpose and receive
a general report on how the money was used, but there is no detailed budget, no deliverables, no
rights in the results and no fixed project period. Gifts are processed by the development office. A
sponsored award, whether called a grant, contract or cooperative agreement, supports a defined
project. It usually has a statement of work, a budget, a start and end date, financial and technical
reporting, terms on intellectual property or publication, and sometimes the return of unspent funds.
Sponsored awards go through the sponsored programs office and carry indirect cost charges.

The word grant causes confusion because foundations call their payments grants even when they
function as gifts. Institutions therefore classify by the terms, not the label, usually with a
written checklist. The line matters. Money treated as a gift generally avoids indirect cost charges
and the compliance reviews attached to research awards, and it may be recorded in a different system
with different approvals. A sum that funds a specific researcher's work can thus travel through
fundraising channels and never appear in research administration records.
