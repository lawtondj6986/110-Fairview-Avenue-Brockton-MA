term: "Honorary title"
short: "A title or distinction an institution confers as a mark of honor, without the duties, pay or rights of a regular appointment."

An honorary title recognizes a person without employing them or admitting them to the faculty in the
ordinary sense. The best known is the honorary degree, awarded at commencement by vote of the
governing board. Others include honorary fellowships and honorary memberships of institutes or
societies.

Honorary degrees are typically awarded for distinction in scholarship, public life, the arts or
philanthropy, and they involve no coursework. Many institutions state that they do not award them in
return for gifts, though major benefactors do receive them. Honorary and courtesy titles at the
level of a school, center or program are less formal. They may be granted by a dean or director
under local rules, and the central administration may keep no complete list of them.

An honorary title is not a credential and not an academic appointment. It conveys no teaching or
supervisory role, no vote, and usually no right to an office, an email account or building access,
although some honorary fellowships do come with practical privileges. Titles of this kind can
nonetheless be presented to the outside world as evidence of a university position. When a record
shows a person using a title, the questions are who conferred it, under what authority, what
privileges came with it, and whether it was ever withdrawn.
