term: "Attorney general (state or territorial)"
short: "The chief legal officer of a U.S. state or territory, who represents the government in court and enforces laws such as those on consumer protection and charities."

Each U.S. state, the District of Columbia and each territory has an attorney general. In most states
the office is filled by election. In some states and territories the governor or another body
appoints the holder. The office is distinct from the Attorney General of the United States, who
heads the federal Department of Justice.

The powers of the office vary by jurisdiction but usually include defending the government in
lawsuits, giving legal opinions to officials, and bringing civil enforcement actions under consumer
protection, antitrust, civil rights and environmental statutes. Attorneys general can generally
issue investigative demands for documents and testimony before filing any case. In most states the
office also oversees charitable organizations and charitable assets, which gives it authority over
nonprofit institutions, including private universities, on matters such as misuse of donated funds.
Criminal jurisdiction ranges from broad, in a few small states and territories, to limited, where
local district attorneys handle most prosecutions.

A lawsuit filed by an attorney general is still a set of allegations until a court rules or the
defendant admits them. Such suits are usually civil and commonly end in a settlement or consent
judgment without admission of liability. A press release from the office presents the government's
position as a litigant and should be read alongside the court filings.
