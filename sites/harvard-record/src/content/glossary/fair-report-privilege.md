---
term: "Fair report privilege"
short: "A legal protection for fair and accurate accounts of official proceedings and public records, even when the reported statements later prove false."
category: "Records and sourcing"
aliases: ["fair reporting privilege", "fair and accurate report privilege"]
related: ["defamation", "court-record", "official-record", "attribution", "public-record"]

The fair report privilege is a defense in defamation law. It protects someone who publishes a fair
and accurate account of an official proceeding or official record from liability for defamatory
statements contained in that proceeding or record. Its rationale is that the public has an interest
in knowing what happens in courts, legislatures and government agencies, and that reporters act as
the public's eyes there.

In general terms the privilege has two conditions. The source must be of a qualifying kind, such as
a judicial proceeding, a legislative hearing, or an official report or action of a government body.
And the account must be fair and accurate, meaning a substantially correct and balanced summary that
is presented as a report of what the record says. Reporting that a complaint "alleges" something,
when it does, is the typical example.

The privilege is a creature of state law, and its scope differs. States vary on which documents
qualify, on whether the privilege can be lost through ill will, and on how clearly the report must
be attributed to the official source. It generally does not cover a writer's own added assertions,
commentary that adopts an allegation as fact, or a one-sided selection that distorts the record. It
has no bearing on whether an allegation is true. This is a general description and not legal advice.
