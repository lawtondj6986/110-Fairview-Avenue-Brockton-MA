---
term: "Retaliation"
short: "Adverse action taken against a person because they reported discrimination, filed a complaint, or took part in an investigation. Civil rights laws prohibit it."
category: "Law and compliance"
aliases: ["reprisal"]
related: ["title-ix", "title-vii", "title-vi", "supportive-measures"]

Retaliation is punishment for using or supporting a civil rights process. Title VI, Title VII, Title
IX and many other laws forbid an institution, and people acting for it, from intimidating,
threatening, coercing or discriminating against someone because that person made a report, filed a
complaint, served as a witness, or refused to take part in a proceeding.

A retaliation claim generally has three parts: the person engaged in protected activity, they
suffered a materially adverse action, and there is a causal link between the two. Adverse actions in
a university can include dismissal, a lowered grade, removal from a project, loss of funding, a
damaging reference, or a campaign of harassment by peers that the institution tolerates. Witnesses
and respondents are protected as well as complainants.

A retaliation claim stands on its own. It can succeed even if the original complaint is not
substantiated, as long as that complaint was made in good faith. For this reason retaliation
findings appear in the record even where the underlying allegation was never proven.

Not every unwelcome event after a complaint is retaliation. The law requires that the protected
activity caused the adverse action. An institution may still act on independent grounds, such as
documented performance problems, though timing often becomes the central dispute.
