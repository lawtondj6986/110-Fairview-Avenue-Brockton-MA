term: "Undue influence in research"
short: "Pressure or inducement that compromises a person's free choice about joining a study, typically an excessive reward or the sway of someone with authority over them."

In research ethics, undue influence is an improper pressure on a person's decision to take part in a
study. Consent must be voluntary, and the regulations direct researchers to seek it only under
circumstances that minimize the possibility of coercion or undue influence.

The two terms are distinguished. Coercion involves a threat of harm for refusing. Undue influence
usually works through an offer or a relationship: a reward so large or inappropriate that it leads
people to discount risks, or a request from someone the person depends on or finds hard to refuse.
Familiar settings include students recruited by their professors, employees recruited by
supervisors, patients recruited by their own physician, and junior laboratory members asked to give
samples for a senior colleague's project. Safeguards include having a neutral person obtain consent,
keeping participation confidential from the person in authority, and offering an equivalent
alternative.

Payment is not undue influence in itself. Reasonable compensation for time and inconvenience is
accepted. The concept in research ethics is also separate from undue influence in the law of wills
and contracts, and from the broader question of whether donors or sponsors improperly influence an
institution's decisions, which is a matter of conflicts of interest and governance.
