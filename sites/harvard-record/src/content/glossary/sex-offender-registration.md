Sex offender registration is a consequence of conviction, not a separate punishment imposed by a
judge case by case. Every U.S. state, territory and many tribal jurisdictions keeps a registry. A
federal law passed in 2006, the Sex Offender Registration and Notification Act, sets minimum
national standards, sorts offenses into three tiers with different registration periods, and makes
it a federal crime to fail to register after moving between jurisdictions.

A registrant must appear in person at set intervals and report home address, employment and school
enrollment. Much of this is posted on public websites, including a national site that searches all
jurisdictions at once. Federal law also requires registrants to tell authorities when they enroll or
work at a college, and requires each college to state in its annual security report where the campus
community can obtain registry information.

Registration turns on the offense of conviction and on the law of each jurisdiction. The same
conduct can lead to different tiers, durations and public visibility in different places, and a duty
to register in one jurisdiction does not always carry over unchanged to another. A registry entry
shows a conviction and a classification. It does not describe the underlying facts in detail, and
the absence of an entry does not show that no offense occurred.
