A genetic variant is a difference in DNA sequence between an individual and a reference genome, or
between individuals. Variants range from a change in a single base, to small insertions and
deletions, to large structural changes in which long stretches of DNA are duplicated, removed or
rearranged. Every person carries several million variants. Most are inherited, and a small number
arise newly in each generation.

Laboratories that interpret variants for medical purposes commonly sort them into five classes:
pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign and benign. The classification
reflects current evidence and can change as more data accumulate. Many variants found in any person
fall into the uncertain category.

The word mutation is often used for the same thing, but in everyday speech it suggests harm. Most
variants have no known effect on health, and some are beneficial. Variant is the neutral term and is
now preferred in clinical genetics. A variant being present also does not mean a condition is
present. For many variants the link to disease is probabilistic, and the chance that a carrier
develops the condition can be well below certainty.
