Summary judgment is the main way civil cases are decided without a trial once evidence has been
gathered. A party, most often the defendant, argues that the important facts are not really in
dispute and that the law requires judgment in its favor. The motion is supported by evidence from
discovery: deposition transcripts, documents, sworn declarations and written answers to questions.

The judge does not weigh credibility or decide who is more believable. The judge asks whether a
reasonable jury, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion,
could find for that party. If so, the motion is denied and the case goes to trial. If not, judgment
is entered. A court can grant the motion on some claims and deny it on others.

Summary judgment differs from a motion to dismiss in what the court looks at. A motion to dismiss
tests the complaint alone. Summary judgment tests the evidence. For that reason summary judgment
filings are often the richest public source in a case, since the parties attach the evidence they
rely on, subject to any sealing.

A denial of summary judgment is not a finding of liability. It means only that there is a factual
dispute for a jury. Many cases settle soon after such a ruling.
