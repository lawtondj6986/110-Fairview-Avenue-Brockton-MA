A retraction is a public notice that a published work should no longer be relied on. In scholarly
publishing, a journal retracts an article when its findings are unreliable, whether because of
honest error or misconduct, or because of problems such as plagiarism, duplicate publication,
unethical research or a compromised peer review. The retraction notice explains the reason and says
who is retracting the article. The original normally stays available, clearly marked as retracted,
so the scholarly record is preserved. An expression of concern is an interim notice that an article
is under question.

In news publishing, a retraction is a statement withdrawing a story or a specific claim. In some
U.S. states, statutes give a timely retraction legal significance in defamation cases, for example
by limiting the damages available. The rules differ by state.

A retraction is not proof of fraud. Many scientific retractions follow honest mistakes that the
authors themselves reported. It also does not erase the work's influence, since retracted papers
often continue to be cited by people unaware of their status. Checking whether a paper has been
retracted or corrected is therefore a basic step before relying on it. A person retracting their own
earlier statement, in the ordinary sense of taking it back, is a different and informal use of the
word.
