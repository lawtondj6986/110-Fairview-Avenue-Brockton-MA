GINA is a federal law with two main parts. Title I bars group health plans and health insurers from
using genetic information to decide eligibility or set premiums, and from requiring genetic tests.
Title II bars employers with fifteen or more employees from using genetic information in hiring,
firing, pay or promotion, and generally forbids them to request or buy such information about
employees or their relatives. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission enforces Title II.

In universities, GINA matters in two settings. As employers, institutions may not ask staff for
family medical history, with narrow exceptions such as voluntary wellness programs. As research
centers, they often mention GINA in consent forms for genetic studies, to tell participants what
protection they have if results become known.

The gaps are as important as the coverage. GINA does not apply to life insurance, disability
insurance or long-term care insurance. It does not cover discrimination based on a condition that
has already appeared, which is a matter for disability law. It does not regulate what researchers,
schools, direct-to-consumer testing companies or private individuals do with genetic data outside
insurance and employment. Some states have passed broader laws, so protection varies by state.
