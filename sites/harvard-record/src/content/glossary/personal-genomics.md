Personal genomics refers to obtaining and interpreting an individual's own genetic data. It covers
consumer genotyping tests, whole-genome or exome sequencing bought by or offered to healthy
individuals, and the tools and services that interpret the resulting data.

The field grew as the cost of sequencing fell. Early personal genomes were sequenced in the late
2000s at great expense, often for prominent or wealthy individuals and volunteers in research
projects. The same analysis now costs a small fraction of that and is offered by laboratories and
companies. Typical outputs are ancestry estimates, carrier status for recessive conditions, a small
number of medically significant variants, drug response information and polygenic scores.

Expectations have often run ahead of results. For most healthy people a genome sequence produces few
findings that change medical care. A minority learn of a significant variant, and that can be
valuable. Many results are uncertain or are probabilities that add little to ordinary risk factors.
A personal genome is also not purely personal, since it reveals information about relatives. Having
been sequenced through a research project or a private arrangement is different from having had a
clinical genetic test, and the data may not meet clinical standards.
