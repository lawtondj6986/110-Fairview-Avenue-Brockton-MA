---
term: "Gene therapy"
short: "Treatment of disease by delivering genetic material into a patient's cells, to supply a working gene or alter a faulty one."
category: "Genetics and biomedical research"
related: ["gene-editing", "crispr", "somatic-vs-germline-editing", "stem-cell"]
---

Gene therapy treats or prevents disease by introducing genetic material into a patient's cells. The
classic form adds a working copy of a gene to compensate for one that does not function. Newer forms
silence a harmful gene or edit the DNA directly.

Delivery is the central technical problem. Most gene therapies use a vector, usually a virus that
has been modified so it cannot cause disease, to carry the genetic material into cells. Some
treatments are given directly into the body (in vivo). Others remove cells from the patient, modify
them in the laboratory and return them (ex vivo). A number of gene therapies have been approved for
specific conditions, including some inherited forms of blindness, certain blood disorders, spinal
muscular atrophy and some cancers.

Approved gene therapies target defined diseases, most of them caused by a single gene, and each went
through clinical trials. They are often very expensive and can carry serious risks, including immune
reactions. Gene therapy is not a general method for enhancing healthy people, and no gene therapy
has been shown to slow human aging. Offers of gene therapy outside regulated trials or approved
products should be viewed with caution.
