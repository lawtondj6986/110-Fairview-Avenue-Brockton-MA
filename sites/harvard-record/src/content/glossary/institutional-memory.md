Institutional memory is what an organization knows about its own history. It exists in two forms.
One is documentary: files, databases, minutes, email and archives. The other is personal: what
experienced employees remember about why something was done and who was involved.

Universities are prone to losing both. Presidents, deans and development officers change every few
years, and each transition can break the chain of knowledge about a donor or an arrangement. Records
are scattered among schools, departments and individual offices. Email is deleted under retention
schedules, databases are replaced, and websites are overwritten without archiving. Much important
business, especially at senior levels and in fundraising, is conducted in conversation and never
written down.

The concept matters in two ways when reading the record. First, a gap in what an institution knows
today is not proof that no one knew at the time. Knowledge may have left with the people who held
it. Second, a statement that the institution "has no record" or "was not aware" describes the result
of a present-day search and present-day recollection. It is weaker than a statement that something
did not happen. Reconstructing events often depends on outside sources, such as court filings,
journalism and personal papers, that preserved what the institution itself did not.
