term: "Quiet phase"
short: "The early, unannounced stage of a fundraising campaign, when an institution secures its largest commitments before making the goal public."

The quiet phase is the part of a campaign that happens before the public launch. It can last several
years. During it, the institution approaches its closest and wealthiest supporters, typically
trustees and past major donors, for lead gifts.

The reasoning is practical. An institution wants to announce a goal it is confident of reaching, and
a large total already raised gives the launch credibility. By convention, a substantial fraction of
the goal is committed during the quiet phase, and the final goal is often adjusted according to how
this stage goes. Gifts made in the quiet phase count toward the campaign total, so a campaign's
counting period begins well before the date the public first hears of it.

The quiet phase is confidential by design. Its negotiations take place among a small group of senior
officials, board members and prospects, and the largest naming and program commitments of a campaign
are usually settled here. Records from this stage are internal. A gift announced at or after launch
may have been negotiated long before, which matters when establishing the order of events. "Quiet"
refers to the lack of publicity, not to secrecy about donors' identities inside the institution, and
quiet phase gifts are subject to the same acceptance policies as any others.
