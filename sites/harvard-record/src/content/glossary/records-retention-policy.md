A records retention policy governs the life of an institution's documents. Its central element is a
retention schedule: a table listing categories of records, the period each must be kept, and what
happens afterward.

Retention periods come from legal requirements, the statute of limitations for possible claims,
operational need and historical value. Financial records, personnel files, student records, research
data, contracts, gift documentation and email each have their own rules. When the period ends,
records are either destroyed or, if they have lasting value, sent to the university archives. The
policy is suspended by a legal hold (also called a litigation hold), an instruction from the general
counsel to preserve everything relevant to an actual or anticipated lawsuit or investigation.
Destroying records to obstruct an official proceeding is a federal crime, whatever the schedule
says. Public universities must additionally follow their state's public records laws.

For someone researching an institution, the policy explains why certain records do not exist.
Routine destruction on schedule is lawful and common, so missing records are not in themselves
evidence of concealment. Equally, compliance with schedules is uneven. Many records survive past
their date in personal files, backups and email accounts, while others are lost early through
neglect. Electronic logs, such as door access data, often have short retention periods.
