---
term: "Plaintiff and defendant"
short: "The plaintiff is the party who brings a civil lawsuit. The defendant is the party sued or, in a criminal case, the person charged."
category: "Legal procedure"
aliases: ["plaintiff", "defendant", "petitioner", "respondent", "complainant", "party"]

Every lawsuit has parties. In a civil case the plaintiff starts the action by filing a complaint and
bears the burden of proving the claims. The defendant is the person or organization against whom the
claims are made. A case name lists the plaintiff first. In a criminal case there is no plaintiff.
The government, called the prosecution, the State, the People or the United States, brings charges
against a defendant.

Other labels mark the same roles in different settings. In some proceedings, including many appeals
and family court matters, the parties are the petitioner and the respondent. On appeal they are the
appellant and the appellee. In campus disciplinary processes the usual terms are complainant, for
the person said to have been harmed, and respondent, for the person accused. Parties may use
pseudonyms with the court's permission.

Being named as a defendant shows only that someone filed a claim. Anyone can be sued, and courts do
not screen complaints for merit before filing. People and organizations mentioned in a complaint are
also not necessarily parties. A document may describe the conduct of many individuals while suing
only one institution. It is worth checking the caption and docket to see who was actually sued, and
whether any defendant was later dropped.
