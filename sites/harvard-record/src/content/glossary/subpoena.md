A subpoena compels someone to provide evidence. One type requires a person to appear and testify at
a deposition, hearing or trial. Another, traditionally called a subpoena duces tecum, requires the
production of documents or other records. In civil litigation, subpoenas are the means of obtaining
evidence from people and organizations that are not parties to the case. Lawyers can usually issue
them in the court's name without a judge's prior approval.

Other bodies have subpoena power too. Grand juries use subpoenas to gather evidence in criminal
investigations. Many government agencies can issue administrative subpoenas. Committees of Congress
and state legislatures can issue them under their own rules. The recipient may object, negotiate the
scope, or ask a court to quash or narrow the demand, for example because it is unduly burdensome or
seeks privileged material. Ignoring a valid subpoena can result in contempt.

Receiving a subpoena carries no implication of wrongdoing. It means only that someone believes the
recipient holds relevant information. A university, bank or telephone company may receive many of
them in matters in which it has no stake. A subpoena is also different from a search warrant, which
a judge issues on a showing of probable cause and which authorizes officers to seize material
themselves.
