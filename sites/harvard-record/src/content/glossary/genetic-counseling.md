Genetic counseling is the process of helping a person understand and adapt to the medical,
psychological and family implications of genetic information. It is provided by genetic counselors,
who have specialized graduate training, and by physicians trained in medical genetics. Counselors
are certified by a professional board, and many U.S. states also license them.

A session before testing typically covers family history, what a test can and cannot detect, the
possible kinds of result, including uncertain ones, and practical matters such as privacy and
insurance. After testing, the counselor explains the result, what it means for the person's health
and for blood relatives, and what options follow. Counseling is common in prenatal care, in cancer
risk assessment and in the diagnosis of rare disorders.

The established approach is to inform and support the person's own decisions, not to direct them.
Genetic counseling is also not the same as the automated reports supplied with consumer tests, and
it is not routinely built into research studies. A study may return a finding with a recommendation
to seek counseling. Whether it pays for or arranges that counseling depends on the protocol.
