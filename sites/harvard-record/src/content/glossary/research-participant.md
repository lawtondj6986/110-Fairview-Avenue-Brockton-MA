A research participant is a person who takes part in a study. U.S. regulations use the older term
human subject and define it as a living individual about whom an investigator obtains information or
biospecimens through intervention or interaction, or whose identifiable private information or
biospecimens are used. A person can therefore be a research subject without ever meeting a
researcher, for example when identifiable stored samples or records are studied.

Participants have defined protections. They are entitled to the information needed for informed
consent, to refuse or withdraw without penalty, to have risks minimized, and to have their privacy
protected. Participants may be healthy volunteers or patients, and they may be paid, reimbursed or
unpaid.

The role should be distinguished from others in the same study. A participant is not a collaborator,
an investigator, a sponsor or a donor to the research, although one person can occupy more than one
of those roles. Such overlaps deserve attention because they can blur who is protecting whom. A
researcher who contributes a sample, or a funder who enrolls in a study they support, is still a
research subject for that purpose. A participant is also not a patient in the usual sense, because
the aim of research is knowledge, not individual care.
