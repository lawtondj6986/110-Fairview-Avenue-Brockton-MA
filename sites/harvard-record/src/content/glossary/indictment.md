An indictment is the document that begins most serious criminal prosecutions. A prosecutor presents
evidence to a grand jury, a panel of citizens that meets in private. If the grand jury finds
probable cause to believe that the person committed the offense, it approves the charges, and the
indictment is filed in court. The U.S. Constitution requires this procedure for federal felonies
unless the defendant waives it. States vary, and many allow a prosecutor to charge by a document
called an information after a preliminary hearing before a judge.

The indictment lists the charges, or counts, and the essential facts for each. Some, called speaking
indictments, tell a detailed story. An indictment can stay sealed until the defendant is arrested,
and prosecutors can replace it with a superseding indictment that adds or changes charges.

An indictment is an accusation. The grand jury hears only the prosecution's side, the defense is not
present, and probable cause is a much lower standard than proof beyond a reasonable doubt. The
defendant is presumed innocent unless and until convicted. Charges in an indictment may later be
dismissed, reduced in a plea agreement, or rejected by a trial jury.

Conversely, the absence of an indictment is not a finding that nothing happened. Prosecutors decline
cases for many reasons, including limits on time and evidence.
