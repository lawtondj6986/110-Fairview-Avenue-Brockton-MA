An employer identification number, or EIN, is a federal tax identifier for an entity. It is written
as two digits, a hyphen and seven digits. Despite the name, an organization does not need employees
to have one. Charities, foundations, trusts and estates all use EINs on their tax filings.

For research into donors and institutions, the EIN is the key that links records together. Names are
unreliable: two foundations can have nearly identical names, an organization can change its name,
and a single family can run several entities. Form 990 and 990-PF filings, grant lists and IRS
exemption records can all be searched by EIN, which makes it possible to confirm that two documents
refer to the same body.

An EIN identifies an entity, not a person, and it does not reveal who controls the entity. Some
related organizations, such as a university and a separately incorporated affiliate, have different
EINs, while a group exemption can cover many chapters. An EIN printed on a document is useful for
matching but is not, by itself, proof that the document is genuine.
