term: "Injunction"
short: "A court order requiring a party to do something or to stop doing something, enforceable through contempt of court."

An injunction is a remedy in which a court directs a party's conduct instead of awarding money. It
may prohibit an action, such as enforcing a policy, or require one, such as reinstating a student.
Disobeying it can lead to fines or other penalties for contempt.

Injunctions come in stages. A temporary restraining order lasts a short time and can be issued
quickly, sometimes before the other side is heard. A preliminary injunction lasts while the lawsuit
is pending. To obtain one in federal court, the party asking generally must show a likelihood of
success on the merits, a likelihood of irreparable harm without the order, that the balance of
hardships favors it, and that the order is in the public interest. A permanent injunction is issued
as part of the final judgment.

A preliminary injunction is a forecast, not a verdict. The court has made an early assessment on a
limited record, and the final outcome may differ. News that a rule or policy has been "blocked"
often refers to this provisional stage.

An injunction in a civil case is also distinct from a no-contact directive issued by a university,
which is an administrative measure with no court behind it, and from a protective order in
discovery, which governs confidential information.
