term: "Fibroblast"
short: "A common connective tissue cell that is easy to grow from a small skin sample and is widely used as starting material in cell research."

A fibroblast is a cell of connective tissue. In the body, fibroblasts produce collagen and the other
structural material that holds tissues together, and they help repair wounds. They are abundant in
skin.

Fibroblasts matter in research because they are easy to obtain and grow. When a small piece of skin
from a biopsy is placed in a nutrient solution, fibroblasts migrate out and multiply. Within a few
weeks a laboratory can have millions of cells that carry the donor's complete genome. These cultures
can be frozen and thawed, used to study how a person's cells behave, tested for inherited metabolic
conditions, or reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells.

Ordinary fibroblasts are not immortal. A culture taken from a person divides a limited number of
times, then slows and stops, a state called senescence. Such a culture is called a primary culture
or a finite cell line. To obtain an unlimited supply, the cells must be deliberately altered, or
reprogrammed into stem cells. Fibroblasts are also not stem cells. They are specialized cells and do
not turn into other tissue types on their own.
