A conflict of interest in research exists when a secondary interest could improperly influence
professional judgment about the research. The interests most often regulated are financial: payments
from a company, equity in a start-up, royalties, or paid consulting related to the work. Personal
relationships, career advantage and loyalty to a funder can also create conflicts. An institution
has a conflict of its own when its finances, or those of its senior officials, could affect research
conducted under its name.

A conflict is managed through a sequence of steps. Researchers disclose their relevant interests to
the institution. The institution decides whether an interest relates to the research and could
affect it. If so, it adopts a management plan, which may include public disclosure, independent
monitoring of the work, changes to the researcher's role, or divestment. Federal health research
funders require this system by regulation, and journals require authors to declare competing
interests.

Having a conflict is not misconduct, and it does not show that the research is biased. It describes
a risk arising from circumstances. The usual failures are not disclosing an interest, or not
following the management plan. Disclosure is also not a cure. A disclosed conflict remains a
conflict.
