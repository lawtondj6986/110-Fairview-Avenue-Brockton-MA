DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the molecule that stores the inherited instructions a cell uses to
build and maintain an organism. It is a long chain made of four chemical units called bases,
abbreviated A, C, G and T. Two chains pair up, A with T and C with G, and twist into the familiar
double helix. The order of the bases is the information.

In people, almost every cell carries a full copy of the person's DNA, packaged into 23 pairs of
chromosomes in the cell nucleus, plus a small separate piece in the mitochondria. One copy of each
chromosome pair comes from each parent. Because the same DNA is present throughout the body, a
laboratory can read it from blood, saliva, a cheek swab, skin or other tissue.

DNA is not a complete description of a person. It influences traits and disease risks, but most
traits also depend on environment, development and chance. A DNA sample is also not the same as a
DNA sequence: a sample is physical material, while a sequence is the data produced when a laboratory
reads that material. The two are stored, shared and protected in different ways.
