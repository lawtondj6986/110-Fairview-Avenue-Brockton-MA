Donor due diligence, or vetting, is the process of checking who a donor is and where the money comes
from. Its aim is to identify legal, financial or reputational problems before the institution
commits itself.

The work is usually done by prospect research staff, sometimes with outside vendors. It commonly
includes a search of news coverage, court records, sanctions lists and regulatory actions, a review
of the donor's business interests, and confirmation of who actually controls a foundation or company
making the gift. Findings are summarized in a memo. Serious concerns are referred to senior
officials or a gift acceptance committee. The depth of the check usually scales with the size of the
gift, so small gifts may receive none.

Due diligence has practical limits. It depends on public information, on a staff member starting the
process, and on the gift being visible to the development office at all. It is typically done at the
point of a gift, not continuously, so later developments may go unnoticed. Finding a problem does
not dictate an outcome: officials still weigh it and decide. Due diligence also differs from
prospect research in purpose. Research estimates how much a person could give. Due diligence asks
whether the institution should accept.
