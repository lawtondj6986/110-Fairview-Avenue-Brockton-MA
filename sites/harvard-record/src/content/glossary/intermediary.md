In fundraising, an intermediary is anyone through whom a gift or a donor relationship passes. The
word covers quite different roles, so it helps to specify which is meant.

A financial intermediary is an entity that makes the payment: a family foundation, a donor-advised
fund sponsor, a company, a trust or a law firm. The institution's receipt goes to that entity, which
is the legal donor, while the person who recommended or controlled the gift may be recorded
separately or not at all. A relational intermediary is a person who acts for the donor or connects
donors with the institution: a wealth adviser, a family office employee, a lawyer, a friend, or a
faculty member who serves as the donor's main contact.

Intermediaries are common and usually unremarkable. Wealthy people often give through foundations
and deal through advisers. The difficulty is one of visibility. A check from an entity may not
reveal who stands behind it, and vetting that examines only the named payer can miss the person who
directed the money. An institution may also bar gifts from a person and still receive money that the
person has encouraged others to give. Describing someone as an intermediary is a claim about their
role in a transaction and should be supported by records showing what they actually did.
