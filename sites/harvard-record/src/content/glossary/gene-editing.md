Gene editing, also called genome editing, means making a deliberate, targeted change to the DNA of a
living cell. The change may switch a gene off, correct a variant, or insert new sequence. CRISPR is
the most widely used method. Earlier tools, known as zinc finger nucleases and TALENs, work on a
similar principle of cutting DNA at a chosen site.

In practice gene editing is used far more in research than in treatment. Scientists edit cells and
laboratory animals to learn what genes do and to build models of disease. It is also used in
agriculture. In medicine, a small number of therapies that edit a patient's own cells have been
approved, and more are being tested, mostly for conditions caused by a single well-understood gene.

Gene editing is often discussed as if it could redesign complex human characteristics. That is not
supported by current science. Traits such as height, intelligence and lifespan are influenced by
very large numbers of variants with tiny individual effects, together with environment, and there is
no known set of edits that would reliably change them. Gene editing also differs from gene therapy
in the classic sense, which adds a working gene without altering the existing one, though the terms
now overlap.
