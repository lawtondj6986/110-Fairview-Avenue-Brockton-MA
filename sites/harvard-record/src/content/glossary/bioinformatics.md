term: "Bioinformatics"
short: "The use of computing and statistics to store, analyze and interpret biological data, especially DNA, RNA and protein sequences."

Bioinformatics is the field that develops and applies computational methods to biological data. Its
central material is sequence data from DNA, RNA and proteins, along with related measurements of how
genes are used in cells.

The field is indispensable to modern genetics because a sequencing machine does not output a
readable genome. It produces hundreds of millions of short fragments. Bioinformatics software aligns
these fragments to a reference, identifies where the sample differs, annotates each difference with
what is known about it, and applies quality checks. Other bioinformatics work compares genomes
across people or species, searches for associations between variants and traits, and predicts the
structure and function of proteins. The chain of programs used for a task is called a pipeline, and
results can differ depending on the pipeline and reference data chosen.

Bioinformatics overlaps with computational biology, and the two terms are often used
interchangeably. It is distinct from biomedical informatics, which centers on clinical and health
data. It also has limits: analysis can identify variants and statistical patterns, but it cannot by
itself establish what a variant does in the body. That requires experimental or clinical evidence.
