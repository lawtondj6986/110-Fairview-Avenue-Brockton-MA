Arbitration is a private process in which a neutral arbitrator, not a judge or jury, decides a
dispute. Mandatory arbitration refers to a clause, signed before any disagreement, that requires the
parties to use arbitration for future claims. Such clauses appear in employment contracts, some
student enrollment agreements and many consumer contracts. The Federal Arbitration Act makes most of
them enforceable, and courts have read it broadly.

Arbitration is usually faster and less formal than litigation, with limited discovery and very
narrow grounds for appeal. Proceedings and awards are typically private. A federal law enacted in
2022 lets a person bringing a sexual assault or sexual harassment claim choose to go to court
despite a predispute arbitration agreement. Unionized employees often arbitrate grievances under a
collective bargaining agreement, which is a different setting. Other limits exist in particular
fields and change over time.

For researchers, the consequence is that a dispute resolved in arbitration may leave no court record
at all. The absence of a lawsuit therefore does not show the absence of a dispute. Arbitration also
differs from mediation, in which a neutral helps the parties agree but decides nothing.
