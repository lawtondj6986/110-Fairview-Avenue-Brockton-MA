---
term: "Finding versus allegation"
short: "An allegation is a claim one party makes. A finding is a conclusion reached by a court, jury, agency or other decision-maker after considering evidence."
category: "Legal procedure"

This distinction is the most important one for reading legal and institutional records. An
allegation is an assertion by a party with an interest in the outcome. A finding is a determination
by a body with authority to decide, made after weighing evidence under a stated standard. A jury
verdict, a judge's findings of fact after trial, an agency's letter of findings, and a determination
of responsibility in a campus process are all findings.

Findings differ in strength and scope. They depend on the standard of proof used, the procedures
followed, and the question the decision-maker was asked. A finding that an institution failed to
follow required procedures is not a finding about what an individual did.

Many documents that look authoritative contain no findings. A judicial opinion on a motion to
dismiss recites the complaint's allegations as if true because the law requires it to. A settlement,
a resolution agreement signed before an investigation ends, and a voluntary dismissal all close a
matter without deciding it.

For each factual statement drawn from a record, a careful reader asks who said it, in what document,
at what procedural stage, and whether any decision-maker ever ruled on it. This site applies that
test, as described at /methodology/.
