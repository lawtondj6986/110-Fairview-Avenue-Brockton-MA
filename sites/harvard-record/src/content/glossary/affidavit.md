---
term: "Affidavit and declaration"
short: "Written statements of fact made under oath or under penalty of perjury and submitted as evidence. An affidavit is sworn before an official. A declaration is signed without one."
category: "Legal procedure"

An affidavit is a written statement that the signer, called the affiant, swears to be true before a
notary or another official authorized to administer oaths. A declaration serves the same purpose
without the official. Under federal law, and in many states, a statement signed and dated "under
penalty of perjury" has the same effect as a sworn affidavit. Courts accept both, and the choice
between them is mostly a matter of convenience.

These statements are used wherever a court needs facts but is not hearing live testimony: to support
or oppose motions, to identify attached exhibits, to describe a search for documents, or to support
an application for a warrant. They are supposed to rest on the signer's personal knowledge and to
state facts, not argument.

A sworn statement is evidence, which gives it more weight than an unsworn allegation in a complaint,
and a knowingly false one can be prosecuted as perjury. It is still untested. It is drafted with the
help of lawyers, presents one person's account, and has not been subject to cross-examination.
Courts treat it as a basis for deciding whether a dispute exists, not as proof that settles the
dispute. Readers should also separate what the signer says they saw or did from what they say they
were told by others.
