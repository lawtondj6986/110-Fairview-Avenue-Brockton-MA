Incapacitation, in campus sexual misconduct policies, describes a condition in which a person lacks
the ability to consent. A person may be incapacitated because they are asleep or unconscious,
because of a temporary or permanent mental or physical condition, or because alcohol or drugs have
impaired them to the point that they cannot understand what is happening or make a reasoned decision
about it.

Policies distinguish incapacitation from intoxication. Someone can have been drinking, even heavily,
and still be capable of consent. Decision-makers therefore look for evidence about the person's
functioning at the time: whether they could walk and speak coherently, whether they were vomiting or
losing consciousness, and whether they understood where they were and what was occurring. Most
policies then ask whether the other person knew, or a sober reasonable person in their position
would have known, of the incapacity.

The term comes from institutional policy and survey research, and its wording varies from one
institution to another. State criminal statutes use their own terms, such as mentally incapacitated
or physically helpless, and some apply them only where the substance was given without the person's
knowledge. Memory loss alone does not establish incapacitation, since a person can act with apparent
awareness during a blackout and not recall it later.
