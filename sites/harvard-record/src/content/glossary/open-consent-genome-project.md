An open-consent genome project is a study design in which participants agree that their genome
sequences, and usually health and trait information about them, will be released publicly for anyone
to use. It differs from the conventional model, in which data are coded, access is restricted to
approved researchers, and participants are told that their privacy will be protected.

The model rests on the view that genetic privacy cannot honestly be guaranteed, so it is better to
recruit people who understand and accept that. Projects of this kind typically require volunteers to
show that they understand the risks, sometimes by passing a test, before they enroll. The stated
risks include being identified, having relatives' information exposed, and uses of the data that
nobody can foresee. Participants may also give tissue samples from which cell lines are made and
distributed. Such projects remain subject to ethics review.

Open consent does not mean an absence of consent or of rules. It is a demanding form of informed
consent with unusually broad terms. It also suits only some people. Volunteers tend to be well
educated and comfortable with exposure, so open datasets are not representative samples. Withdrawal
is of limited effect, because data already published and copied cannot be recalled.
