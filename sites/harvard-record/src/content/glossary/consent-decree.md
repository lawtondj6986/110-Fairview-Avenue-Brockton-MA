---
term: "Consent decree"
short: "A court order, entered with the agreement of the parties, that resolves a lawsuit and remains enforceable by the court."
category: "Law and compliance"
related: ["consent-judgment", "resolution-agreement", "settlement", "injunction", "without-admission-of-liability"]
---

A consent decree is a settlement that a judge has approved and entered as an order of the court. The
parties negotiate the terms, and the court reviews them, typically to confirm that they are fair and
lawful, before signing. Government enforcement suits in areas such as civil rights, antitrust and
environmental law often end this way.

The decree usually requires the defendant to take or stop certain actions over a period of years. It
may appoint an independent monitor and require regular reports. Because it is a court order, a party
that violates it can be brought back before the same judge and held in contempt, without the need
for a new lawsuit. The court keeps jurisdiction until the decree ends or is modified.

Consent decrees typically state that the defendant does not admit liability, so the decree is not a
judicial finding that the defendant broke the law. It is also different from a resolution agreement
with an administrative agency, which no court supervises, and from a private settlement, which is an
ordinary contract. The terms consent decree and consent judgment overlap heavily. Decree is the more
common word when the order requires continuing conduct and not only payment of money.
