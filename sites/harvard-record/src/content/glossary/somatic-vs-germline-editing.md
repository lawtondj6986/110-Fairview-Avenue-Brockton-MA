---
term: "Somatic versus germline editing"
short: "Somatic editing changes DNA in body cells of one person and is not inherited; germline editing changes eggs, sperm or embryos and would pass to descendants."
category: "Genetics and biomedical research"
aliases: ["germline editing", "heritable genome editing", "somatic editing"]
related: ["gene-editing", "crispr", "gene-therapy", "eugenics"]
---

The distinction turns on which cells are changed. Somatic cells are the cells of the body other than
eggs and sperm. An edit made in a patient's blood, liver or eye cells affects that patient only and
is not passed to children. Germline cells are eggs, sperm and the cells of an early embryo. An edit
made there would be present in every cell of the resulting person and could be inherited by later
generations.

Somatic editing is treated by regulators much like other new therapies. It goes through clinical
trials, and some treatments have been approved. Heritable germline editing in humans is in a
different position. It is prohibited or tightly restricted in many countries, and in the United
States federal law has barred the Food and Drug Administration from reviewing applications for
clinical studies that involve creating a pregnancy from an intentionally edited embryo. Scientific
bodies have said that it is not yet safe enough to attempt and that there is no broad societal
agreement on whether to permit it.

Laboratory research on embryos that are not used to start a pregnancy is a separate category, with
its own rules that differ by country. Proposals to "improve" future children genetically fall on the
germline side of the line.
