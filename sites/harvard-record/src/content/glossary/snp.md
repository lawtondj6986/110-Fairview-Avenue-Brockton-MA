A single nucleotide polymorphism, abbreviated SNP and pronounced "snip", is a position in the genome
where one base differs between people, for example where some carry an A and others a G. By
convention the term is used for variants that are reasonably common in a population. The broader
term single nucleotide variant covers rare ones as well.

Millions of SNPs are known in humans. Most have no direct effect on health or traits. They are
useful mainly as markers. Because nearby stretches of DNA tend to be inherited together, a SNP can
signal which version of a neighboring region a person carries. Researchers use this property to look
for statistical associations between regions of the genome and traits, and testing companies use it
to estimate ancestry and to find relatives.

A SNP associated with a trait is usually not the cause of that trait. It is a signpost for a region,
and the effect attached to any single common SNP is typically very small. Statements that a person
"has the SNP for" a characteristic generally overstate what is known. A list of someone's SNPs is
also not their full genome. It is a sample of preselected positions.
