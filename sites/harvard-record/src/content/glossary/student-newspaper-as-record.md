Student newspapers report on their own institutions week by week, often over many decades. Their
archives are a standing chronicle of campus events and frequently the only public account of
meetings, appointments, visits, controversies and official statements at the time they occurred.

Most student papers at U.S. universities are produced by students with little or no control by the
administration. Some are legally independent corporations with their own finances. Others are funded
or housed by the institution. Reporters attend faculty meetings, interview administrators and
receive documents from sources inside the community. Many papers have digitized their back issues,
and university archives and libraries usually hold complete runs in print or microfilm. For a
researcher, the value lies in contemporaneity. An article dated in a given year shows what was
publicly known and said then, before later events shaped recollection.

The usual cautions about journalism apply with some force. Student reporters are trainees working
under deadline, staff turn over every year, and errors may not have been corrected in the archived
version. Coverage is uneven, since a topic no one pitched went unreported. A newspaper report
establishes that something was reported, and by whom it was said. Wherever possible it should be
checked against primary documents. See `/sources/` and `/methodology/` for how this site weighs such
material.
