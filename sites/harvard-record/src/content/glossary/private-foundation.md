In federal tax law, every 501(c)(3) organization is either a public charity or a private foundation.
An organization is a private foundation by default unless it shows that it qualifies as a public
charity, usually through broad public support or through its type, as with schools and hospitals.
The typical private foundation is an endowment created by an individual, a family or a corporation
that makes grants to other charities.

Because a small group controls the money, Congress imposed extra rules. A private foundation must
distribute a minimum amount for charitable purposes each year, set by reference to five percent of
its investment assets. It pays an excise tax on net investment income. It may not engage in
self-dealing transactions with its founders, managers or their families, and it faces limits on
business holdings and risky investments. Its annual return, Form 990-PF, lists its contributors and
each grant it makes, and the public copy includes them.

The word foundation in an organization's name does not settle its tax classification. Many
university-affiliated and community foundations are public charities. Conversely, a private
foundation need not call itself one. To learn which category applies, check the organization's tax
filings or the IRS's public search tool for exempt organizations.
