The difference between clinical-grade and research-grade sequencing lies less in the machines, which
may be identical, than in the system around them. Clinical-grade sequencing is performed in a
laboratory certified to test human samples for medical purposes. The laboratory must validate its
methods, track each sample to guard against mix-ups, employ qualified personnel, pass inspections
and proficiency tests, and issue a signed report with an interpretation.

Research-grade sequencing is performed to answer a scientific question about a group. It is usually
optimized for cost and scale. Sample tracking, depth and confirmation of findings may be adequate
for statistics across many people while falling short of what is needed to make a decision about one
person. Research data are often analyzed without anyone reviewing an individual's results at all.

The consequence is practical. In the United States, results used for diagnosis or treatment
generally must come from a certified laboratory, so a finding from a research study is normally
confirmed in such a laboratory before it is returned to a participant or acted on. "Research-grade"
is not a criticism of the science. It describes fitness for a purpose. A document that mentions
someone's genome being sequenced often does not say which kind it was.
