---
term: "Financial disclosure in research"
short: "The reporting by researchers of their financial interests and sources of support to their institution, funders, ethics boards, journals or study participants."
category: "Research ethics"

Financial disclosure is the mechanism by which research conflicts of interest are brought to light.
A researcher reports interests and support to those who need to weigh them. Several separate
disclosures exist, each with its own audience and rules.

Institutions typically require researchers to report outside income, equity and positions on a
regular schedule and when circumstances change. Funders require applicants to list all current and
pending research support, including support from foreign and private sources. Ethics boards ask
whether investigators have a financial stake in a study, and may require that participants be told.
Journals require authors to state their funding sources and competing interests, which are printed
with the article. Reporting thresholds and definitions vary and are revised from time to time, so
the current policy text should be checked for any particular case.

Each disclosure is only as complete as the person making it. Institutions generally rely on
self-reporting and do not independently audit every statement. The systems are also not connected,
so an interest reported to an employer may not appear in a paper, and the reverse. Funding
acknowledged in a publication shows that a relationship was declared there. Its absence from a
publication does not establish that no support existed.
