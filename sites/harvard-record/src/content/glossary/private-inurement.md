---
term: "Private inurement"
short: "The prohibition, built into federal tax exemption, on any part of a charity's net earnings benefiting an insider such as a founder, trustee or officer."
category: "Law and compliance"
aliases: ["Inurement", "Private benefit", "Inurement prohibition"]
related: ["tax-exempt-status", "excess-benefit-transaction", "self-dealing", "fiduciary-duty", "attorney-general"]
---

To qualify under section 501(c)(3), an organization must be organized and operated so that no part
of its net earnings inures to the benefit of any private shareholder or individual. This is the
inurement prohibition. It targets insiders, people who have a personal stake in the organization or
control over it, who take its money or assets for themselves through unreasonable pay, sweetheart
deals or the use of charitable property.

A related but broader doctrine, private benefit, says a charity must serve public rather than
private interests, and may not confer more than incidental benefits on anyone, insider or not. The
two are often mentioned together. The ultimate penalty for inurement is loss of tax exemption, which
the IRS rarely imposes; the excise taxes on excess benefit transactions were created partly to give
a lesser remedy. State attorneys general can also act to protect charitable assets.

Inurement does not cover ordinary, reasonable salaries or fair contracts, and it does not mean any
benefit a donor receives. A donor who gets recognition or tickets raises separate questions about
the deductible amount of a gift, not about inurement.
