---
term: "Mosaicism"
short: "The presence in one person of two or more populations of cells that differ genetically, because a change arose after conception and was copied into only some cells."
category: "Genetics and biomedical research"
aliases: ["Genetic mosaicism", "Somatic mosaicism", "Germline mosaicism", "Mosaic"]
related: ["germline-and-somatic-variants", "genetic-variant", "sequencing-depth", "cell-line", "dna-sequencing"]
---

A person is mosaic when not all of their cells share the same genome. A variant that arises early in
development may be present in many tissues; one that arises later may be confined to a small patch.
Some well-known conditions can occur in mosaic form, often with milder or patchier effects than
the non-mosaic form. When the change is present in the cells that form eggs or sperm, it is called
germline mosaicism, and it can be passed on even if it is hard to detect in a blood test.

Mosaicism matters for testing. A variant present in only a fraction of cells produces a fraction of
the sequencing reads at that position, so detection depends on sequencing depth and on which tissue
was sampled. A blood sample and a skin sample from the same person can give different results.
Cultured cells can also drift genetically over time, producing mosaic cell populations in the
laboratory.

Mosaicism is not contamination or laboratory error, though a low-level signal can be hard to tell
apart from either. It is also distinct from chimerism, in which a person carries cells derived from
a different individual, such as after a transplant or during pregnancy.
