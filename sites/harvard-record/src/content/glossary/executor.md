---
term: "Executor"
short: "The person or institution named in a will, and confirmed by a court, to administer a deceased person's estate."
category: "Legal procedure"
aliases: ["personal representative", "administrator", "co-executor", "executrix"]
related: ["estate", "plaintiff-and-defendant", "settlement"]
---

An executor carries out a will. After a death, the person named in the will asks the probate court
to admit the will and to confirm the appointment. Once appointed, the executor collects the assets,
notifies creditors, pays valid debts and taxes, handles lawsuits by and against the estate, and
distributes what is left as the will directs. If there is no will, or no named executor is able to
serve, the court appoints an administrator to do the same work. Many states use the single term
personal representative for both.

An executor is a fiduciary. That means a legal duty to act loyally and prudently for the estate and
those entitled to it, to keep accounts, and to avoid self-dealing. The probate court supervises the
work and can remove an executor who breaches those duties. Executors are often family members,
lawyers, business associates or trust companies, and a will can name more than one.

An executor named in a lawsuit is usually sued in a representative capacity. The claim is against
the estate, and any judgment is paid from estate assets. That is different from a claim that the
same person is personally liable for their own conduct, which must be pleaded separately. Serving as
someone's executor does not imply involvement in or knowledge of anything the deceased did.
