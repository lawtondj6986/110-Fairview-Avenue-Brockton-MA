term: "Pro se"
short: "Representing oneself in a legal proceeding without a lawyer; a party who does so is called a pro se litigant."

Pro se is Latin for "on one's own behalf." A pro se litigant files and argues their own case. Anyone
may represent themselves as an individual, though organizations such as corporations and nonprofits
generally must appear through a lawyer. Courts give pro se filings some leniency in form and read
them liberally, but the same rules of law apply.

Pro se cases are common in federal and state courts, and many district courts have clerks or help
desks that assist self-represented parties with procedure. Pro se complaints are often longer, less
organized and more sweeping in their claims than those drafted by lawyers, and a higher share are
dismissed at an early stage.

For a reader, the main point is that a pro se complaint is an allegation like any other, and is not
screened for accuracy by a lawyer before filing. Its claims may be accurate, partly accurate or
unfounded. A dismissal of a pro se case often reflects procedural defects rather than a finding on
the facts. Courts may also restrict filings by a litigant who repeatedly files cases judged
frivolous.
