---
term: "Exome sequencing"
short: "A laboratory method that captures and reads only the protein-coding portions of a person's genes rather than the whole genome."
category: "Genetics and biomedical research"
aliases: ["whole-exome sequencing", "WES"]

Exome sequencing is a method for reading the protein-coding parts of a person's genes. The
laboratory breaks the DNA into fragments, uses molecular "baits" to capture the fragments that come
from exons, and then reads those fragments on a sequencing machine. Software aligns the reads to a
reference genome and lists the places where the person's sequence differs.

In medicine, exome sequencing is used mainly to look for the cause of a suspected inherited
disorder, especially when the symptoms do not point to one specific gene. In research, it is used to
compare many people at lower cost than whole-genome sequencing. A typical result is a list of tens
of thousands of variants, nearly all of them harmless, that must be filtered and interpreted.

Exome sequencing does not read the DNA between genes, and its coverage of the exons themselves is
uneven. It is poor at detecting some kinds of change, such as large rearrangements and expansions of
repeated sequences. A normal exome result lowers the likelihood of some genetic causes. It does not
rule out a genetic condition, and it says little about traits shaped by many genes.
