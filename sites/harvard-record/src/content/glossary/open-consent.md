Open consent is a form of research consent in which participants agree that their data, and
sometimes their biological samples, may be shared widely or published, and accept that they may be
identified. It was developed for genomic research, where conventional promises of confidentiality
are hard to keep because a genome is inherently identifying.

In practice, open consent places more weight on the participant's understanding than a standard
consent does. Projects that use it typically explain in detail the possible harms, such as
identification, discrimination, unwelcome discoveries about family, and unforeseeable future uses.
Some require volunteers to pass a comprehension test. Participants generally keep the right to stop
contributing, but they are told that data already released cannot be withdrawn.

Open consent should be distinguished from two neighbors. Broad consent allows future, unspecified
research uses of stored samples and data, usually under access controls and with confidentiality
protections. Blanket permission with no meaningful disclosure is not valid consent of any kind. Open
consent is also not open access publishing, which concerns who can read a journal article. The model
remains debated, in part because one person's decision exposes information about relatives who were
not asked.
