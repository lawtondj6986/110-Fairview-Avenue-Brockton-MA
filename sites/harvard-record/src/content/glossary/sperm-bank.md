A sperm bank, also called a cryobank, is a facility that collects sperm, tests it, freezes it and
stores it for later use. Donor insemination is the use of sperm from someone other than the
recipient's partner to try to achieve a pregnancy, either by placing sperm in the uterus or through
in vitro fertilization. People also bank their own sperm, for example before medical treatment that
may affect fertility.

In the United States, donated sperm is regulated by the Food and Drug Administration as human
reproductive tissue. Rules require that donors be screened and tested for specified infectious
diseases. Banks commonly add their own medical history review and genetic carrier testing, which
vary in extent. Professional societies publish guidance, including on limiting the number of
families per donor. Rules on donor anonymity, payment and limits differ considerably between
countries.

Screening reduces risks. It does not guarantee a child's health, and catalog details about a donor's
education or talents do not predict a child's traits, most of which are polygenic and shaped by
environment. Past projects that sought donors with exceptional achievements reflected eugenic
thinking and had no sound scientific basis. Consumer DNA testing has also made lasting donor
anonymity difficult to assure.
