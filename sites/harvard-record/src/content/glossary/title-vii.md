Title VII is the main federal law against discrimination in employment. It bars employers with
fifteen or more employees from discriminating in hiring, firing, pay, promotion and other terms of
employment because of race, color, religion, sex or national origin. Sexual harassment at work is
treated as a form of sex discrimination under the statute, and retaliation for complaining is
separately prohibited.

Universities are employers, so Title VII covers faculty, staff, and student workers to the extent
they are employees. A person who believes the law was violated must normally file a charge with the
U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, or a partner state agency, within a fixed period
before suing. The agency investigates, may attempt conciliation, and issues a notice of right to
sue.

Title VII and Title IX overlap but are not the same. Title VII protects employees and applies
whether or not the employer receives federal money. Title IX protects students and employees alike
but only in federally funded education programs, and it covers sex alone. A harassment complaint by
a university employee can raise both. Whether a graduate student counts as an employee for a given
purpose is a recurring and fact-dependent question.
