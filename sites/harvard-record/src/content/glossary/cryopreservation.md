Cryopreservation is the preservation of living cells or tissue by cooling them to very low
temperatures, typically in or above liquid nitrogen at around minus 196 degrees Celsius. At that
temperature biological activity effectively stops. Cells are frozen in a solution containing a
protective chemical that limits damage from ice crystals, and they are cooled at a controlled rate.
Properly stored cells can remain viable for decades.

Cell banking is the organized use of this technique. A laboratory grows a batch of cells, divides it
into many small vials, freezes them, and records what each vial contains and where it is kept. Vials
can later be thawed to restart a culture or shipped to another laboratory. The same approach is used
for sperm, eggs, embryos, cord blood and research cell lines.

Two confusions are common. Freezing cells is different from freezing extracted DNA, which is a
stable chemical and needs less demanding storage. And cryopreservation of cells is an established
laboratory method, while the freezing of whole human bodies or brains after death in the hope of
future revival, sometimes called cryonics, has no demonstrated means of revival and is not an
accepted medical practice.
