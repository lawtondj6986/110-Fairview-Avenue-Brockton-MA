---
term: "Civil versus criminal"
short: "A criminal case is brought by the government to punish an offense. A civil case is brought by a private party or the government to obtain compensation or a court order. The two differ in purpose, standard of proof and outcome."
category: "Legal procedure"
aliases: ["civil case", "criminal case", "civil liability", "civil suit"]
related: ["burden-of-proof", "preponderance-of-the-evidence", "conviction", "plaintiff-and-defendant", "statute-of-limitations"]
---

The legal system handles wrongdoing through two separate tracks. A criminal case is prosecuted by
the government in the name of the public. The possible outcomes are conviction or acquittal, and a
conviction can bring imprisonment, probation, fines and a criminal record. The prosecution must
prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, and the defendant has constitutional protections such as the
right to counsel and the right not to testify.

A civil case is usually brought by the person or organization claiming harm. The outcomes are a
finding that the defendant is liable or not liable, followed by money damages or an order to act or
refrain. The usual standard is preponderance of the evidence. The decision to sue belongs to the
plaintiff, whereas the decision to prosecute belongs to the prosecutor, not to the victim.

The same events can give rise to both, and the results need not match. A person can be acquitted, or
never charged, and still be held liable in a civil suit, because the standards of proof and the
rules differ.

University disciplinary proceedings are a third track. They are administrative, apply the
institution's own policies, and can lead to sanctions such as suspension or dismissal, but they
produce neither a conviction nor a civil judgment.
