A correction is a formal acknowledgment that something published was wrong, together with the right
information. News organizations append corrections to articles or run them in a designated space.
Scholarly journals publish them as separate, citable notices linked to the original article. Some
journals use erratum for an error introduced in production and corrigendum for an author's error. A
clarification addresses wording that was not strictly wrong but was misleading or incomplete.

A proper correction states what was wrong and what is right, is dated, and is attached to the
corrected item so that later readers see it. Editing a published text without notice is sometimes
called stealth editing and is regarded as poor practice, because readers and anyone who cited the
earlier version cannot tell what changed. This site records corrections in its update log and does
not make them silently.

A correction is not a retraction. It signals that the work as a whole stands and a specific part has
been fixed. It is also not a mark against the publisher. Outlets that publish corrections are
generally more reliable than those that never do, since errors occur everywhere and the difference
lies in whether they are acknowledged. When relying on an article, it is worth checking whether a
correction has been attached since it was first read.
