Re-identification risk is the likelihood that a person can be identified from data that were
supposed to be de-identified. The usual route is linkage: combining the dataset with other
information, such as public records, social media or another database, until only one person fits.

Genetic data carry particular risks, and several have been demonstrated in published research. A
genome is unique. Surnames can sometimes be inferred from markers on the Y chromosome using
genealogy databases, and combined with age and place of residence this has been enough to identify
research participants. Consumer genealogy databases allow a person to be found through relatives who
have tested, even if that person never did. Details such as birth date, sex and postal code are also
surprisingly identifying in combination.

The risk is real but should not be overstated. Re-identification usually takes effort, skill and
access to auxiliary data, and it is prohibited by the agreements under which most research data are
shared. The sound conclusion is that anonymity of genetic data cannot be guaranteed, not that any
dataset can be trivially unmasked. Some projects respond by restricting access, others by telling
participants plainly that privacy cannot be promised.
