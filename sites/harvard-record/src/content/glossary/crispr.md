CRISPR is a family of tools for changing DNA at a chosen location. It is adapted from a system that
bacteria use to defend themselves against viruses. The best-known version, CRISPR-Cas9, has two
parts: an enzyme (Cas9) that cuts DNA, and a short guide RNA that matches the target sequence and
leads the enzyme to it. After the cut, the cell's own repair machinery rejoins the DNA, which can
disable a gene or, with a supplied template, rewrite it.

Since it was shown to work as a programmable editing tool in 2012, CRISPR has become a standard
laboratory method because it is cheaper and easier to design than earlier techniques. Newer
variants, called base editors and prime editors, change individual letters without cutting both
strands of the DNA. The first CRISPR-based medicine was approved by regulators in 2023, for certain
inherited blood disorders, and other treatments are in clinical trials.

CRISPR is not perfectly precise. It can cut at unintended sites, produce unintended changes at the
intended site, and edit only some of the cells it is applied to. Delivering it to the right cells in
a living person remains a major practical obstacle. The existence of the tool does not mean that any
given trait can be edited.
