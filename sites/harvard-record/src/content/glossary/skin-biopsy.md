term: "Skin biopsy"

A skin biopsy removes a small piece of skin for examination or study. The usual research method is a
punch biopsy. After a local anesthetic, a circular blade a few millimeters wide takes a small core
of skin, typically from the arm or another inconspicuous site. The wound heals in one to two weeks
and may leave a small scar. The procedure is done by a clinician and carries minor risks such as
bleeding and infection.

In clinical care, a biopsy is taken to diagnose a skin condition. In research, the purpose is often
different: to obtain living cells. Fibroblasts, a type of connective tissue cell, grow out of the
skin fragment in a culture dish and can be multiplied, frozen, studied, or reprogrammed into stem
cells.

A skin biopsy is therefore a more consequential sample than a saliva kit. Saliva provides DNA that
can be read. A biopsy provides living cells that can be expanded into a renewable supply, stored for
decades and shared between laboratories. In a research setting the consent form should say what the
cells may be used for, who may receive them and how long they will be kept.
