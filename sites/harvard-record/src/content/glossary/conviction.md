A conviction is the legal result of a criminal case in which guilt is established. It comes about in
two ways. The defendant pleads guilty or no contest and the court accepts the plea, or a jury or
judge finds the defendant guilty at trial, where the prosecution must prove each element of the
offense beyond a reasonable doubt. The court then enters a judgment of conviction and imposes a
sentence.

A conviction is specific to particular offenses. A person charged with several counts may be
convicted of some, acquitted of others, or plead to a lesser charge while the rest are dismissed.
The conviction establishes guilt only for the counts in the judgment. It may later be reversed on
appeal, vacated, or in some jurisdictions expunged or pardoned, and each of those has a distinct
legal meaning.

Precision matters when describing criminal history. Arrested, charged, indicted and convicted are
different stages, and only the last is a determination of guilt. It is not accurate to describe a
person as convicted of conduct that was alleged but not part of the judgment. An acquittal means the
prosecution did not meet its burden. It is not a finding that the events did not occur.
