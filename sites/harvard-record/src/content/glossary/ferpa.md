FERPA is a 1974 federal law that applies to schools receiving funds from the U.S. Department of
Education. It gives students the right to inspect their education records, to ask for corrections,
and to control most disclosures of personally identifiable information from those records. The
rights belong to parents until a student turns eighteen or enrolls in a postsecondary institution,
and to the student after that.

The law has many exceptions. A school may disclose records without consent to school officials with
a legitimate educational interest, in response to a subpoena or court order, in a health or safety
emergency, and in several other situations. It may also release directory information, such as name
and dates of attendance, unless the student has opted out. A school may tell a victim of an alleged
violent crime or sex offense the final result of a disciplinary proceeding against the accused
student.

FERPA is often cited as a reason records cannot be released, and sometimes more broadly than the law
requires. It covers student records, not records about employees, donors or visitors. It does not
create a right to sue. Enforcement is by the Department of Education, and the formal penalty is loss
of federal funding.
