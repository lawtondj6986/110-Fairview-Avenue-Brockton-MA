A base rate is the background frequency of something in a relevant population. Before deciding what
a particular observation means, it helps to ask how often that observation occurs anyway. Base rate
neglect is the common error of judging by the specific detail alone.

The standard illustration comes from medical testing. If a condition is rare, most positive results
from even a fairly accurate test are false alarms, because the few true cases are outnumbered by
errors among the many people who do not have the condition. The same logic applies to reading
records. Suppose a wealthy donor's contact list includes a number of scientists. How notable that is
depends on how many prominent scientists appear in the contact lists of major donors in general,
which may be a great many. An email from a fundraiser to a prospective donor, a thank-you letter, or
a meeting request is routine in university life, and its presence in a file is weak evidence of
anything unusual.

Base rates are often unknown, and a reference work should not invent them. The useful habit is to
ask the question: compared with what? This site's `/comparison/` page exists for that reason. A base
rate does not explain away a specific documented fact. It calibrates how much weight an ambiguous
one can bear.
