A capital campaign is a concentrated fundraising drive that runs for a fixed number of years toward
an announced goal. The name originally referred to campaigns for capital projects such as buildings
and endowment. Most university campaigns today are comprehensive, meaning they count nearly all
gifts received during the campaign period, for any purpose.

A campaign is planned well in advance. Leaders set priorities with deans and the governing board,
test the goal through a feasibility study, recruit volunteer leadership from among trustees and
major donors, and raise a large share of the goal privately before any announcement. After a public
launch, the campaign continues for several years with events, publicity and widened solicitation,
and ends with a final announced total.

Campaign totals need careful reading. They generally include pledges not yet paid and may include
revocable bequest intentions, which are counted under rules the institution chooses and should
disclose. A total is a measure of commitments, not cash available. Campaigns also raise the pressure
on an institution to find large gifts within a deadline, and they give leaders and fundraisers
personal targets. That context is relevant when assessing how carefully prospects were examined
during a campaign, though a campaign does not by itself indicate that standards were lowered.
