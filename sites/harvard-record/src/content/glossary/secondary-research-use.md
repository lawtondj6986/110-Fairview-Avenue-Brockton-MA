term: "Secondary research use"
short: "The use of data or biological samples that were collected for one purpose, such as clinical care or an earlier study, in new research not planned when they were collected."

Secondary research use means doing research with information or specimens that already exist.
Examples include analyzing medical records, reusing samples stored in a biobank, or sequencing
blood collected during an earlier study. Much biomedical research depends on it, because collecting
new samples for every question would be slow and costly.

The Common Rule treats secondary use in several ways. Research using information or specimens that
are not identifiable to the researcher may fall outside the definition of human subjects research.
Some secondary research with identifiable material can qualify for an exemption under specified
conditions, including where participants gave broad consent for future use. Otherwise an
institutional review board must review the research and may waive consent if criteria are met. HIPAA
and state laws add separate conditions for health information and genetic data.

Secondary use is not inherently improper. The ethical questions are whether the original consent
covers the new use, how well privacy is protected, and whether participants would reasonably expect
it. A study described as using "existing samples" may still have needed review and consent, so the
approval documents are worth checking.
