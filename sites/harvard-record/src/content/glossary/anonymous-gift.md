An anonymous gift is one the institution agrees not to attribute publicly. Anonymity has degrees,
and the word is used for all of them.

In the most common form, the donor is known to the development office and senior officials and is
simply left out of donor lists, announcements and naming. In a stricter form, knowledge is limited
to a handful of named staff, and the database record is flagged or restricted so that other
employees cannot see it. In the rarest form, the gift arrives through a lawyer, bank, donor-advised
fund or other intermediary, and the institution itself does not know the ultimate source. Many gift
acceptance policies discourage or prohibit that last form, because the institution cannot vet a
donor it cannot identify.

Donors ask for anonymity for many ordinary reasons, including modesty, privacy, religious conviction
and a wish to avoid further requests. Anonymity can also serve an institution's interest when it
expects criticism for an association. The two cases look identical in the public record. For that
reason, "anonymous" should be read narrowly: it describes what was disclosed, not what was known
inside. The questions to ask are who within the institution knew the donor's identity, who asked for
anonymity, and whether anonymous treatment was the donor's wish or the institution's.
