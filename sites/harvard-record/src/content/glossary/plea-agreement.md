A plea agreement is a negotiated resolution of a criminal case. The defendant agrees to plead
guilty, or in some courts no contest, to one or more charges. The prosecution typically agrees to
drop other charges, to charge a lesser offense, or to recommend a particular sentence. The large
majority of criminal convictions in the United States come from pleas, not trials.

A judge must accept the plea before it takes effect. In federal court, the judge questions the
defendant in open court to confirm that the plea is voluntary, that the defendant understands the
rights being given up, and that there is a factual basis for the plea. The judge is often not bound
by the sentencing recommendation. Many agreements include a written statement of facts that the
defendant admits, and a waiver of most rights to appeal.

A guilty plea produces a conviction with the same legal force as a jury verdict. At the same time,
the charges in a plea agreement are the product of negotiation, so they may not reflect everything
that was alleged or investigated. Dropped charges are not findings of innocence, and they are not
findings of guilt. A no contest plea leads to conviction but generally cannot be used as an
admission in a later civil lawsuit.
