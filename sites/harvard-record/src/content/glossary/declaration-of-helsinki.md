The Declaration of Helsinki is a set of ethical principles for medical research on people, including
research on identifiable human material and data. It was adopted in 1964 by the World Medical
Association, an international body representing physicians, and built on the Nuremberg Code that
followed the trials of Nazi doctors. It has been revised a number of times, so references should
specify the version.

Its core commitments are that the well-being and rights of the individual participant take
precedence over the interests of science and society, that research follow a written protocol
reviewed by an independent ethics committee, that participants give voluntary informed consent, that
risks be assessed and justified, and that vulnerable groups receive special protection. Later
versions added provisions on registering studies in public databases, publishing negative as well as
positive results, disclosing funding and conflicts of interest, the use of placebos, and access to
beneficial interventions after a trial.

The Declaration is addressed primarily to physicians and is not itself legally binding. It gains
force indirectly, because national laws, research funders, ethics committees and medical journals
incorporate or refer to it. It is one of several international ethics documents. U.S. federal
regulations are based on their own framework and do not simply enact it.
