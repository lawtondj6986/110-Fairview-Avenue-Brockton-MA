Longevity research studies the biology of aging and asks whether the aging process can be slowed so
that people stay healthy longer. It is a legitimate scientific field, and it also attracts a great
deal of speculation. The two should be kept apart.

Some findings are well established. Changes in single genes can substantially extend lifespan in
worms, flies and mice. Reducing calorie intake without malnutrition extends lifespan in many
laboratory animals. Certain drugs, such as rapamycin, extend lifespan in mice in repeated studies.
In humans, not smoking, physical activity, blood pressure control and similar measures clearly
reduce disease and early death. Human lifespan is only modestly heritable.

What is not established is that any drug, supplement, gene therapy, cell treatment or blood-based
procedure slows aging or extends lifespan in humans. Candidates under study include drugs that act
on nutrient-sensing pathways, compounds that clear aged cells, and partial cellular reprogramming.
Trials are difficult because lifespan takes decades to measure, so studies rely on indirect markers
whose link to lifespan is itself unproven. Claims about reversing aging, radical life extension, or
preserving or "upgrading" a person through their DNA or frozen tissue go beyond current evidence.
