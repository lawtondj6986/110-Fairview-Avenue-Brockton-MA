An endowment is money that an institution invests for the long term and spends only in part each
year. It is not one account. A university endowment is typically made up of thousands of individual
funds, each created by a gift and many carrying a restriction set by the original donor.

The funds are usually invested together, with each fund holding a share of the pool. Each year the
governing board approves a spending rate under a written spending policy, and that distribution
supports the purpose of each fund: a professorship, scholarships, a library, or general operations.
In most U.S. states, the management and spending of charitable endowments is governed by a version
of the Uniform Prudent Management of Institutional Funds Act, which requires prudent investment and
attention to donor intent and to preserving purchasing power.

A large endowment total is often read as money the university can spend as it likes. Much of it is
restricted to particular purposes or schools, and the principal of a true endowment is meant to stay
invested. The term also differs from quasi-endowment, which is money the board has chosen to invest
alongside the endowment but could legally spend. Endowment figures in public reports seldom show
which funds are which.
