A confidential resource is someone a member of the campus community can talk to without triggering a
report to the institution. Typical examples are licensed mental health counselors, medical
providers, clergy acting in a pastoral role, and staff of a sexual assault resource center or ombuds
office whom the institution has designated as confidential. Institutions publish lists of these
resources so that people can choose whom to approach.

The basis for confidentiality varies. Some resources are covered by a legal privilege or
professional duty, such as the one between therapist and patient, which limits disclosure even in
court. Others are confidential only by institutional policy, meaning the institution has agreed that
they need not report to the Title IX coordinator. The second kind offers real privacy within the
institution but may not protect records from a subpoena.

Confidentiality has limits everywhere. State laws commonly require reporting of suspected child
abuse and allow or require disclosure when there is an imminent risk of serious harm. Some
confidential resources still provide anonymous, non-identifying counts for Clery Act statistics.

Speaking to a confidential resource does not put the institution on notice. No investigation will
begin and no protective steps that require official action will follow unless the person also
reports through a non-confidential channel.
