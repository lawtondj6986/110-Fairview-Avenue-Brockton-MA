A research protocol is the written plan that governs a study. It states the question, the background
and rationale, who may take part and how they will be recruited, what will be done to or asked of
them, what samples and data will be collected, how the data will be analyzed, and how risks will be
handled. In human research it also describes the consent process, confidentiality protections and
plans for storing or sharing samples and data.

The protocol is the central document in ethics review. An ethics board approves a specific version,
and the researchers are obliged to follow it. Any change, such as adding a procedure, a new category
of participant or a new use for samples, requires an approved amendment before it is put into
effect, except where an immediate change is needed to protect participants. Clinical trial protocols
are often registered publicly so that the planned design can later be compared with what is
reported.

A protocol is a plan and a commitment. It is not evidence of what actually happened. An activity
that the protocol does not describe is not covered by the approval, however closely related it may
seem. The word is also used loosely in laboratories to mean a recipe for a technique, which is a
different thing.
