---
term: "Immortalized cell line"
short: "A cell line that can divide indefinitely in the laboratory, either because it came from a tumor or because it was deliberately altered."
category: "Genetics and biomedical research"
aliases: ["continuous cell line", "lymphoblastoid cell line"]

An immortalized cell line is a population of cells that keeps dividing in culture without the limit
that stops normal cells. Normal human cells stop dividing after a number of generations, in part
because the protective ends of their chromosomes, called telomeres, shorten with each division.
Immortalized cells have escaped that limit.

Some lines are immortal because they were derived from cancers. Others are made deliberately. Common
laboratory methods include adding the gene for telomerase, an enzyme that maintains telomeres,
introducing certain viral genes that override the cell's controls on division, or, for white blood
cells, infecting them with Epstein-Barr virus to produce what is called a lymphoblastoid cell line.
Biobanks have long used the last method to turn a blood sample into a renewable source of a donor's
DNA.

Immortal refers to the cell population in the dish. It has no bearing on the lifespan of the donor
and is not a longevity treatment. Immortalization also changes the cells, which may behave
differently from normal tissue and drift genetically over time. The practical point is that an
immortalized line is an effectively unlimited supply of one person's cells and DNA, which makes
consent and control over distribution important.
