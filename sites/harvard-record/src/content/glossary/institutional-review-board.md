An institutional review board (IRB) is a committee that reviews proposed research involving people
before it starts and monitors it while it continues. In the United States, federal regulations
require IRB review for most human subjects research that is federally funded or that involves
products regulated by the Food and Drug Administration. Many institutions apply the same requirement
to all their research. Other countries use similar bodies, usually called research ethics
committees.

Under the federal rules an IRB has at least five members and must include a scientist, a
non-scientist and someone not otherwise affiliated with the institution. The board examines the
protocol and consent materials. To approve a study it must find, among other things, that risks are
minimized and reasonable in relation to expected benefits, that participants are selected fairly,
that informed consent will be obtained and documented, and that privacy is protected. It can approve
a study, require changes, or disapprove it, and it can suspend approved research.

An IRB reviews what it is shown. It does not usually audit laboratories, verify funding sources or
judge scientific merit beyond what bears on risk. Approval means the plan met the standards. It does
not confirm that the study was carried out as approved.
