Pharmacogenomics studies how genetic variation influences the way people respond to drugs. Much of
the effect runs through genes for the liver enzymes that break drugs down. Depending on their
variants, people may process a given drug slowly, so that it accumulates and causes side effects, or
quickly, so that it is cleared before it can work. Other variants affect drug targets or the risk of
rare severe reactions.

This is one of the areas where genetic testing has clear, accepted clinical uses. Examples include
testing before prescribing certain drugs for HIV, seizures, cancer and blood clotting, where a
specific variant predicts either a dangerous reaction or a need for a different dose. Drug labels
for many medicines include pharmacogenomic information, and expert groups publish guidelines that
translate test results into prescribing advice.

The field is narrower than its promotion sometimes suggests. Well-validated gene and drug pairs
exist for a limited number of medicines. For most drugs, age, kidney and liver function, other
medications and adherence matter more than genotype. Broad consumer panels that claim to select the
best drug, notably for psychiatric conditions, have weaker evidence. Results should be interpreted
by a prescriber or pharmacist, not acted on alone.
