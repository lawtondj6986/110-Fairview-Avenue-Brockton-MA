DNA sequencing is the process of determining the order of the bases (A, C, G and T) in a piece of
DNA. The term covers a family of technologies. Older methods read one fragment at a time and are
still used to confirm individual results. Modern high-throughput methods, often called
next-generation sequencing, read millions of short fragments in parallel. Newer long-read methods
read much longer fragments, which helps with repetitive regions.

Sequencing has several stages. DNA is extracted from a sample and prepared, the machine generates
raw reads, and software aligns the reads to a reference and identifies differences. A person or
program then interprets those differences. Errors can enter at each stage, so quality controls and
sufficient depth matter.

Sequencing should be distinguished from genotyping. Sequencing reads every base in the targeted
region and can find variants nobody has seen before. Genotyping checks a preselected list of known
positions. The word is also used loosely. "We sequenced the sample" may refer to a whole genome, an
exome, a panel of a few genes or a single gene, and a reader should look for which one is meant.
