---
term: "Link rot"
short: "The tendency of web links to stop working over time as pages are moved, renamed or deleted; content that changes at the same address is called content drift."
category: "Records and sourcing"

Link rot is the gradual failure of hyperlinks. A link rots when the page it points to is deleted,
the site is reorganized, the domain lapses, or the material is placed behind a paywall or login. A
related problem is content drift: the address still works, but the page now says something different
from what it said when it was cited.

Studies of scholarly articles, court opinions and news sites have repeatedly found that a
substantial share of links stop working within a few years. A citation consisting only of a web
address is therefore fragile. Standard countermeasures are to cite stable identifiers where they
exist, such as an official docket number, a statutory citation or a persistent identifier for a
journal article, to record the title, author, publisher and date so the item can be found again, to
save an archive snapshot, and to note the date on which the link was last checked.

A dead link does not mean the source was invented or retracted. Most link rot results from ordinary
website maintenance. It also does not mean that the claim it supported is now unsupported, only that
the source must be found another way. When a page is deliberately removed or altered, that is a
distinct fact, and it should be established with evidence such as dated snapshots.
