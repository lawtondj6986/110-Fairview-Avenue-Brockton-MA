---
term: "Optical character recognition (OCR)"
short: "Software that converts images of text, such as scanned pages or photographs of documents, into machine-readable text that can be searched and copied."
category: "Records and sourcing"

Optical character recognition, usually called OCR, reads the shapes of letters in an image and
produces text. A scanned PDF often has an invisible OCR text layer behind the page image, which is
what lets a reader search it. Large document releases are usually processed with OCR before
publication, and search engines for such releases rely on it.

OCR is imperfect. Poor scans, handwriting, unusual fonts, tables, stamps, redaction boxes and
low-contrast copies all cause errors, such as the letter l read as the numeral 1, or rn read as m.
Names and numbers are especially vulnerable. As a result, a keyword search across an OCR text layer
can miss documents that contain the word, and can produce false hits.

The practical rule is that OCR text is a finding aid, not a quotation source. Before quoting or
relying on a passage, check it against the page image. Counts of how often a word appears in a
collection are only as good as the OCR behind them. Poorly done redaction can also leave the
hidden text in an OCR layer, which is a reason the redaction itself, not only the image, should be
checked.
