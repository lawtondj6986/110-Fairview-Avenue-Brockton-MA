---
term: "Dismissal with prejudice"
short: "A dismissal that ends a claim permanently, so that the same claim cannot be filed again. A dismissal without prejudice leaves the plaintiff free to refile."
category: "Legal procedure"
aliases: ["with prejudice", "without prejudice", "dismissal without prejudice"]
related: ["voluntary-dismissal", "motion-to-dismiss", "settlement", "amended-complaint"]
---

Prejudice, in this phrase, means legal harm to the right to bring the claim again. A dismissal with
prejudice is final. It operates as a judgment against the plaintiff on that claim and bars a new
lawsuit on the same claim against the same defendant. A dismissal without prejudice ends the current
case but leaves the claim alive, so the plaintiff may file again if time limits allow.

Both kinds arise in several ways. A court that grants a motion to dismiss may do so without
prejudice and allow an amended complaint if the defect looks curable, or with prejudice if it does
not. Dismissals for lack of jurisdiction are ordinarily without prejudice, since the court has no
power to decide the merits. Parties who settle usually agree to a dismissal with prejudice, because
the defendant wants assurance that the dispute is over.

The phrase describes legal finality and does not explain the reason. A dismissal with prejudice may
reflect a judge's ruling that the claim fails as a matter of law, or simply the last step of a
settlement in which the plaintiff was paid. To know which, read the order or stipulation and the
entries before it on the docket. Neither form of dismissal is a factual finding about whether the
alleged events took place.
