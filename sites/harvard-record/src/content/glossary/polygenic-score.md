A polygenic score combines information from many genetic variants into one number. For each variant
linked to a trait in large studies, the person's genotype is multiplied by that variant's estimated
effect, and the results are summed. When the trait is a disease, the result is often called a
polygenic risk score.

Scores are useful mainly at the level of groups. People in the top few percent of a score for
coronary heart disease, for example, have a measurably higher average risk than people in the
middle. Researchers are testing whether such scores can improve screening and prevention when added
to ordinary risk factors such as age, blood pressure and family history.

For an individual, a polygenic score is a weak predictor. It shifts a probability and does not
determine an outcome, and most people with a high score for a condition will not have a dramatically
different life from those with an average one. Scores also perform less well in people whose
ancestry differs from that of the study populations used to build them, which have been
predominantly European. A score describes a statistical tendency in comparison with a reference
group. It is not a diagnosis, and scores for behavioral or cognitive traits are especially limited.
