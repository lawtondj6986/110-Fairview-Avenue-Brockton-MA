A second-hand account comes from a person who learned of something from someone else instead of
observing it. "She told me he attended" is second-hand as to the attendance. In everyday speech this
is called hearsay. Each further link in the chain, a third-hand account and so on, adds more room
for error.

Second-hand accounts are weaker than direct ones for understandable reasons. The reader cannot
assess the original observer. Details shift in retelling. The person relaying the account may have
misunderstood, may remember poorly, or may add interpretation without marking it. Such accounts
still have uses. They can point to who has direct knowledge, they can show that a claim was
circulating at a given time, and they may be the only record available. The sound practice is to
label them plainly, identify the chain as far as it is known, and look for the first-hand source or
a document.

The sourcing sense of hearsay should not be confused with the hearsay rule in evidence law. That
rule governs which out-of-court statements a court may admit, and it has many technical exceptions.
A statement can be inadmissible in court yet reliable, or admissible yet weak. Calling an account
second-hand in a reference work is a description of how the information travelled. It is not a legal
conclusion.
