---
term: "Consent judgment"
short: "A final court judgment whose terms the parties have agreed on, combining a settlement with the enforceability of a court order."
category: "Legal procedure"
aliases: ["agreed judgment", "stipulated judgment", "judgment by consent"]
related: ["consent-decree", "settlement", "without-admission-of-liability", "attorney-general", "injunction"]
---

A consent judgment is a judgment entered by a court at the request of the parties, on terms they
negotiated. It ends the lawsuit like any other final judgment. The difference is that the judge has
not decided the disputed issues. The judge reviews the proposal, usually for basic fairness and
legality, and signs it.

Parties choose this form over a private settlement when they want the court's enforcement power. If
a party fails to pay or to do what the judgment requires, the other side can return to the same
court to enforce it, without filing a new suit for breach of contract. State attorneys general and
federal agencies frequently resolve enforcement actions through consent judgments, which typically
provide for payments, restitution to affected people, and changes in the defendant's practices.

A consent judgment usually states that the defendant does not admit the allegations. In that case it
establishes the obligations the defendant accepted and nothing about whether the alleged conduct
occurred. Some consent judgments do include stipulated facts or admissions, so the text must be
read.

The term overlaps with consent decree. Usage varies by court and by area of law, and the two are
often interchangeable. Decree tends to be used where long-term supervised conduct is required.
