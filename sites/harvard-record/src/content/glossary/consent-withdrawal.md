---
term: "Consent withdrawal"
short: "A research participant's decision to stop taking part in a study, or to stop future use of their data or samples, which federal rules say can be made at any time without penalty."
category: "Research ethics"

Under the Common Rule, a consent form must tell participants that taking part is voluntary and that
they may stop at any time without penalty or loss of benefits they are otherwise entitled to. Consent
withdrawal is the exercise of that right. A participant may leave a study entirely, decline a
particular procedure, or ask that stored samples and data not be used for further research.

What happens to material already collected depends on the study's consent form, institutional
policy and the applicable rules. Researchers can usually stop collecting new data and stop future
use of identifiable samples. Data already analyzed, included in published results or shared with
other researchers often cannot be recalled. For studies regulated by the Food and Drug
Administration, guidance generally allows data collected before withdrawal to be kept to protect the
integrity of the study. The consent form should explain these limits.

Withdrawing consent is different from never having given valid consent, which raises questions
about the original study. It is also distinct from a participant's rights under health privacy law
to revoke an authorization, which follow their own rules.
