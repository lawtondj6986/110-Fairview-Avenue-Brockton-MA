---
term: "Preemption"
short: "The principle that valid federal law overrides conflicting state or local law."
category: "Law and compliance"

Preemption comes from the Supremacy Clause of the U.S. Constitution, which makes federal law the
supreme law of the land. When a state or local law conflicts with a valid federal statute or
regulation, the federal law prevails and the conflicting rule cannot be enforced.

Courts recognize several forms. Express preemption occurs when a federal statute says in so many
words that it displaces state law on a subject. Field preemption occurs when federal regulation is
so thorough that it leaves no room for state rules. Conflict preemption occurs when a person cannot
comply with both laws, or when the state law stands as an obstacle to the federal law's purpose.
Federal regulations can also state their own preemptive effect.

Many federal laws relevant to universities set a floor, not a ceiling. The HIPAA privacy rules, for
example, leave in place state laws that protect health information more strictly. Federal civil
rights and campus safety laws likewise coexist with state statutes that add duties, such as state
rules on consent definitions or on public records. As a result, compliance at one institution can
differ from another because of state law, and a federal rule is not always the last word on what a
school must do.
