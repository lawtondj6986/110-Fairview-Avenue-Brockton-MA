---
term: "Internal review and external review"
short: "An internal review is conducted by the institution's own staff or faculty, while an external review is carried out by people from outside it."
category: "University governance"
aliases: ["Internal review vs. external review", "Internal investigation", "External review", "Outside review"]

---

When something goes wrong, an institution chooses who will look into it. The basic choice is between
its own people and outsiders.

An internal review is run by employees: the general counsel's office, internal audit, a human
resources or compliance office, or a committee of faculty and administrators. It is quicker and
cheaper, and the reviewers know the institution and can obtain records easily. An external review is
assigned to outsiders, usually a law firm, sometimes a consulting firm or a panel of people from
other institutions. Outsiders bring distance and specialized experience, and their conclusions tend
to carry more public credibility.

The labels describe who does the work, not how independent it is. An external firm is hired, paid
and given its scope by the institution, and it may have other business with the same client. An
internal committee of tenured faculty may in practice be freer to criticize leadership than a
retained firm. The questions that matter are who commissioned the review, who the reviewers report
to, who defined the scope, whether they had full access to records and people, and who controls
release of the findings. Reviews of both kinds should also be distinguished from periodic academic
reviews of departments, which evaluate scholarly quality and are unrelated to misconduct.
