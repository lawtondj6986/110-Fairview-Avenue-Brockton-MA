In the fundraising context, a conflict of interest arises when someone who influences how the
institution deals with a donor also stands to gain or lose personally from the outcome.

Several patterns recur. A trustee who is also a major donor may take part in decisions that affect a
program they fund. A faculty member may receive research support, personal payments, investments or
hospitality from a donor while also advising the institution about that donor. An institution itself
can have a conflict when its financial interest in a gift could bias its research or its handling of
a complaint. Universities address these through conflict of interest policies that require periodic
written disclosure of outside financial interests and relationships, review by a designated office
or committee, and management steps such as recusal from the decision.

Having a conflict is not misconduct. Conflicts are common, and the usual expectation is disclosure
and management, not avoidance of every tie. Problems arise when a conflict is concealed or when the
conflicted person makes the decision anyway. Most disclosure forms focus on defined financial
interests above stated thresholds, so personal benefits from a donor, such as travel, gifts or
introductions, may fall outside what a form asks. A clean disclosure record therefore shows only
that nothing reportable was reported.
