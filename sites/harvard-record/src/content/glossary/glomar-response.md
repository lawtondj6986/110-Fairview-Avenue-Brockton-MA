term: "Glomar response"
short: "A reply to a records request in which an agency refuses to confirm or deny whether the requested records exist at all, because saying so would itself reveal protected information."
category: "Law and compliance"

A Glomar response is a specific kind of refusal under the federal Freedom of Information Act. Instead
of saying "we found records and are withholding them" or "we found nothing," the agency says it will
neither confirm nor deny that responsive records exist. The name comes from a 1970s request about a
ship called the Glomar Explorer, in which the government gave this kind of answer.

Agencies use the response when the existence of a record is itself sensitive. A common example is a
request for records about a named private person in a law enforcement file: confirming that such
records exist could reveal that the person was investigated. The agency must tie the response to a
FOIA exemption, and a requester can challenge it on appeal and in court, for example by showing that
the agency has already officially acknowledged the records.

A Glomar response is not an admission that records exist, and it is not a statement that they do
not. It should be reported as exactly what it is. Readers sometimes treat it as a hint; that
inference is not supported by the response alone.
