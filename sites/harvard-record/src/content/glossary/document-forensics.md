Document forensics asks whether a document is what it claims to be. For paper records, trained
examiners look at handwriting, signatures, ink, paper, printing methods and signs of alteration.
For digital records, analysts examine metadata such as creation and modification dates, the
software that produced a file, embedded fonts, revision history and, for email, the routing
information in message headers and cryptographic signatures added by sending servers.

Most research does not need a forensic laboratory. Simpler checks catch many problems: whether fonts
and formatting match other documents from the same source and period, whether dates fall on the
right weekdays, whether names and titles were correct at the time, and whether the document's
provenance can be traced. When stakes are high, a formal examination by a qualified examiner may be
needed.

Forensic findings speak to authenticity, not to truth. A genuine document can contain false
statements, and a forged one can repeat accurate facts. Metadata can also be wrong for innocent
reasons, such as a computer with an incorrect clock or a file copied between systems. Conclusions
should state the checks done and their limits.
