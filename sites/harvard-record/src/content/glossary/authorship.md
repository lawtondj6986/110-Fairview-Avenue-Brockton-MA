Authorship of a scientific paper signifies both credit for the work and accountability for it.
Conventions differ between fields, but widely adopted criteria in biomedical publishing set four
conditions for an author: a substantial contribution to the conception or design of the work or to
the acquisition, analysis or interpretation of data; drafting the paper or revising it critically;
approval of the final version; and agreement to be accountable for the work. People who helped in
other ways are named in the acknowledgments.

In many laboratory sciences the order of names carries meaning. The first author usually did most of
the hands-on work, and the last is often the senior scientist who led the group. A corresponding
author handles communication with the journal and readers. Many journals now publish a statement of
each author's specific contribution.

Three misuses are recognized. Guest or honorary authorship lists someone who did not meet the
criteria, such as a department head or a funder. Ghost authorship omits someone who did, for example
a paid writer. Coercive authorship is demanded by a person with power. Providing funding, space or
materials does not by itself merit authorship. Conversely, appearing in the acknowledgments or
funding statement does not make someone an author or indicate that they shaped the findings.
