A polygenic trait is a characteristic shaped by many genetic variants rather than by one gene.
Height is the standard example: thousands of variants across the genome each nudge it slightly. Most
common human traits and common diseases are polygenic, including blood pressure, body weight, type 2
diabetes and coronary heart disease. Behavioral and cognitive traits are also polygenic, and
environment plays a large part in all of them.

Polygenic traits are studied statistically. Researchers compare the genomes of very large numbers of
people and look for variants that are slightly more common in those with a trait. Each associated
variant typically explains a tiny fraction of the differences between people. Even combined, the
known variants usually explain only part of the inherited component.

This is the opposite of the pattern seen in single-gene (monogenic) conditions, where one variant
can have a large and fairly predictable effect. A common mistake is to carry single-gene thinking
over to polygenic traits, as in the idea of "the gene for" intelligence or longevity. No such gene
exists. For the same reason, polygenic traits cannot be reliably predicted for an individual from
DNA, and they are not realistic targets for gene editing.
