A U.S. research university is organized as a set of schools, also called faculties or colleges. Each
has its own dean, faculty, budget, degree programs and administrative staff.

The usual pattern is a central unit for the liberal arts and basic sciences, often named a faculty
or college of arts and sciences, which contains the undergraduate college, the graduate school for
doctoral study, and departments such as physics, history and mathematics. Around it are professional
schools, typically medicine, law, business, engineering, public health, education and others.
Departments sit inside schools. A scientist in a biology department and a scientist at the medical
school may do similar research while working under different deans, policies and budgets.

The word faculty has two meanings that are easy to mix up. It can mean the body of professors
collectively, and it can mean an organizational unit equivalent to a school. The structure matters
for reading the record because rules, records and oversight often belong to the school and not to
the university. A policy adopted by one school may not apply in another. Affiliations, gift records
and personnel files may be kept separately. People and research centers can also hold joint
appointments across schools, which raises the question of which dean was responsible.
