An incidental finding is a result that turns up in the course of a test or study and has nothing to
do with the original question. Broad genetic tests make such findings likely. Sequencing an exome or
genome to explain one condition can reveal, for example, a variant that raises the risk of an
inherited cancer or a heart rhythm disorder.

A distinction is often drawn between incidental findings, which are stumbled upon, and secondary
findings, which a laboratory deliberately looks for in addition to the main analysis. In clinical
genetics, professional guidance recommends that laboratories performing exome or genome sequencing
offer to analyze a defined list of genes in which harmful variants have serious consequences and
effective interventions exist. Patients can decline.

In research the position is less settled. Whether a study will return individual results is decided
in advance, described in the consent form and reviewed by an ethics board. Many studies return
nothing. Others return only findings that are medically actionable and confirmed in a clinical
laboratory. Participation in a genetic study is therefore not a health check, and the absence of any
communication from a study does not mean nothing of concern was found.
