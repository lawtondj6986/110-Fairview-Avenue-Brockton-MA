Title IX is a federal civil rights law. It says that no person in the United States may, on the
basis of sex, be excluded from, denied the benefits of, or subjected to discrimination under any
education program or activity that receives federal financial assistance. Because almost every
college and university accepts federal student aid or research funding, the law reaches nearly all
of them, public and private.

In practice, Title IX covers admissions in most programs, athletics, employment, and the way a
school responds to sexual harassment and sexual violence. Each school must name a Title IX
coordinator, publish a nondiscrimination notice, and maintain grievance procedures. The U.S.
Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights enforces the law administratively. Individuals can
also sue in court.

Title IX is not a criminal law. A school's Title IX process decides whether its own policy was
violated and what the school will do about it. It does not decide guilt or innocence under criminal
law. The detailed regulations were rewritten in 2020 and again in 2024, and a federal court set
aside the 2024 version in 2025, so readers should check which rules applied on the date of the
events they are reading about.
