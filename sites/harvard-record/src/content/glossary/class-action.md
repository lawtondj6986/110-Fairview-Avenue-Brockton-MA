A class action lets many similar claims be decided in a single case. A small number of class
representatives file the suit for themselves and for others in the same position. The device is used
where individual suits would be impractical, for example when each person's loss is small or the
group is very large.

A case becomes a class action only when the judge certifies the class. In federal court the
plaintiffs must show, among other things, that the group is too numerous for everyone to be joined,
that there are common questions of law or fact, that the representatives' claims are typical, and
that they and their lawyers will protect the interests of the class. Until certification, the group
is called a putative, or proposed, class. In most damages classes, members receive notice and may
opt out to pursue their own claims.

Because absent members are bound, any settlement must be approved by the court as fair, reasonable
and adequate after notice and a hearing. This makes class settlements unusually public, with the
terms, total amount and attorney fees on the record.

A complaint labeled "class action" shows only what the plaintiffs seek. Many proposed classes are
never certified. Certification is also a procedural ruling, not a decision that the claims are
valid.
