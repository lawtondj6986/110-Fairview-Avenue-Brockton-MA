A chronology arranges events by date. In research and legal work it is more than a list: each entry
records the date, what happened, the source, and how precise the date is. A meeting known to the day
from a calendar entry differs from one placed in "spring" by a later memoir, and a good chronology
shows that difference instead of smoothing it over.

Building one often reveals problems. Two sources may give different dates for the same event, a
document may be dated by when it was written rather than when the event occurred, or a reported
sequence may turn out to be impossible. Time zones, fiscal years and academic years can shift events
by a day or a year. Entries should say which date the source actually gives, such as the date of a
letter, the date it was received, or the date stamped on a filing.

A chronology shows order, not cause. Two events close together in time may be unrelated, and the act
of placing them side by side can suggest a connection the evidence does not support. Readers should
treat any explanation offered between entries as interpretation, not as part of the timeline itself.
