When a university examines a problem through its own legal office, the product is sometimes a report
issued in the name of the general counsel, alone or together with an outside law firm retained to
assist.

Such a review typically proceeds by collecting documents and electronic records, interviewing
current and former employees, and reconstructing a chronology. The report sets out findings of fact
and often recommends policy changes. The institution decides the scope at the start: which years,
which units, which questions. It also decides what to publish. Options include the full report, an
executive summary, a letter from the president describing the findings, or nothing.

Several features should be kept in mind when reading one. The authors are the institution's lawyers,
whose professional duty runs to the institution. They generally cannot compel anyone outside the
university to cooperate, and former employees may decline to be interviewed. Findings are bounded by
the stated scope, so the absence of a finding on a topic may mean it was not examined. Underlying
interview notes and documents usually stay privileged and unpublished, so the report's conclusions
cannot be checked against its evidence. Such reports are often the most detailed source available.
Each should be cited for what it says it examined and found, with its limits stated.
