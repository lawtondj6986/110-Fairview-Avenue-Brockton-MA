---
term: "Congressional oversight letter"
short: "A letter from members of Congress or a congressional committee to an agency, company or institution asking for information or documents as part of an inquiry."

Congress has implied constitutional authority to investigate matters on which it may legislate or
appropriate money, and to oversee how laws are carried out. Most of this work is done by committees,
and its everyday instrument is the letter. An oversight letter typically sets out the committee's or
member's concern, cites public reporting or documents, and asks the recipient to answer questions
and produce records by a stated date.

Such a letter is a request. It carries no legal compulsion by itself. Recipients often comply in
whole or in part, because of the committee's influence over legislation and funding, and because
refusal can lead to a subpoena. Only a committee, acting under its rules and usually through its
chair, can issue a subpoena, and enforcing one requires further steps. Letters and responses are
frequently published on committee or member websites, which makes them a source of otherwise
unavailable documents.

The statements in an oversight letter are assertions by its authors. They are not findings, and
Congress is not a court. Letters may be signed by a committee chair, by the senior minority member,
by a bipartisan group, or by a single legislator. Who signed affects what powers stand behind the
request. The reply, and any documents produced, usually matter more than the letter.
