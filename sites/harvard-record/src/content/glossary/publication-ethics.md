Publication ethics is the set of norms for communicating research honestly. It applies to everyone
in the publishing process. Authors are expected to report methods and results accurately, give
credit through proper authorship and citation, disclose funding and competing interests, obtain
ethics approval and consent for research with people, and avoid submitting the same work to more
than one journal or publishing it twice. Peer reviewers are expected to keep manuscripts
confidential, declare their own conflicts and not use what they read for personal advantage. Editors
are expected to decide on merit, independent of commercial or personal pressure.

Journals put these norms into practice through instructions to authors, required disclosure and
ethics statements, checks for plagiarism and image manipulation, and procedures for handling
complaints. When a published article proves flawed, the tools are a correction, an expression of
concern or a retraction. International editors' associations publish guidance and flowcharts that
many journals follow.

Journals have limited investigative power. They cannot compel evidence, so they generally refer
serious allegations to the authors' institution and act on its findings. Publication in a
peer-reviewed journal therefore indicates that a paper passed review. It is not a guarantee that the
work is correct, complete or free of undisclosed interests.
