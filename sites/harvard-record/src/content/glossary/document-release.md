A document release is the act of making a body of records public. Releases happen when a court
unseals filings, an agency answers a records request or publishes files on its own initiative, a
legislative committee publishes materials it has gathered, or an institution issues documents with a
report. A large release made all at once, with little organization, is informally called a document
dump. The term production is used in litigation for documents one party hands to another.

Large releases share certain features. They are usually incomplete, since material is withheld or
redacted and what is released reflects what the releasing body held and chose to disclose. They
contain many duplicates and near-duplicates. They mix kinds of material with very different value:
formal findings, unverified tips, routine correspondence, news clippings and third-party
submissions. Documents frequently arrive without the surrounding context needed to understand them,
and files may be released in stages.

Inclusion in a release is not a finding. A person's name can appear because they were a witness, a
correspondent, a subject of a tip that went nowhere, or an entry in someone's address book. An
official release establishes that the documents are authentic records of the releasing body. It does
not vouch for the truth of every statement in them. Careful use cites the specific document.
