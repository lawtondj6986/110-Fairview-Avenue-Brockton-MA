term: "Magistrate judge"
short: "A federal judicial officer appointed by the district judges of a court to handle many pretrial matters, such as warrants, discovery disputes and initial hearings, and some trials with the parties' consent."

In federal district courts, magistrate judges work alongside district judges. District judges are
nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate for life; magistrate judges are selected by
the district judges for fixed, renewable terms. They issue search and arrest warrants, conduct
initial appearances and detention hearings in criminal cases, supervise discovery and settlement
conferences in civil cases, and try misdemeanors.

A district judge can refer a dispositive matter, such as a motion to dismiss, to a magistrate judge,
who then issues a report and recommendation. The parties may object, and the district judge decides
whether to adopt it. If all parties consent, a magistrate judge can preside over an entire civil
case, including trial and judgment. Docket entries often show the magistrate judge's initials after
the case number.

A report and recommendation is not yet a ruling of the court. Readers sometimes report it as the
decision; it becomes one only when the district judge accepts it. State courts use the word
magistrate for different officers with different powers, so the term should not be carried across
systems without checking.
