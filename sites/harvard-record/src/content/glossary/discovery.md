Discovery is the compulsory exchange of information between parties to a lawsuit. Its purpose is to
let each side learn the facts and the other side's evidence before trial. In federal court, a party
may generally obtain any non-privileged matter that is relevant to a claim or defense and
proportional to the needs of the case.

The main tools are requests for production of documents and electronic records, interrogatories
(written questions answered under oath), requests for admission, and depositions, in which witnesses
are questioned under oath. Subpoenas extend discovery to people and organizations that are not
parties. Disputes over what must be handed over are resolved by the judge. Criminal cases have a
narrower form of discovery, centered on what the prosecution must disclose to the defense.

Most discovery material never becomes public. It passes between the parties' lawyers and is not
filed with the court. It is often also covered by a protective order restricting further use.
Material typically enters the public record only when a party attaches it to a motion or uses it at
trial, and even then it may be sealed or redacted. The public file of a case is therefore a small
and selected portion of what the parties saw. The absence of a document from the docket does not
mean it was never produced.
