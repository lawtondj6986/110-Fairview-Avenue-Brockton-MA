---
term: "Deposition"
short: "Out-of-court questioning of a witness under oath, recorded word for word, taken as part of discovery in a lawsuit."
category: "Legal procedure"
aliases: ["depo", "deposition transcript", "deponent"]
related: ["discovery", "subpoena", "affidavit", "exhibit", "sealed-record"]
---

A deposition is sworn testimony taken before trial. A lawyer for one party questions the witness,
called the deponent, in the presence of a court reporter who produces a verbatim transcript. Many
depositions are also video recorded. Lawyers for the other parties attend, may object, and may ask
their own questions. No judge is present. Objections are noted for the record, and the witness
usually answers anyway, except where a privilege is claimed.

Parties can be required to appear by notice, and non-parties by subpoena. An organization can be
required to send a representative to testify about listed topics on its behalf. The witness may
review the transcript afterward and submit corrections. At trial or on summary judgment, deposition
testimony can be used to establish facts or to challenge a witness whose account has changed.

Deposition testimony is evidence given under penalty of perjury, which makes it more substantial
than an allegation in a complaint. It is still one witness's account and may be contradicted by
other evidence. Transcripts are not automatically public. They appear on the docket only when a
party files them, and what is filed is often a set of excerpts chosen by one side to support its
argument. Read short excerpts with that in mind, and note where a witness declined to answer.
