---
term: "Attribution"
short: "Stating clearly who or what is the source of a piece of information, so that readers can judge it and trace it."
category: "Records and sourcing"
aliases: ["sourcing"]
related: ["on-the-record", "citation", "corroboration", "second-hand-account", "fair-report-privilege"]

Attribution is the practice of telling the reader where a statement comes from. "According to the
report", "the complaint alleges" and "a spokesperson said" are attributions. Attribution lets
readers weigh information for themselves, because who is speaking, and with what knowledge and
interest, affects how much a statement is worth.

Good attribution is specific. It identifies the source as precisely as possible, shows how the
source is in a position to know, and distinguishes direct quotation from paraphrase. It also keeps
the source's own level of certainty. If a document says something "may" have happened, the
attributed version should not turn that into a flat assertion. Verbs matter: "said", "alleged",
"found", "concluded" and "testified" describe different acts and should not be swapped loosely.

Attribution does not make a claim true, and it does not move all responsibility onto the source. A
writer who repeats a damaging false statement can in some circumstances still be responsible for
republishing it. Attribution is also different from citation. Attribution is the statement in the
text of who says something. Citation is the reference that lets the reader find the source.
Unattributed statements in a careful text should be limited to matters that are undisputed or
directly shown by the cited record.
