A freedom of information request asks a government body to disclose records under a statute that
gives the public a right of access. In the United States, the federal Freedom of Information Act
(FOIA) applies to federal executive branch agencies. It does not apply to Congress or the federal
courts. Each state has its own public records law covering state and local bodies, with its own
name, scope, deadlines and exemptions.

A request is made in writing and should describe the records sought clearly enough for staff to
locate them. Anyone may file one, and a requester generally does not have to give a reason. The
agency must search for the records and release them unless an exemption applies. Federal exemptions
cover, among other things, classified information, personal privacy, trade secrets, law enforcement
records and internal deliberations. Agencies may redact exempt portions, charge fees in some
circumstances, and often take much longer than the statutory deadlines. Denials can be appealed.

These laws reach government records. Private universities and other private organizations are
generally not subject to them, although their communications with public agencies may be obtainable
from the agency. Public universities usually are covered by state law. A response that no records
were found describes the result of that agency's search. It does not establish that no records exist
anywhere.
