---
term: "Standing"
short: "The requirement that a person bringing a lawsuit have a real, personal stake in it: an injury caused by the defendant that a court ruling could remedy."
category: "Legal procedure"
aliases: ["Article III standing", "legal standing", "injury in fact"]
related: ["motion-to-dismiss", "plaintiff-and-defendant", "dismissal-with-prejudice", "class-action"]
---

Standing is a threshold question about who may sue. Federal courts can decide only actual cases or
controversies, and they read that limit to require three things of a plaintiff. The plaintiff must
have suffered, or be about to suffer, a concrete and particular injury. The injury must be fairly
traceable to the defendant's conduct. A favorable court decision must be likely to redress it. State
courts apply their own versions of the doctrine, some of them more relaxed.

Standing is assessed separately for each claim and each form of relief. A former student may have
standing to seek damages for a past injury, for example, and lack standing to seek an order changing
a policy that no longer affects them. Organizations can sometimes sue on behalf of their members.
Defendants commonly raise standing in a motion to dismiss, and a court may revisit it at any stage.

A dismissal for lack of standing is not a ruling on the merits. The court has not decided whether
the defendant acted lawfully. It has decided that this plaintiff is not the right party to raise the
question. Such dismissals are normally without prejudice. The doctrine also explains why some
apparent legal violations are never tested in court: no one with a qualifying injury has sued.
