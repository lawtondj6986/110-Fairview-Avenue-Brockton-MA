Pay-to-participate research describes arrangements in which the people being studied pay for the
privilege, either as a fee for enrollment or by funding the study in which they then take part.
Ordinarily research is paid for by a sponsor or grant, and participants are not charged for research
procedures. Some are compensated for their time.

The model appears in several forms: clinics that charge patients to receive unproven interventions
labeled as studies, trials financed by the patients enrolled in them, and individuals who pay a
laboratory or company to have their own genome sequenced or cells banked within a research project.
It is not illegal as such, and U.S. drug regulations allow charging for an investigational product
only in limited circumstances with authorization.

Ethicists raise recurring concerns. Payment can select participants by wealth instead of scientific
criteria, which undermines fairness and can bias results. Paying participants may expect benefit and
treat the activity as a service, which blurs the line between research and care. Researchers who
depend on the fees have a conflict of interest. And small self-funded studies often lack controls,
so they yield little reliable knowledge. The term should not be confused with paying participants,
which is the common and accepted practice of compensating volunteers.
