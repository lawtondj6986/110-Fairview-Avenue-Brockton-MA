Notice-and-comment rulemaking is the process the Administrative Procedure Act prescribes for most
binding federal regulations. The agency publishes a notice of proposed rulemaking in the Federal
Register, with the proposed text and its explanation. The public then has a period, often thirty to
sixty days or more, to submit written comments. The agency must consider the comments and publish a
final rule with a statement of its basis and purpose, which normally responds to the significant
points raised.

A final rule is codified in the Code of Federal Regulations and has the force of law. Courts can
review it and may set it aside if the agency exceeded its authority, skipped required steps, or
failed to give a reasoned explanation. Changing or repealing a rule generally requires the same
process, which is why regulations are more durable than guidance.

The distinction between a rule and guidance is important when reading compliance documents.
Interpretive statements, policy memoranda and Dear Colleague letters are exempt from notice and
comment and do not bind the public in the same way. The comment process is also not a vote. An
agency must respond to substantive arguments, but it is not obliged to follow the majority of
commenters.
