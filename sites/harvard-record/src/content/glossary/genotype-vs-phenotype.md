---
term: "Genotype versus phenotype"
short: "Genotype is a person's genetic makeup at one or more positions; phenotype is the observable characteristic that results from genes, environment and chance."
category: "Genetics and biomedical research"
aliases: ["genotype", "phenotype", "penetrance"]
related: ["genetic-variant", "polygenic-trait", "heritability", "genetic-database"]
---

Genotype is the genetic makeup of an individual, either at one position (for example, which two
versions of a variant the person carries) or more broadly. Phenotype is any observable or measurable
characteristic: height, eye color, blood pressure, a diagnosis, a laboratory value, a behavior.

The relationship between the two is rarely one to one. A phenotype reflects genotype, environment,
development and chance. Geneticists use the term penetrance for the proportion of people with a
given genotype who actually show the associated phenotype. For some variants penetrance is close to
complete. For many it is far lower, so that a person can carry a disease-associated variant and
never develop the disease.

The distinction matters when reading about genetic data collections. Genotype data alone have
limited research value. Their usefulness comes from linking them to phenotype data, such as medical
records, questionnaires or measurements, which is why projects collect both and why the combination
is more sensitive than either part. The distinction also guards against a frequent error: assuming
that knowing a genotype means knowing the outcome. A genetic result reports a genotype. Whether,
when and how strongly a phenotype follows is a separate question.
