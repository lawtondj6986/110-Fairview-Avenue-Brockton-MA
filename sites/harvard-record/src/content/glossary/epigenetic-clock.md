Epigenetic marks are chemical changes that affect how genes are used without changing the DNA
sequence. One of the best studied is methylation, the attachment of small chemical groups to DNA at
specific sites. Methylation at some sites changes in a regular way with age. An epigenetic clock
measures methylation at a selected set of sites and combines them, using a model trained on many
samples, into an estimated age.

Early clocks were trained to predict chronological age and do so closely. Later models were trained
on health outcomes and are presented as measures of biological aging, in which an estimate older
than a person's actual age is read as faster aging. Clocks are widely used in aging research to
compare groups and to test whether an intervention shifts the measure. Consumer tests based on them
are also sold.

Clock results vary with the tissue sampled, the laboratory method and the model used, and repeated
tests on one person can differ. An estimated biological age is a research measure, not a diagnosis.
Showing that an intervention lowers a clock reading is not the same as showing that it extends life
or health.
