term: "Special committee"
short: "A temporary committee, usually of governing board members, created to oversee a specific sensitive matter such as an investigation into the institution's conduct."

A special committee is a body formed for one task and dissolved when the task is done. In governance
usage it most often means a committee of the governing board appointed to handle a matter that the
regular structure cannot handle credibly, because senior management or other board members are
involved or have a stake in the outcome.

The board creates the committee by resolution, which names the members and defines its mandate and
powers. Members are chosen for their lack of connection to the events. The committee usually hires
its own outside counsel, supervises the investigation, receives the findings, and makes
recommendations to the full board on discipline, policy changes and disclosure. Its authority may be
limited to recommending, or it may be empowered to decide.

The same phrase is used more loosely for faculty or administrative committees set up by a president,
provost or dean to study a problem. These are different in kind. A committee appointed by management
reports to management. For any special committee, the founding charge is the key document. It shows
who appointed the members, what they were asked to examine, what they were not asked to examine, and
to whom they report. A committee's conclusions should be read against that charge, because a narrow
mandate produces narrow findings.
