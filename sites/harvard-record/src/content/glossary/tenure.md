Tenure is an appointment without a fixed end date. A tenured professor can be dismissed only for
adequate cause, or under extraordinary conditions such as genuine financial emergency or the closing
of a program, and only after due process. The standard account of the principle is the 1940
Statement of Principles on Academic Freedom and Tenure, issued by the American Association of
University Professors together with an association of colleges and endorsed by many scholarly
bodies.

A professor usually earns tenure after a probationary period of several years on the tenure track.
The case is reviewed by the department, outside experts in the field, a school committee, the dean
and the provost, with final approval by the president or governing board. A candidate denied tenure
typically must leave. Many teaching and research staff, including lecturers, adjuncts and most
research scientists, hold appointments outside the tenure system.

Tenure is not immunity. Tenured faculty remain subject to institutional policies and can be
sanctioned, suspended or dismissed for serious misconduct, neglect of duty or similar grounds
defined in the faculty handbook. What tenure provides is procedure: notice of charges, a hearing,
usually before faculty peers, and a burden on the institution to justify the action. Those
protections make dismissal slow and rare, which affects how institutions choose to respond to
misconduct.
