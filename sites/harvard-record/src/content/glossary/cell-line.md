term: "Cell line"
short: "A population of cells descended from an original sample and maintained by growing them in the laboratory."

A cell line is a population of cells grown in the laboratory that descends from cells taken from one
source, such as a person's skin, blood or tumor. The cells are kept in dishes or flasks with a
nutrient liquid and are periodically divided into new containers as they multiply, a step called
passaging.

Cell lines are either finite or continuous. A finite line, grown from normal tissue, divides a
limited number of times and then stops. A continuous line, also called an immortalized line, can
divide indefinitely. Lines are a basic research tool because they give many scientists a supply of
genetically identical material to experiment on. They are stored frozen, distributed by
repositories, and usually transferred under written agreements.

A human cell line carries the genome of the person it came from. Removing the name from the label
does not remove that link, since the DNA can be read from the cells at any time. Cell lines also
change. They accumulate genetic alterations as they are grown, and lines are sometimes mislabeled or
contaminated by other lines, so careful laboratories periodically confirm a line's identity.
