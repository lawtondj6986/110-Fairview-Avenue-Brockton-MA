---
term: "Preferential treatment in research access"
short: "Giving a person earlier or easier access to a study, test or scarce research resource because of money, status or connections, not the criteria that apply to others."
category: "Research ethics"
aliases: ["queue-jumping", "VIP access", "special access"]
related: ["pay-to-participate-research", "research-conflict-of-interest", "undue-influence-in-research", "belmont-report", "research-protocol"]
---

Preferential treatment, informally called queue-jumping, means that someone obtains access ahead of
or outside the normal process because of who they are or what they can offer. In research the thing
obtained might be a place in a study, sequencing or other analysis that is not generally available,
the personal attention of a senior scientist, or early results.

Research ethics addresses this under the principle of justice, which calls for the fair selection of
participants. Ethics boards are required to consider whether selection is equitable, and protocols
set eligibility criteria and recruitment methods so that enrollment follows scientific reasons.
Enrolling a donor, official or acquaintance outside those criteria is a departure from the protocol.
It can also create a conflict of interest if the favor is connected to a gift or to an expected
benefit to the researcher or institution.

Not every individual arrangement is improper. Researchers may legitimately study a specific person
with a rare condition, and some projects deliberately recruit particular volunteers. The questions
that matter are whether the arrangement was within an approved protocol, whether the same terms were
available to others in a similar position, whether money or influence drove the decision, and
whether it was disclosed. Access gained through connections is a fairness concern even when no
participant is harmed.
