---
term: "Contemporaneous record"
short: "A document created at or very near the time of the events it describes, such as an email, calendar entry, receipt or meeting note, as opposed to an account written later."
category: "Records and sourcing"
aliases: ["Contemporaneous document", "Contemporaneous note", "Business record", "Record made at the time"]

A contemporaneous record is made while events are happening or shortly afterward, before memories
fade or interests change. Examples include emails, text messages, calendar entries, sign-off forms,
invoices, lab notebooks and minutes. Courts and historians tend to give such records particular
weight, and the law of evidence has an exception to the hearsay rule for records kept in the regular
course of business.

Their value lies in timing. A later account, such as an interview, a memoir or a statement prepared
for an investigation, may be accurate, but it is written with hindsight and sometimes with a purpose.
Comparing a later account with contemporaneous records is one of the most effective checks on it.
Where the two disagree, the difference is worth recording, not resolving by assumption.

Contemporaneous does not mean accurate. A note written at the time can contain errors, reflect only
the writer's view, or describe plans that were never carried out. A calendar entry shows that
something was scheduled, not that it occurred. Records can also be backdated or altered, so
metadata and provenance still matter.
