Cell lines are easily mixed up or contaminated. A fast-growing line can overtake another in a shared
flask, labels can be swapped, and bacteria such as mycoplasma can infect cultures without being
visible. Studies have repeatedly found that a meaningful share of published research used cell lines
that were not what the authors believed. Authentication guards against this.

For human cell lines, the standard method is short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, which produces a
DNA fingerprint that can be compared with the profile of the original donor's tissue or with a
public reference database. Species can be checked by other genetic tests, and mycoplasma by a
separate assay. Many journals ask authors to report authentication, and the National Institutes of
Health asks grant applicants to describe how they will authenticate key biological resources. An
international committee keeps a public register of known misidentified lines.

Authentication tests identity and contamination at the time of testing. It does not show that cells
were obtained with proper consent, and it does not show that the cells are genetically unchanged
since they were derived; cultured cells can acquire new variants over time.
