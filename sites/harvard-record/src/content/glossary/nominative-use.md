---
term: "Nominative use of a name"
short: "Using a name or trademark simply to refer to the actual person, institution or product it identifies, not to suggest affiliation or endorsement."
category: "Records and sourcing"
aliases: ["nominative fair use", "referential use"]
related: ["defamation", "fair-report-privilege", "attribution", "citation"]
---

Nominative use means using a name in order to talk about the thing named. The concept comes from
trademark law, where it is usually called nominative fair use. A trademark, which can include the
name of a company or a university, identifies the source of goods and services. Trademark law is
aimed at uses likely to confuse the public about who is behind something. It does not forbid people
from referring to an organization by its name in order to describe, review, criticize or report on
it.

U.S. courts differ in how they analyze the question, but the factors commonly considered are
similar. They ask whether the name is needed to identify the subject, whether only as much of the
mark is used as is necessary (the plain name, for instance, and not a logo, seal or distinctive
design), and whether anything suggests sponsorship or endorsement by the mark's owner. A clear
statement of independence helps, though it is not decisive by itself.

The concept explains how news reports, scholarship and independent reference works can name the
institutions they discuss. It does not permit imitating an institution's visual identity or implying
official status . It is also a trademark doctrine only. It does not address defamation, privacy or
copyright. This is a general description, not legal advice.
