A motion to dismiss asks the court to end a case, or part of it, without a trial and usually before
any evidence is exchanged. The most common ground is failure to state a claim: the argument that the
facts in the complaint, even if all true, do not amount to a legal violation. Other grounds include
lack of jurisdiction, lack of standing, and a claim that is plainly too late under the statute of
limitations.

In deciding the motion, the judge assumes that the complaint's factual allegations are true and
draws reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's favor. The question is whether those allegations make
a plausible claim. The judge generally does not consider outside evidence.

That assumption is the key to reading the resulting opinion. When a court denies a motion to
dismiss, it has not found that the allegations are true. It has decided only that the case may
proceed. Passages in such an opinion that recount the facts are restating the complaint. When a
court grants the motion, it has not found the allegations false. It has decided that the law gives
no remedy on those facts, or that the suit is in the wrong court or out of time. Dismissal may come
with permission to file an amended complaint.
