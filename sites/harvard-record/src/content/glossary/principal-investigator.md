A principal investigator, or PI, is the person named as responsible for a research project. On a
grant, the PI is the individual the institution designates to direct the work and account for it.
More loosely, the term means the head of a laboratory or research group.

Grants are legally awarded to the institution, not the individual, but the PI holds day-to-day
control. The PI decides the scientific agenda, hires and supervises students, postdoctoral fellows
and staff, approves spending from the project's accounts, and certifies compliance with rules on
human subjects, animal welfare, safety and financial conflicts. Institutions set eligibility rules
for who may serve as a PI, usually limiting the role to faculty and certain senior research staff.

This autonomy is central to how research universities work, and it shapes the record. A PI can form
relationships with funders, invite visitors, and extend informal affiliations with little
involvement from a chair or dean, provided no rule is broken. Oversight usually depends on the PI's
own disclosures. PI status does not mean the person owns the grant money or the laboratory, and it
does not place the PI outside university policy. It means the institution has entrusted that person
with responsibility for the work.
