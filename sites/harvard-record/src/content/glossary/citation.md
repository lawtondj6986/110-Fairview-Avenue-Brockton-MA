A citation is a pointer from a statement to its source. Its job is practical: a reader should be
able to find the exact item, and ideally the exact passage, that supports the claim. A complete
citation normally identifies the author or issuing body, the title, the publisher or publication,
the date, and a locator such as a page, paragraph, section, docket entry or document number. Online
material adds the web address.

Different fields use different formats, and legal citation has its own conventions for statutes,
regulations and cases. The format matters less than precision. Citing a 300-page report as a whole
is of little help to a reader. Citing the page is. A pinpoint citation, or pin cite, is one that
gives the specific page or paragraph.

Citation and attribution work together but are distinct. Attribution says in the text who is the
source of a statement. Citation supplies the reference. A citation also does not vouch for a source.
It shows where a claim comes from and leaves the reader to judge the source's strength. The presence
of many citations is not evidence of accuracy if the sources are weak, circular or do not say what
they are cited for. The test is whether the cited passage supports the sentence it is attached to.
