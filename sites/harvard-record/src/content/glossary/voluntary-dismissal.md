term: "Voluntary dismissal"

A voluntary dismissal is a withdrawal of claims by the party who brought them. In federal court a
plaintiff can dismiss a case without the court's permission by filing a notice before the defendant
has served an answer or a motion for summary judgment. After that point, dismissal requires either a
stipulation signed by all parties who have appeared or an order from the judge.

Unless the notice or stipulation says otherwise, a first voluntary dismissal is without prejudice,
which means the plaintiff may file the claim again, subject to the statute of limitations. A
stipulated dismissal that follows a settlement usually says that it is with prejudice, which closes
the matter for good.

The docket entry for a voluntary dismissal is brief and rarely gives a reason. The plaintiff may
have settled, decided to sue in a different court, concluded that the case was too costly or too
weak, or dropped one defendant to concentrate on others. From the dismissal alone, a reader cannot
tell which. In particular, it is not a ruling for the defendant and not an admission by the
plaintiff that the claims were untrue.

It should be distinguished from an involuntary dismissal, which the court orders over the
plaintiff's objection, for example on a motion to dismiss.
