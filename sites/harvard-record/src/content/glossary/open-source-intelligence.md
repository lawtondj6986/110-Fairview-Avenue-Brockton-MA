Open-source intelligence, usually shortened to OSINT, is research that relies on information anyone
can lawfully obtain. The term comes from government intelligence work, where it distinguishes
publicly available material from secret sources, and it is now used by journalists, academic
researchers, human rights investigators and hobbyists. Here "open source" refers to the openness of
the information and has nothing to do with open-source software.

Typical materials include court and corporate filings, property and aviation records, archived web
pages, published photographs and video, satellite imagery and social media posts. Typical methods
include establishing where and when an image was taken, tracing the earliest appearance of a
document or claim, comparing versions of a web page over time and cross-referencing independent
records. Good practice documents every step so that others can repeat it.

The method has known hazards. Public availability does not make information accurate, and material
found online may be mislabeled, manipulated or stripped of context. Crowdsourced investigations have
at times identified the wrong person. Publicly available is also not the same as ethically
publishable. Compiling scattered details about a private individual can cause harm even when each
detail is public. OSINT findings need the same corroboration and attribution as any other reporting.
