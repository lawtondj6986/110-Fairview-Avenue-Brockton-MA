HIPAA is a 1996 federal law. Its best-known effects come from regulations issued under it: the
Privacy Rule, the Security Rule and the Breach Notification Rule. Together they govern protected
health information, meaning individually identifiable health information held by a covered entity or
its business associate.

The Privacy Rule allows use and disclosure of health information for treatment, payment and health
care operations, and otherwise generally requires the patient's written authorization. It lists
exceptions, including certain disclosures to public health authorities, to law enforcement, and for
research under specified conditions. It gives patients a right to see and get copies of their
records. The Office for Civil Rights at the Department of Health and Human Services enforces the
rules and can impose civil penalties.

HIPAA is frequently invoked where it does not apply. It binds covered entities and business
associates, not everyone who knows something about a person's health. Employers acting as employers,
journalists, and most researchers working with data they collected themselves are outside it.
Student health records at a university clinic are usually excluded from HIPAA and fall under FERPA
and state law instead. Individuals cannot sue under HIPAA itself, though state law may give them a
claim.
