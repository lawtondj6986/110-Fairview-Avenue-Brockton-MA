A naming gift is a gift recognized by putting a name on something: a building, a wing, a laboratory,
a center, a professorship, a scholarship or a school. The name is usually the donor's, a family
member's or a person the donor wants to honor.

Institutions generally keep a naming policy that sets who may approve a name and what level of gift
is expected for each kind of object. Naming a building or a school commonly requires approval by the
president and the governing board. The gift agreement records the name, how long it lasts (for the
life of the building, for a term of years, or indefinitely) and, increasingly, the circumstances in
which the institution may remove it. A typical removal clause covers conduct by the donor that would
damage the institution's reputation, or failure to pay the pledge.

A naming is a form of recognition, not a sale of control. The donor does not own the building or
direct the program that carries the name. A naming is also more visible and more durable than most
forms of recognition, which is why it draws closer review before acceptance and more difficulty
afterward if the institution wants to end the association. Removing a name does not by itself settle
what happens to the money.
