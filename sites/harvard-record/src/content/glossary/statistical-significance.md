Many studies test a null hypothesis, the assumption that there is no difference or no effect. The
p-value is the probability of seeing a result at least as extreme as the one observed if the null
hypothesis were true. When it falls below a chosen threshold, most often 0.05, the result is called
statistically significant. Fields that run many tests at once, such as genetics, use much stricter
thresholds.

Significance depends heavily on sample size. A very large study can find a tiny, unimportant
difference significant, and a small study can miss a large, real one. Running many analyses and
reporting only the significant ones, sometimes called p-hacking, produces misleading results. For
these reasons, statisticians encourage reporting effect sizes and confidence intervals alongside
p-values.

Statistically significant does not mean important, large or proved. A non-significant result does
not show that there is no effect; it may only show that the study could not detect one. In everyday
reporting the word "significant" is ambiguous, so it is worth checking whether a writer means the
statistical sense or simply "notable."
