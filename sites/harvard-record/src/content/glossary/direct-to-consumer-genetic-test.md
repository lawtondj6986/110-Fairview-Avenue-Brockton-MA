A direct-to-consumer (DTC) genetic test is one that a member of the public buys without going
through their own health care provider. The customer usually orders a kit, provides saliva or a
cheek swab, mails it to a laboratory, and receives a report online. Reports may cover ancestry,
matches with relatives, traits, carrier status for some inherited conditions, and selected health
risks.

Most DTC tests use a genotyping array, which checks a preselected set of positions rather than
reading the whole genome. In the United States, tests that make health claims are subject to
oversight by the Food and Drug Administration, and the scope of that oversight has changed over
time. Company terms of service and privacy policies, rather than medical privacy law, usually govern
what happens to the sample and data, including whether they are stored or used in research.

A consumer report is not a diagnosis. A health-related test often examines only a few variants in a
gene, so a negative result does not exclude other variants. Raw data files can contain errors.
Results that might affect medical decisions are normally confirmed in a clinical laboratory.
