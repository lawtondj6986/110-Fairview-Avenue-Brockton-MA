Informed consent is the process by which a person agrees to take part in research, or to undergo a
medical procedure, with an understanding of what it involves. It has three components. The person
must be given the relevant information. The person must understand it. And the decision must be
voluntary, made without coercion or undue influence, by someone with the capacity to decide.

In research, U.S. federal rules list what a prospective participant must be told. The list includes
the purpose of the study, the procedures, foreseeable risks and benefits, alternatives, how
confidentiality will be handled, whom to contact, and a statement that participation is voluntary
and can be ended at any time without penalty. For studies that collect biological samples, the rules
also call for disclosure of matters such as possible commercial use and whether results will be
returned. An ethics board reviews the consent process and can waive or alter requirements in limited
circumstances.

Informed consent is a process, not a signature. A signed form is evidence that a conversation
occurred. It does not prove understanding. Consent is also specific. Agreement to give a sample for
one purpose does not automatically cover other uses, and consent to research is not the same as
consent to clinical care.
