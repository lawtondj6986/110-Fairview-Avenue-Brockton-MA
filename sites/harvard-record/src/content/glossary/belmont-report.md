The Belmont Report is a short statement of ethical principles for research involving people. It was
issued in 1979 by a national commission that Congress created in 1974, after public disclosure of
serious abuses in research, including studies in which participants were deceived or denied
available treatment.

The report identifies three principles and links each to a practical requirement. Respect for
persons means treating individuals as autonomous and protecting those with diminished autonomy. It
leads to informed consent. Beneficence means not harming people and maximizing possible benefits
while minimizing possible harms. It leads to the systematic assessment of risks and benefits.
Justice concerns who bears the burdens of research and who receives its benefits. It leads to fair
procedures for selecting participants. The report also discusses the boundary between research and
ordinary medical practice.

The Belmont Report is not a law or a regulation and contains no enforceable rules. It is the ethical
framework on which the U.S. human subjects regulations draw, and institutions commonly pledge to be
guided by it. Its principles are deliberately general and can pull in different directions in a
given case. Applying them is the task of ethics boards and investigators.
