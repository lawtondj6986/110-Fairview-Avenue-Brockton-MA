Academic freedom is the professional principle that faculty members are free to pursue research and
publish the results, to discuss their subject in the classroom, and to speak as citizens, without
being penalized for the positions they take. In the United States its classic formulation is the
1940 Statement of Principles on Academic Freedom and Tenure.

At private institutions academic freedom is protected chiefly by institutional policy and contract,
since faculty handbooks commonly incorporate it. At public institutions the First Amendment also
applies, and courts have recognized academic freedom as a concern of constitutional importance,
though its exact legal scope is unsettled. It is the main reason gift policies refuse conditions
that would let a donor select faculty or shape research findings.

Academic freedom has limits that are often misstated. It protects scholarly judgment within
professional standards. It does not exempt a professor from rules on research integrity, financial
disclosure, harassment, safety or the proper use of university resources. It also concerns what a
scholar studies and says, not whom they may bring onto campus or accept money from. An institution
can regulate a faculty member's choice of funders, visitors and affiliates without restricting the
content of the scholarship, although where exactly that line falls is sometimes contested.
