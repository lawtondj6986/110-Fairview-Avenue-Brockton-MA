term: "Independent investigation"
short: "An inquiry meant to be free of control by those whose conduct is examined, usually run by an outside firm reporting to the governing board or a board committee."

An independent investigation is one structured so that the people being examined cannot direct it.
The term has no fixed legal definition for universities, and institutions use it freely, so the
structure has to be checked each time.

The usual model borrows from corporate practice. The governing board, or a special committee of
board members with no involvement in the events, retains a law firm that has little or no prior
relationship with the institution. The firm reports to the committee, not to the president or
general counsel. It is given authority to collect documents and email, interview employees, and
follow the evidence beyond the original question if needed. At the end it delivers findings, and the
board decides whether to publish them.

Independence is a matter of degree. Indicators of a stronger process include a reporting line that
bypasses management, a public written mandate, investigators without conflicts, an advance
commitment to release the full report, and freedom to examine senior leaders. Even a well-designed
investigation has limits. It is paid for by the institution, cannot compel outsiders or former
employees to cooperate, and has no power to place witnesses under oath. It is not a court or a
government inquiry. Its findings are the conclusions of the investigators based on the evidence they
were able to obtain.
