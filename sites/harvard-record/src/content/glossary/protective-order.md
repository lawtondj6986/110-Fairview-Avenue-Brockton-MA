In civil procedure, a protective order is an order that shields a party or witness from annoyance,
embarrassment, oppression or undue burden in discovery. Its most common form is a confidentiality
order. The parties, often by agreement, ask the court to rule that documents marked confidential may
be seen only by the lawyers, the parties and specified experts, and may be used only for the
lawsuit. Such orders are routine in cases that involve medical, financial, student or commercially
sensitive records.

A protective order governs material that the parties exchange. It does not by itself decide what may
be kept from the public once a document is filed with the court. Sealing a court filing usually
requires a separate request and a stronger justification, because of the public's presumptive right
of access to judicial records. Protective orders also commonly say how a party may challenge a
confidentiality marking, and what happens to the material when the case ends.

The same words have an unrelated meaning. A protective order, or order of protection, is also a
court order under state law directing a person to stay away from and not contact another person,
issued in cases of domestic violence, stalking or harassment. Context usually makes clear which is
meant. Neither kind should be confused with a university's no-contact directive.
