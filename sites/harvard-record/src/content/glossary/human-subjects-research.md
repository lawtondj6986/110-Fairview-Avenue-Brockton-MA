Human subjects research is the regulatory term for research that involves people in a way that
triggers ethical oversight. Under the main U.S. federal policy, known as the Common Rule, two tests
apply. The activity must be research, meaning a systematic investigation designed to develop or
contribute to generalizable knowledge. And it must involve a human subject, meaning a living
individual about whom the investigator obtains information or biospecimens through intervention or
interaction, or whose identifiable private information or identifiable biospecimens are used.

When both tests are met, the study generally needs review by an institutional review board and
informed consent, unless an exemption or waiver applies. The Common Rule binds research funded or
conducted by the federal agencies that have adopted it. Separate regulations cover studies of drugs
and devices. Institutions often extend the same protections to privately funded work as a matter of
policy.

The definition has edges. Work with samples or data that cannot be linked to individuals may fall
outside it. So may quality improvement, journalism and some oral history. Scientists who study
themselves, or who collect samples informally from colleagues, friends or donors, are not thereby
outside the rules. Institutions generally expect the determination to be made by their review
office, not by the researcher.
