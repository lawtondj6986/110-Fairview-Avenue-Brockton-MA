short: "The share of people invited to take a survey who completed it. It is a basic indicator of how far the results can be generalized to the whole group."

The response rate is the number of completed surveys divided by the number of eligible people who
were invited. If a survey went to ten thousand students and two thousand finished it, the response
rate is twenty percent. Reports should state how a completed survey was defined, since partial
responses can be counted in different ways.

The figure matters because of nonresponse bias. If people who answer differ in relevant ways from
people who do not, the results will not match the whole population. In surveys on sexual misconduct
the direction of that bias is not obvious. People with relevant experiences may be more motivated to
respond, or may avoid a distressing subject. Researchers try to reduce the problem by weighting
responses so that the sample matches known characteristics of the population, such as gender and
year of study, and sometimes by studying late responders or conducting follow-up studies of
nonrespondents.

A low response rate does not by itself make a survey wrong, and a high one does not guarantee
accuracy. Weighting corrects only for the characteristics that were measured. The sensible reading
is to treat estimates from low-response surveys as having more uncertainty than their stated margins
of error suggest, and to be cautious about comparing institutions whose response rates differ
widely.
