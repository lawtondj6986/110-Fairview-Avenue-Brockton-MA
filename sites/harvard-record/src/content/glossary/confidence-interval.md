term: "Confidence interval"
short: "A range of values, calculated from sample data, that is constructed so that intervals made the same way would contain the true value a stated share of the time, often 95 percent."

A confidence interval gives a range around an estimate rather than a single number. A study might
report that a rate is 12 percent with a 95 percent confidence interval of 9 to 15 percent. The
interval expresses how precise the estimate is: a narrow interval means the data pin the value down
closely; a wide one means they do not. The margin of error in a poll is half the width of a
confidence interval.

Intervals appear in surveys, medical studies and many other kinds of research. When two groups'
intervals do not overlap, the difference between them is usually statistically significant. When
they overlap a little, the difference may or may not be, and a formal test is needed. Graphs often
show intervals as error bars, which should be labeled because error bars sometimes show other
measures.

A 95 percent interval does not mean there is a 95 percent probability that the true value lies in
this particular range, though it is often described that way. Strictly, the 95 percent refers to the
method. More important for most readers, the interval reflects only random sampling error and assumes
the study was designed and carried out without bias.
