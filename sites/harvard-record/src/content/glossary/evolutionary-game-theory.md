Classical game theory studies how rational players choose strategies. Evolutionary game theory drops
the assumption of rationality. Instead, strategies are treated like inherited traits: individuals
using a strategy that earns higher payoffs leave more descendants, or are copied more often, so that
strategy becomes more common. The approach is used to model animal behavior, the evolution of
cooperation, the spread of drug resistance, cancer cell competition and the dynamics of social
norms.

A standard example is the prisoner's dilemma, in which each player does better by defecting, yet
both do better if both cooperate. Researchers ask which conditions, such as repeated encounters,
reputation or population structure, allow cooperation to persist. Results are usually expressed as
equations and computer simulations, sometimes tested against experimental or field data.

These models are simplifications. A result shows what follows from stated assumptions; it does not
by itself show that a real population behaves that way. Much of the work is theoretical, so its
main products are papers, code and proofs, and a model's predictions need separate empirical tests.
