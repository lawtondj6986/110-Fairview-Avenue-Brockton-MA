Quid pro quo is Latin for "something for something." In fundraising it describes a payment made
partly or wholly in exchange for a benefit.

Tax law uses the term in a narrow, routine sense. When a donor pays more than the value of goods or
services received, such as a dinner ticket, the excess is the deductible gift. U.S. federal rules
require a charity to give the donor a written statement of the value of what was provided when such
a payment exceeds a set amount. Token items and simple acknowledgment, such as a name on a plaque,
are generally not treated as a return benefit. Development offices handle this through standard
receipt language.

The broader sense concerns exchanges that an institution should not make at all. Examples would be a
gift conditioned on admitting a particular applicant, appointing a particular person, awarding a
degree or steering research to a desired conclusion. Gift acceptance policies prohibit conditions of
this kind, and some such exchanges could raise legal issues beyond tax. Proof of an explicit bargain
is rare. More often the record shows a gift and a favorable decision close together, which
establishes sequence but not agreement. A reader should distinguish a documented condition from an
inference drawn from timing, and say which one the evidence supports.
