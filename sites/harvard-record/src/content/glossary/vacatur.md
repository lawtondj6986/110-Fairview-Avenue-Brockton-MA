Vacatur is the act of vacating, which means cancelling the legal force of something. The term is
used in two main settings. An appeals court may vacate a lower court's judgment, often sending the
case back for further proceedings. A court reviewing agency action may vacate a regulation that it
finds unlawful, relying on the Administrative Procedure Act's instruction to hold unlawful and set
aside such action.

When a regulation is vacated, the usual result is that the rule it replaced comes back into effect.
Courts have commonly treated vacatur of a rule as applying to everyone, not only to the parties who
sued, although the proper scope of that remedy is disputed. A court can also remand a rule to the
agency without vacating it, leaving it in place while the agency repairs a defect, or vacate only
part of a rule.

Vacatur differs from an injunction. An injunction orders a party to do or stop doing something.
Vacatur acts on the rule or judgment itself. A vacated court judgment is also not the same as a
reversal on the merits. A judgment may be vacated because the case became moot or was settled,
without any ruling that the original decision was wrong.
