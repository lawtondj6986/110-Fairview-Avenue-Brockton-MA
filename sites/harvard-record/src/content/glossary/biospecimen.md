A biospecimen is a sample of human biological material. In research ethics the term covers the
physical sample and, in practice, the information linked to it, such as a code number, collection
date and clinical details. Biospecimens can be used up in a single test or stored for years and
reused, and cells grown from them can be shared between laboratories.

The 2018 revision of the Common Rule defines an identifiable biospecimen as one for which the
identity of the person is or may readily be ascertained by the investigator or associated with it.
Research using identifiable biospecimens is generally human subjects research and requires review
and consent, unless an exemption or waiver applies. The rule also created an option called broad
consent for storage and future unspecified research. State laws on genetic testing and privacy may
add requirements.

Removing a name does not necessarily make a specimen non-identifiable. A sample's DNA is itself
identifying, so a coded specimen can often be linked back to a person by sequencing. Ownership of
specimens is a separate question from consent, and courts have generally not treated donors as
retaining property rights in samples they gave for research.
