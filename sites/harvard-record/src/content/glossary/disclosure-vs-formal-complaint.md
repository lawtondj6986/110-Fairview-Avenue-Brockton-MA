---
term: "Disclosure versus formal complaint"
short: "A disclosure is telling someone at an institution about an incident. A formal complaint is a signed request that the institution investigate. Most disclosures never become formal complaints."
category: "Campus safety"

Institutions distinguish several steps that everyday language lumps together as reporting. A
disclosure is any occasion on which a person tells someone at the institution what happened. If the
listener is a confidential resource, such as a counselor, the information normally goes no further.
If the listener is an employee with a duty to report, the information reaches the Title IX office
and becomes a report. A formal complaint is a further, deliberate step: a signed document asking the
institution to investigate a named person.

Each step has different consequences. After a report, the Title IX office contacts the person
affected, offers supportive measures and explains the options. An investigation and decision
generally follow only from a formal complaint. Many people choose support without an investigation,
for reasons that include privacy, fear of retaliation, and doubts about the process.

The figures institutions publish reflect these steps. A typical annual report may show many
disclosures, far fewer formal complaints, fewer completed investigations, and still fewer findings
of responsibility. None of these numbers measures how often misconduct occurred. A small number of
formal complaints in a department is therefore not evidence that little happened there, and the lack
of a formal complaint against a person does not show that nobody raised a concern.
