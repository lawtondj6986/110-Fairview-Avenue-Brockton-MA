---
term: "Answer"
short: "The defendant's formal written response to a civil complaint, admitting or denying each allegation and raising defenses."
category: "Legal procedure"
aliases: ["answer and affirmative defenses", "counterclaim"]
related: ["complaint-civil", "motion-to-dismiss", "allegation", "plaintiff-and-defendant"]
---

An answer is the pleading in which a defendant responds to a complaint. It goes through the
complaint paragraph by paragraph and, for each allegation, admits it, denies it, or states that the
defendant lacks enough information to admit or deny, which has the effect of a denial. The answer
also sets out affirmative defenses, such as expiry of the statute of limitations, and may include
counterclaims against the plaintiff.

In federal court a defendant generally has a short period after being served to file an answer,
unless it first files a motion to dismiss, which postpones the deadline. Failure to respond at all
can lead to a default judgment. Allegations that are not denied are generally treated as admitted.

An answer is useful to a reader because it shows precisely what is in dispute. Admissions in an
answer are reliable evidence of the admitted facts. Denials are less informative than they may seem.
Defendants routinely deny allegations in general terms, and a denial is not evidence that the
allegation is false. It only means the plaintiff will have to prove it. Many cases also end, through
dismissal or settlement, before any answer is filed, so the lack of one does not mean the defendant
accepted the claims.
