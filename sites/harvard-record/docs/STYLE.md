# Editorial style

The site's authority comes from restraint. These rules keep the writing plain and the claims exact.

## Voice

- Write for an intelligent reader who is not a specialist. Define a term when it first appears, or link it to
  the glossary.
- Short sentences. One idea each.
- State what a source says. Do not characterise it. "The review found" is better than "the review admitted".
- No adjectives that carry a verdict: shocking, troubling, damning, cozy, shadowy, notorious.
- No rhetorical questions. No scare quotes. No suggestive juxtaposition.
- The site has no view about what the reader should conclude.

## Attribution

| Situation | Wording |
|---|---|
| An institution's own finding | "Harvard's review found that ..." |
| A regulator's finding | "The Office for Civil Rights found that ..." |
| A claim in a lawsuit | "The complaint alleges that ..." Never "the lawsuit shows". |
| A letter from a legislator | "The letter asserts that ..." |
| Reporting based on documents | "CNN reported, from the released emails, that ..." |
| A person's own statement | "He said ..." or "She told the newspaper ..." |
| A second-hand account | "X said that Y had told him ..." and grade it B2. |
| Nothing found | "We found no public record of ..." Never "there is no ...". |

## Verbs that overclaim

Avoid: proves, reveals, exposes, confirms (unless the source is the person or body confirming), linked to,
tied to, connected to, associated with, part of a network.

Prefer: records, reports, states, lists, describes, found, said, gave, visited, received.

"Linked", "tied" and "connected" are the most dangerous words on a site like this. They assert a
relationship without saying what it is. Say what the record shows instead: who gave what, who met whom,
who wrote to whom, and when.

## People

- Use a person's full name on first mention in each page, then title and surname or surname alone,
  consistently.
- Give the role that explains why they appear: "then a physician at ...", "the Program's director".
- State a denial or response in the same paragraph as the finding or allegation it answers.
- For someone who appears only as a recipient, bystander or source, say so in plain words.
- Never name a person who made a complaint, and never describe a complainant in a way that identifies them.
- No photographs of people.

## Numbers

- Give the figure, the period it covers, and what is being counted, every time.
- Where sources differ, say so and say which figure the page uses and why.
- Do not add figures from different sources or different periods into a new total unless the page shows the
  arithmetic.
- A count of search results is not a count of documents, and a count of documents is not a count of messages.
- A capacity rating is not a gift. A pledge is not a gift. A priced proposal is not a payment.

## Dates

- Day, month, year in running text: 1 May 2020.
- Use the precision the source supports. "Spring 2026" is better than a guessed date.
- A range placed on a timeline is placed where it began.

## Quotations

- Quote exactly, briefly, and with attribution. Mark omissions.
- Do not quote from a summary of a document as though it were the document.
- A quotation inside a source page inherits that page's grade, not the grade of the original document.

## Evidence states

Use the three labels exactly as defined in `/methodology/`. Do not invent intermediate states. If a claim
sits between two, use the weaker.

## Spelling and typography

- Reference content (glossary, law, guides) uses American English, as set by the content brief.
- Curly quotation marks and apostrophes in prose.
- No em dashes. Use a comma, a colon, parentheses or two sentences.
- Sentence-case headings.
- Bold only for the term being defined in a list, never for emphasis.

## Headlines and page titles

Describe the subject. Do not tease, and do not put a conclusion in a title.

| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| The secret office | Program for Evolutionary Dynamics |
| Follow the money | Donor influence and introduced funding pathways |
| What they knew | Institutional response |
