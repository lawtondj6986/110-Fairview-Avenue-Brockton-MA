Jeffrey Epstein was convicted in Florida in 2008. Harvard stopped taking his money that year. Its own 2020 review found that he nonetheless kept physical access to a research program he had funded, visited it dozens of times, and introduced other donors whose gifts reached the same faculty.1 A large Department of Justice document release in early 2026 added detail and prompted Harvard to reopen and widen its review.2

The pages below set out that record section by section. They cover what happened at Harvard, how it compares with the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and, separately, the University’s public record on sexual misconduct and campus safety.

Program for Evolutionary Dynamics

Epstein’s $6.5 million gift founded this research program in 2003. Harvard took no money from him after 2008, yet he kept a key card and the use of an office, and visited more than 40 times before 2018. Harvard closed the program in 2021.

Summary

The Program for Evolutionary Dynamics was a mathematical biology group led by Professor Martin Nowak. It was established with a $6.5 million gift from Epstein in 2003 and worked from a private office building at One Brattle Square in Harvard Square.1

Harvard’s review counted $9.1 million in gifts from Epstein to the whole University between 1998 and 2008. After his 2008 conviction, President Drew Faust decided that Harvard would accept nothing further from him, and the review found that it did not.13 The Program is where the relationship continued in other forms: physical access, a presence on the Program’s website, and gifts from donors he had introduced.

A figure that is often misread

The $9.1 million is the total of Epstein’s gifts to Harvard, all received by 2008. It is not the amount given to this Program, and none of it arrived after the conviction. Some coverage gives the total as $9,179,000 or “nearly $9.2 million”.4 That figure would round to $9.2 million, so the sources do not simply differ by rounding. This site uses the University’s own “$9.1 million” until the report itself has been checked. The money that reached Professor Nowak’s work after 2008 came from other donors.

What the access consisted of

Harvard’s review describes four things.1

  • Visits. The records are imprecise. The review understood that he came to the Program’s offices more than 40 times between 2010 and 2018.
  • Entry. He had a visitor key card, the passcode, and a key to an individual office.
  • An office. The Program informally called one room “Jeffrey’s Office.” Professor Nowak told reviewers that other visitors used it too.
  • A web presence. The Program’s site linked to his foundation’s websites from 2013 and carried a page about him in 2014, until a complaint led to its removal.

The review said he used the visits mainly to meet prominent academics from the Cambridge area. It reported that it “did not find evidence” that he engaged with undergraduate students during the visits or as a Visiting Fellow.1 That sentence is limited to undergraduates and to what the review could find. The Crimson’s account of the report notes that he attended one of Professor Nowak’s undergraduate classes.4

Volume of contact

After the 2026 Justice Department release, reporters searching the public database found Professor Nowak’s name in more than 4,000 results.9 Outlets describe the unit differently (documents, results, appearances), and search hits include duplicates and forwarded threads. The number shows that contact was frequent. It is not a count of messages, and it says nothing by itself about their content.

The Leon Black pathway

The clearest example in the record is the financier Leon Black. Reporting on the 2020 review attributes $7 million in unrestricted gifts for Professor Nowak’s work to Mr Black, his wife and their family foundation between 2011 and 2015.2 That $7 million sits inside the review’s $7.5 million total for all introduced donors to Professor Nowak. Two figures of $7.5 million that are not the same thing

The $7.5 million in Harvard’s review is the sum given by all introduced donors for Professor Nowak’s work. Separately, in 2018 the Debra and Leon Black Family Foundation gave $7.5 million to fund fellowships for veterans and serving military at Harvard Kennedy School and other Harvard schools.6 We found no public record connecting the fellowship gift to Epstein, and this site does not treat it as part of the introduced-donor pathway.

These are allegations in a complaint that ended in a settlement. No court ruled on them. For present purposes the relevant point is narrower: there is no public output, paper, product or dataset showing that Southern Trust did genetic work of any kind.

The Zorro Ranch account

In July 2019 the New York Times reported that Epstein had told scientists and others of a wish to have women inseminated at his New Mexico ranch. The most specific version came from the author Jaron Lanier, who was relaying what a scientist had told him at a dinner. He did not hear it from Epstein. The Times reported that there was no evidence the idea came to anything.6

We grade the newspaper’s reporting A2 and the relayed account B2. It is evidence of something said.

Outside calibration

How much weight should a stated plan carry? One reference point is the Repository for Germinal Choice, a California sperm bank that opened in 1980 with the stated aim of producing gifted children, and to which the Times account compared the ranch idea. The journalist David Plotz traced its history and found that it produced about 215 children and did not produce what its founder promised.9 Even an implemented plan of this kind did not do what its author said it would. An unimplemented one deserves less weight still.

The legal boundary: GINA

The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 bars health insurers and employers with fifteen or more employees from using genetic information in coverage or employment decisions.10 It does not extend to life, disability or long-term-care insurance, and it does not create a general right of privacy in genetic data.11 It would bear on the material here only if a covered insurer or employer used someone’s results against them. No source reviewed alleges that.

Assessment

The items sit side by side and do not connect. The record shows no shared database, no research output and no operational programme. It shows one $2,000 sequencing job, proposals that went unfunded, retail kits sent as gifts, a business description that drew a fraud allegation, and a reported remark.

Frequently asked questions

Short answers to the questions readers ask most, with links to the pages that carry the detail.

About the site

Is this an official Harvard website?

No. This is an independent project. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or sponsored by Harvard University, the Harvard Alumni Association, or any Harvard school or office. Harvard’s own alumni site is at alumni.harvard.edu. The full disclaimer is on the About page.

Who is this site for?

Prospective students and their families, current students, alumni, and researchers. The summary pages are written for a general reader. The claim ledger and source register are there for people who want to check the work.

What is the site’s position on the people it names?

It does not have one. The site reports what cited records say and marks how strong each record is. Appearing in a document, or on this site, is not evidence of wrongdoing. Where an institution has made a finding about a person, the finding is attributed to that institution. Where a person has denied a claim, the denial is included.

Why does the site use Harvard’s name?

Only to identify the institution being discussed. The site does not use Harvard’s shield, seal, colors or typefaces, and every page carries a notice that it is independent.

No. It carries no advertising, sells nothing, and does not ask for donations or personal information.

Does the site contain private or leaked material?

No. Everything comes from public sources: institutional reports, court filings, government releases, statutes, and published reporting. The editorial rules set this out.

Reading the evidence

What do the grades A1, A2, B1 and B2 mean?

They describe the kind of source. A1 is a primary official record, such as an institutional report, a court filing or a statute. A2 is established reporting that quotes or closely follows such records. B1 is secondary analysis with clear sourcing. B2 is thin or unverified material, always flagged. The full definitions are in the methodology.

Does a high grade mean a claim is true?

Not by itself. A grade tells you how reliable the source is as a record of what it says. A court complaint is an A1 record of what a party alleged. It is not proof that the allegation is correct. That is why each claim also carries an evidence state.

What are the three evidence states?

Stated intent means something was proposed or said. Documented activity means a record shows something was done. Verified implementation means an official finding or independent result confirms the act and its outcome. A fourth label, nothing on record, marks a statement that no record was found.

Why keep a proposal separate from an act?

Because the record contains several priced proposals and stated plans that were never carried out. Presenting them alongside things that did happen, without marking the difference, would mislead. The DNA and genetics page shows the method applied item by item.

What does “pending check” mean?

It means the entry was compiled from published summaries of the source and has not yet been re-read against the original document by a maintainer. Entries lose the label when a check date is recorded. Until then, treat the detail as provisional.

What does “no record found” mean?

Exactly that: the sources reviewed do not contain it. It is not a finding that something did not happen. The site states absences this way on purpose.

Why do some figures differ from what I have read elsewhere?

Outlets round differently, count different periods, or count different things. Where the site found a discrepancy, the page says so and explains which figure it uses. A common example is a university-wide gift total being quoted as if it were the amount given to one program.

Why are unofficial document archives and wikis not cited?

They can be useful for finding a document, but they are not a reliable record of it. Text can be mis-transcribed, summarized inaccurately, or mixed with commentary. The site cites established reporting until the original document can be linked directly.

The record itself

Did Harvard take money from Jeffrey Epstein after his 2008 conviction?

Harvard’s own 2020 review found that it did not. The review counted about $9.1 million in gifts, all received between 1998 and 2008. What continued after 2008 was physical access to one research program and gifts from other donors he had introduced. See the Program for Evolutionary Dynamics page.

What is an “introduced donor”?

A donor who was introduced to a faculty member or program by a third person. Harvard’s review recorded $9.5 million from donors Epstein had introduced. The donors denied that he directed their gifts, and the review found no evidence that he did. See donor pathways.

Does the record show a genetics program?

No. It shows one small sequencing job, some unfunded proposals, consumer test kits sent as gifts, a business description that later drew a fraud allegation, and a second-hand account of something said. The record does not connect these, and it shows no shared database or research output. See the DNA and genetics record.

Were people who received a DNA test kit involved in anything?

Nothing in the record suggests so. The emails show staff buying and posting a retail product as a gift. They do not show whether a recipient used a kit, and receiving one implies nothing about the recipient.

The 2020 review is complete and published. A second review opened in November 2025 and was widened in February 2026. No findings from it had been published when the pages were last reviewed. Each page shows its own “last reviewed” date.

How does Harvard compare with MIT?

The patterns differ. MIT accepted gifts directly after the conviction, with the knowledge of senior administrators. Harvard did not accept direct gifts but allowed continued physical access at one program. The comparison table sets the two side by side.

Campus safety

Where can I find current campus crime numbers?

In the Annual Security Report that every U.S. college participating in federal student aid must publish each October. The campus safety page links to Harvard’s, and the guide to reading a campus security report explains what the tables do and do not count.

Do higher reported numbers mean a campus is less safe?

Not necessarily. Reported incidents depend heavily on whether people feel able to report. A campus with strong reporting channels can show higher numbers than one where incidents go unreported. Survey prevalence and reported incidents measure different things and should be read together.

Only where the University itself announced a finding or sanction, or a court record exists. Each summary is attributed, and each includes the person’s denial where one was made.

No. Harvard’s 2020 review said it did not find evidence that Epstein engaged with undergraduate students during his visits, and no source reviewed documents a complaint arising from them. The two subjects are presented separately for that reason.

METHOD · https://www.harvarduniversityalumni.com/methodology/

Methodology and sourcing standards

Every claim on this site carries a source, and every source carries a grade. This page explains the grades, the rules for writing about people, and what the site will not do.

Reliability grades

A grade describes the kind of source. It is not a judgement of the people a source discusses.

GradeMeaningDefinition
Reliability grade A1 Primary official record An official document speaking for itself: an institutional report, a court filing, a government release, a statute, or hearing minutes.
Reliability grade A2 Reporting that tracks the record Reporting by an established outlet that quotes or closely follows primary documents, or records an on-the-record statement.
Reliability grade B1 Secondary analysis Books, scholarship, or analysis with clear sourcing and context. Used for background and calibration, not for new factual claims about individuals.
Reliability grade B2 Thin or unverified A single uncorroborated account, a second-hand recollection, or material we could not check against a primary document. Always flagged in the text.

Three rules follow from the grades.

  • A factual claim about a named living person needs an A1 or A2 source.
  • A primary record of an allegation, such as a complaint or a congressional letter, is graded A1 for what it says and is always described as an allegation or assertion.
  • When a well-sourced article relays a weaker account, the weaker grade applies to that account.

Evidence states

Separately from the grade of the source, each claim is placed in an evidence state.

StateDefinition
Stated intentSomething was proposed, pitched, or said. The record does not show that it happened.
Documented activityA record shows that something was done: a payment, a visit, a shipment, an order.
Verified implementationAn official finding or an independent result confirms the activity and what it produced.
Nothing on recordWe found no public record of this. Absence of a record is not proof of absence.

A claim moves up a state only when a source supports the move. A well-documented proposal is still a proposal.

Verification status

Each entry in the source register records the date on which a maintainer last opened the original document and checked the cited facts against it. Entries without a date are labelled “pending check” wherever they are cited. They were compiled from published summaries of the source and should be treated as provisional until checked. Current progress is on the verification status page.

First edition

In this first edition most entries are pending check. Figures that differ between outlets are noted in the text where we found them.

Editorial rules

  • Public sources only. No leaked, private or paywalled-only material that a reader cannot trace.
  • No inference of coordination. Two facts side by side are not presented as connected unless a source connects them.
  • Denials and responses are included where a person or institution has made one.
  • Absence is stated as absence. “No record found” means we did not find one.
  • Unofficial document mirrors and wikis are not cited. Where a document from the Justice Department release is discussed, it is cited through established reporting until the original can be linked.
  • People named only as recipients or bystanders are named when the cited reporting names them, and the text says plainly that no wrongdoing is implied.
